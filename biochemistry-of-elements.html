Abstract. The ancient natural philosophers organized the material world around a small set of elemental principles — water, fire, wind (air), and earth. While these categories have no standing in modern chemistry, they persist as a strikingly useful heuristic for organizing the coupled biogeochemical machinery that regulates the Earth's surface environment and sustains life. This review adopts water, fire, and wind as organizing metaphors for three interlocking domains of Earth and life science: the hydrological cycle and the biochemistry of aqueous solvation; the carbon cycle, biological oxidation, and combustion as manifestations of controlled and uncontrolled "fire"; and the atmospheric circulation, nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur cycles that constitute the biogeochemical "wind." A fourth domain — rock and mineral weathering — is treated as the lithospheric substrate that couples all three cycles together and regulates planetary climate on geological timescales. We synthesize primary literature spanning geochemistry, atmospheric science, microbiology, and biomedicine to show how these cycles are mechanistically coupled, how industrial-era human activity has perturbed each of them, and what the consequences are for climate and human health. We close with case studies illustrating cycle coupling in real systems — the Amazon Basin, ocean upwelling zones, enhanced weathering field trials, wildfire-affected landscapes, and coastal dead zones — and outline research and policy priorities going forward.

1. Introduction: The Elements as an Organizing Frame

1.1 Why revisit the classical elements?

For roughly two millennia, from Empedocles through medieval natural philosophy, Western thought organized the material world around four root substances — earth, water, fire, and air. The scheme was empirically wrong in the sense that mattered to Lavoisier and Dalton: water is not an element but a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, fire is not a substance at all but a visible signature of rapid exothermic oxidation, and air is a mixture of several true elements and compounds in gaseous form. Modern chemistry replaced the four-element scheme with the periodic table, and modern biochemistry replaced vitalistic explanations of life's chemistry with mechanistic accounts grounded in thermodynamics, kinetics, and molecular structure.

And yet the classical categories persist — not as chemistry, but as a taxonomy of processes. "Water" names a category of phenomena organized around solvation, hydration, and the hydrological cycle. "Fire" names a category organized around oxidation-reduction (redox) chemistry, whether uncontrolled (combustion, wildfire) or controlled (respiration, metabolism). "Wind" names a category organized around the gas-phase cycles — the circulation of the atmosphere itself and the biogeochemical cycles of nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur that are carried through it. This review uses the classical triad as an organizing device, not a scientific claim, to draw together three literatures — geochemistry, atmospheric and ocean science, and biochemistry — that are often reviewed separately despite being mechanistically inseparable in the real Earth system.

The device has a genuine intellectual justification beyond mnemonic convenience. Earth system science increasingly treats the planet's surface environment as a network of coupled biogeochemical cycles — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, water — that exchange matter and energy between the atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and lithosphere (Wikipedia contributors, Biogeochemical cycle; ScienceDirect Topics, Biogeochemical Cycle). A 2025 synthesis in Frontiers in Environmental Science explicitly frames the challenge of contemporary geochemistry as "integrating elemental cycles, anthropogenic pressures and planetary boundaries" into a single dynamical picture, because the cycles cannot be understood, or managed, in isolation (Frontiers in Environmental Science, 2025). The classical elements, reinterpreted as process categories, map cleanly onto this systems view: water onto the hydrological cycle, fire onto the carbon/redox cycle, and wind onto the atmospheric gas cycles (nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur) and the physical circulation that transports heat, moisture, dust, and gases around the globe.

1.2 Scope and aims

This review has four aims. First, to summarize, at a level appropriate for an interdisciplinary graduate or professional audience, the core chemistry and biochemistry underlying each of the three domains — water, fire, wind — with attention to both the molecular mechanisms (hydrogen bonding, enzymatic catalysis, electron transport) and the planetary-scale cycles they aggregate into. Second, to treat rock and mineral weathering as a fourth, connective domain: the lithosphere is the substrate that supplies nutrients to the biosphere, regulates atmospheric CO₂ on geological timescales through the carbonate-silicate cycle, and is where "fire" (oxidative weathering), "water" (aqueous dissolution), and biology (root and microbial weathering) physically meet. Third, to synthesize how industrial-era human activity — fossil fuel combustion, industrial nitrogen fixation, land-use change, mining — has perturbed each cycle, with emphasis on the carbon cycle and climate change as the central, cycle-spanning anthropogenic disruption of the current era. Fourth, to connect these biogeochemical dynamics to human health outcomes, from the cardiovascular effects of combustion-derived particulate matter to the nutritional consequences of soil micronutrient depletion, closing the loop between planetary-scale geochemistry and the biochemistry of individual human bodies.

The review is organized as follows. Sections 2 through 4 treat water, fire, and wind in turn, moving from molecular mechanism to planetary cycle. Section 5 treats rock and mineral weathering as the geochemical substrate connecting the three. Section 6 integrates the cycles explicitly, describing feedback loops and coupling mechanisms. Section 7 focuses on anthropogenic perturbation and climate change specifically. Section 8 addresses environmental and health dimensions. Section 9 presents five case studies chosen to illustrate cycle coupling in real systems. Section 10 concludes with research and policy priorities.

1.3 A note on framing and evidence

Two cautions are warranted. First, the classical-elements framing is heuristic, not explanatory; no claim is made that water, fire, and wind are natural kinds in the way carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus are. Second, every biogeochemical cycle discussed here is itself a simplification of an enormously more complex and spatially heterogeneous reality — box models of global cycles necessarily average over reservoirs (atmosphere, ocean, biosphere, crust) that are internally structured at every scale from the molecular to the planetary (ScienceDirect Topics, Biogeochemical Cycle). Throughout, we cite primary and review literature and flag where quantitative estimates carry substantial uncertainty.

2. Water: The Hydrosphere and the Chemistry of Life

2.1 The molecular basis of water's biochemical roles

Water's biological centrality traces to a single structural fact: the bent geometry of the H₂O molecule combined with the electronegativity difference between oxygen and hydrogen gives each molecule a permanent dipole and the capacity to form up to four hydrogen bonds with neighboring molecules — two as a donor through its hydrogen atoms, two as an acceptor through its oxygen lone pairs. This tetrahedral hydrogen-bonding network is the physical basis of nearly every biologically relevant property of liquid water: its anomalously high boiling point relative to comparably sized molecules, its high heat capacity and heat of vaporization (which buffer organisms and climates against rapid temperature change), its density maximum at 4°C (which prevents water bodies from freezing solid from the bottom up), and, most importantly for biochemistry, its exceptional capacity as a solvent (Chaplin, Water's Hydrogen Bond Strength; Adivaasmart, Hydrogen Bonding in Water).

Because water molecules are simultaneously hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, water can solvate both cationic and anionic species and can hydrogen-bond directly to polar and charged solutes — the basis of its designation as the "universal solvent" and the reason it can transport nutrients, ions, and waste products through cells, tissues, and circulatory systems (Adivaasmart, Hydrogen Bonding in Water). Hydrogen bonds are also directly implicated in the architecture of biological macromolecules themselves: the double helix of DNA is held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs, protein secondary structure (α-helices, β-sheets) is stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds, and substrate recognition by enzymes frequently depends on a precise geometry of hydrogen bonds between enzyme and substrate (Biochemistry/ACS, Hydrogen Bonds: Simple after All?; PubMed 33075210, Molecular Recognition Mediated by Hydrogen Bonding in Aqueous Media). Because these same hydrogen-bonding forces operate between biomolecules and the surrounding solvent, water is not merely a passive medium in which biochemistry happens — it is an active participant that competes with, stabilizes, and sometimes catalyzes conformational and chemical transitions in biomolecules. This is why the hydrogen bond in water has proven so difficult to characterize spectroscopically relative to the same interaction in aprotic solvents: the interaction in water is heavily perturbed and screened by the surrounding solvation shell (Journal of Physical Chemistry B, Nature of the Hydrogen Bond of Water in Solvents of Different Polarities).

Water participates directly in biochemical transformations, not merely as solvent. Hydrolysis reactions — the enzymatic or spontaneous cleavage of a chemical bond by insertion of a water molecule — underlie the digestion of proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids (via proteases, amylases, and lipases), the turnover of ATP into ADP and inorganic phosphate that powers cellular work, and the breakdown of nucleic acids. The reverse reaction, condensation with loss of water, is how cells polymerize amino acids into proteins and nucleotides into nucleic acids. Water is also a direct substrate or product in central metabolic pathways: it is split during the light reactions of oxygenic photosynthesis (releasing molecular oxygen as a byproduct) and is produced as the terminal product of aerobic respiration at Complex IV of the electron transport chain, where oxygen is reduced to water (Britannica, Electron transport chain; NCBI Bookshelf, Biochemistry, Electron Transport Chain). In this sense, essentially every unit of atmospheric oxygen humans and other aerobic organisms breathe is ultimately returned to the hydrosphere as water, closing a loop between the "water" and "wind" domains of this review that is elaborated in Section 6.

2.2 Water homeostasis and osmoregulation in organisms

At the organismal level, water's chemistry constrains physiology through osmoregulation. Cell membranes are selectively permeable, and the concentration gradients of solutes across them determine the osmotic movement of water via aquaporins and passive diffusion. Multicellular organisms have evolved elaborate systems — the vertebrate kidney, the plant vascular system (xylem and phloem), and the contractile vacuoles of protists — to manage water balance against gradients imposed by the external environment (marine, freshwater, or terrestrial). In plants, the cohesion-tension mechanism by which water is pulled from roots to leaves against gravity relies directly on water's hydrogen-bond-derived cohesive strength and adhesion to the walls of xylem vessels; without hydrogen bonding, trees taller than a few meters would be physically impossible to hydrate via passive transport.

2.3 The global hydrological cycle

At the planetary scale, water cycles continuously between the ocean, atmosphere, land surface, cryosphere, and groundwater, driven by solar radiation and gravity (ESSIC, The Global Water Cycle). The cycle comprises evaporation and transpiration (jointly, evapotranspiration) from oceans, soils, and vegetation; atmospheric transport of water vapor; condensation and precipitation; and runoff, infiltration, and groundwater flow returning water to the ocean. Roughly 86% of global evaporation and 78% of global precipitation occur over the ocean, but the comparatively small terrestrial branch of the cycle is disproportionately important for sustaining freshwater ecosystems and human water supply (ESSIC, The Global Water Cycle).

A defining feature of the water cycle in the current era is its intensification under anthropogenic warming. Basic thermodynamics (the Clausius-Clapeyron relation) predicts that the atmosphere's water-holding capacity increases by roughly 7% per °C of warming, and observational reconstructions using ocean salinity as an integrator of the global water cycle have inferred an amplification of the cycle of approximately 3.0 ± 1.6% per °C over 1950–2010 — an amplification rate that, notably, appears to exceed some model-based estimates and remains an active area of research (PMC5146653, Global water cycle amplifying at less than the Clausius-Clapeyron rate). A comprehensive review in the inaugural volume of a water-resources journal concluded that intensification of the hydrological cycle, observed since at least 1980, is exacerbating both flooding and drought, with hydrological extremes becoming more extreme and vulnerable "hot-spot" regions emerging where adaptation needs are most acute (ScienceDirect S2666683921000213, Hydrological cycle and water resources in a changing world: A review). The Wikipedia synthesis on climate effects on the water cycle summarizes the same conclusion succinctly: warming causes more evaporation from land and ocean, which intensifies the cycle and increases the frequency of extreme precipitation events, even as some regions experience longer and more severe droughts (Wikipedia contributors, Effects of climate change on the water cycle).

2.4 Water–rock interactions

Water is also the primary agent of chemical weathering, the process by which rock and mineral surfaces are dissolved, hydrolyzed, hydrated, and oxidized by contact with meteoric water, forming the basis of soil formation, nutrient release, and — as detailed in Sections 3 and 5 — long-term climate regulation via the carbonate-silicate cycle. Weathering reactions typically require water that is at least mildly acidic (from dissolved atmospheric CO₂ forming carbonic acid, from organic acids exuded by roots and microbes, or from sulfuric and nitric acid inputs from atmospheric pollution), and it is precisely these reactions that liberate phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and trace metal nutrients from primary minerals into forms available to the biosphere (ScienceDirect Topics, Chemical Weathering; Springer, Chemical Weathering of Rocks and Its Controlling Factors). Biological catalysis of weathering — through root exudation of organic acids, microbial production of chelating siderophores, and the metabolic generation of CO₂ and organic acids in soils — allows the biosphere to "route" weathering reactions through lower activation-energy pathways than abiotic dissolution alone would permit, a theme revisited in Section 5 (University of Montana ScholarWorks, Water-rock interaction and life).

2.5 Water quality, contamination, and human health

Water's role as universal solvent is also the mechanistic basis of its capacity to carry contaminants into biological systems. Waterborne pathogens, heavy metals, nitrate from agricultural runoff, and industrial organic pollutants all exploit the same solvation chemistry that makes water biologically indispensable. Climate change compounds these risks: altered precipitation patterns, more intense flooding events, and heat-driven water scarcity increase both the concentration of contaminants in reduced water supplies and the likelihood of infrastructure failures that allow untreated or contaminated water into distribution systems, with documented associations between climate-driven hydrological extremes and waterborne disease outbreaks (PMC6119235, Climate Change Impacts on Waterborne Diseases: Moving Toward Designing Interventions). These linkages are developed further in Section 8.

2.6 Synthesis

Water's biochemical importance is inseparable from its geochemical and climatic role. The same hydrogen-bonding chemistry that makes water an effective intracellular solvent and enzymatic reaction medium also governs its behavior as a planetary fluid — its high heat capacity moderates climate, its solvent power drives rock weathering and nutrient cycling, and its phase changes structure the entire hydrological cycle that, in turn, is now being measurably intensified by anthropogenic warming. This dual character — water as molecular participant in biochemistry and water as planetary-scale climate regulator — recurs as a theme throughout this review, most explicitly in the discussion of the carbonate-silicate thermostat (Section 3.3) and the Amazon Basin case study (Section 9.1).

3. Fire: Oxidation, Combustion, and the Carbon Cycle

3.1 Biological oxidation as "controlled fire"

Combustion and cellular respiration are, at the level of net chemical transformation, the same reaction: the oxidation of a reduced carbon substrate by molecular oxygen to yield carbon dioxide, water, and energy. Wood combusts to CO₂ and H₂O releasing heat and light; glucose is oxidized by aerobic organisms to CO₂ and H₂O releasing energy captured as ATP. The difference is entirely one of kinetics and control. Combustion releases the free energy of oxidation abruptly, as heat and light, through free-radical chain reactions that are only loosely regulated. Cellular respiration releases the same free energy in small, enzymatically gated increments through the electron transport chain, allowing the cell to capture a large fraction of it as chemical energy in ATP rather than losing it as heat.

The electron transport chain (ETC), located in the inner mitochondrial membrane of eukaryotes (and the plasma membrane of many prokaryotes), is the biochemical apparatus that performs this controlled oxidation. Electron carriers generated upstream in glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the citric acid (Krebs) cycle — principally NADH and FADH₂ — donate electrons to a sequence of membrane-bound protein complexes (Complexes I–IV), each of which catalyzes a redox reaction that releases a small, defined quantum of free energy (NCBI Bookshelf, Biochemistry, Electron Transport Chain; Britannica, Electron transport chain). That energy is used to pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, building an electrochemical gradient that ATP synthase then discharges to phosphorylate ADP to ATP — the process of oxidative phosphorylation. At the terminus of the chain, Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) transfers electrons to molecular oxygen, the final electron acceptor, reducing it to water (Britannica, Electron transport chain). This is the biochemical version of "fire under control": the same overall redox chemistry as combustion, staged across a membrane to maximize useful work rather than heat.

3.2 The global carbon cycle

At the ecosystem and planetary scale, the fluxes of carbon among the atmosphere, oceans, terrestrial biosphere, and lithosphere constitute the carbon cycle. Photosynthetic fixation of atmospheric CO₂ into organic carbon, respiration and decomposition that return it to the atmosphere, and burial of a small fraction of organic carbon in sediments (which, over geological time, becomes the fossil fuel reservoir) together define the "fast" biological carbon cycle, operating on timescales of days to millennia. Superimposed on this fast cycle is the "slow" geological carbon cycle — volcanic outgassing of CO₂ and its removal via silicate weathering and carbonate burial — operating on timescales of hundreds of thousands to millions of years (see Section 3.3 and Section 5).

Human combustion of fossil carbon has, within roughly two centuries, injected a geologically anomalous pulse of carbon into the fast cycle. The Global Carbon Project's 2024 budget estimated fossil fuel CO₂ emissions of 37.4 billion tonnes for 2024, up 0.8% from 2023, with coal, oil, and gas contributing 41%, 32%, and 21% of the total respectively; including land-use-change emissions of roughly 4.2 billion tonnes, total anthropogenic CO₂ emissions for 2024 were estimated at 41.6 billion tonnes (Global Carbon Budget 2024; ESSD, Global Carbon Budget 2024). The resulting atmospheric CO₂ concentration was projected to reach approximately 422.5 ppm in 2024, about 52% above pre-industrial levels, with researchers noting "no sign" that global fossil emissions have yet peaked (Global Carbon Budget 2024; Future Earth, Annual Global Carbon Budget Shows Fossil Fuel Emissions Hit Record High in 2024).

3.3 The carbonate-silicate cycle: Earth's long-term thermostat

The "slow" carbon cycle operates through a negative feedback loop first quantitatively modeled by Walker, Hays, and Kasting in 1981: atmospheric CO₂ reacts with silicate minerals during chemical weathering (accelerated by warmer, wetter conditions), and the resulting bicarbonate is transported by rivers to the ocean, where it is precipitated as carbonate rock (largely via biogenic calcification) and ultimately subducted and metamorphosed, releasing CO₂ back to the atmosphere through volcanism (arXiv:1805.06943, Long Term Planetary Habitability and the Carbonate-Silicate Cycle; Nature Communications, Carbonate-silicate cycle predictions of Earth-like planetary climates). Because weathering rates increase with temperature and precipitation, the cycle is self-stabilizing: a warming episode accelerates silicate weathering, drawing down atmospheric CO₂ and cooling the climate; a cooling episode slows weathering, allowing volcanic CO₂ to accumulate and rewarm the climate. This feedback is widely credited with having kept Earth's surface temperature within a habitable range for most of the planet's history despite a roughly 30% increase in solar luminosity since the Archean (arXiv:1805.06943; Grokipedia, Carbonate–silicate cycle).

This same mechanism is the scientific basis for "enhanced weathering" as a proposed carbon dioxide removal strategy, discussed in Section 5.4 and Section 9.3: by crushing silicate rock (typically basalt) and spreading it on agricultural land, the natural weathering reaction can be accelerated on a timescale of years to decades rather than the tens of thousands of years typical of natural weathering, deliberately engineering the same thermostat that has regulated planetary climate over geological time.

3.4 Wildfire biochemistry and pyrogenic carbon

Uncontrolled biological "fire" — wildfire — is itself a significant biogeochemical process rather than merely a hazard. Incomplete combustion of vegetation during wildfires produces pyrogenic carbon (PyC), also called black carbon or charcoal, at an estimated global rate of roughly 116–385 teragrams of carbon per year (Frontiers in Environmental Science, Function of Wildfire-Deposited Pyrogenic Carbon in Terrestrial Ecosystems). PyC's chemical structure — condensed, graphene-like aromatic carbon rings with a high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio — makes it one of the most degradation-resistant organic carbon pools in soils and sediments, though its ultimate long-term fate remains an active research question (PMC10867845, Potentially Massive and Global Non-Pyrogenic Production of Condensed "Black" Carbon through Biomass Oxidation). Wildfire-derived PyC is subsequently mobilized into river networks, where it alters dissolved organic matter composition, microbial community structure, and biofilm enzymatic activity, and appears to interact with nitrogen and sulfur cycling downstream (ACS ES&T, Wildfire-Derived Pyrogenic Carbon Modulates Riverine Organic Matter and Biofilm Enzyme Activities; ACS Environmental Science & Technology, Appearance of Recalcitrant Dissolved Black Carbon and Dissolved Organic Sulfur in River Waters Following Wildfire Events). Because PyC is both a carbon sink (owing to its recalcitrance) and a source of bioavailable nutrients and dissolved organic matter to aquatic ecosystems, wildfire regime shifts under climate change — generally toward more frequent and severe fires in many biomes — carry substantial and still incompletely quantified consequences for regional and global carbon budgets.

3.5 Combustion, air quality, and health

The same oxidative chemistry that releases energy in combustion also generates a suite of byproducts with direct human health consequences, chief among them fine and ultrafine particulate matter (PM2.5 and smaller), which are treated in depth in Section 8.1. Because fossil fuel combustion is simultaneously the dominant source of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and a major source of combustion-derived particulate air pollution, mitigation strategies that reduce fossil fuel combustion (renewable electrification, transportation electrification) tend to deliver joint climate and public-health benefits — a point returned to in Section 10.

3.6 Synthesis

"Fire," reinterpreted biochemically, unifies phenomena that range from the sub-nanometer scale of electron transfer in a mitochondrial cytochrome to the continental scale of a wildfire season and the geological scale of the carbonate-silicate thermostat. In every case, the underlying chemistry is oxidation-reduction; what differs is the degree of biological or geochemical control over the rate and locus of electron transfer. The next section turns to "wind" — the atmospheric and gas-cycle domain that both supplies the oxidant (O₂) for every oxidation reaction discussed here and receives its principal waste product (CO₂).

4. Wind: Atmosphere, Circulation, and the Gaseous Cycles

4.1 Atmospheric circulation

Global wind patterns are driven by the differential solar heating of the Earth's surface as a function of latitude, redistributed by the planet's rotation. In the tropics, intense solar heating drives moist air upward in a band near the equator; this air cools, releases its moisture as precipitation (forming the heavy rainfall belt of the Intertropical Convergence Zone), and spreads poleward aloft before sinking around 30° latitude in both hemispheres, forming the subtropical high-pressure belts associated with the world's major deserts, including the Sahara (Britannica, Hadley cell; Wikipedia contributors, Hadley cell). This circulation cell — the Hadley cell — is the dominant driver of the trade winds and, together with the mid-latitude Ferrel cells and polar cells, constitutes the basic three-cell model of global atmospheric circulation. This circulation is not incidental background to biogeochemistry: it is the physical transport mechanism that carries water vapor (Section 2.3), mineral dust (Section 4.4), and reactive gases and aerosols (Sections 4.2–4.3) around the globe, and its position and strength are themselves sensitive to climate change, with recent reconstructions indicating a weakening of the Northern Hemisphere Hadley circulation in association with tropical warming (arXiv:2503.05331, Radiosonde-constrained reconstructions reveal a weakening Northern Hadley circulation).

4.2 The nitrogen cycle and its anthropogenic transformation

Nitrogen is the most abundant gas in the atmosphere (as inert N₂) but is biologically unavailable in that form to all but a small number of nitrogen-fixing archaea and bacteria (free-living and symbiotic, e.g., Rhizobium in legume root nodules), which use the nitrogenase enzyme complex to reduce N₂ to biologically usable ammonia. Fixed nitrogen then cycles through nitrification (oxidation of ammonia to nitrite and nitrate by chemoautotrophic bacteria), assimilation into biomass, and denitrification (microbial reduction of nitrate back to N₂ gas, typically under anoxic conditions), closing the loop back to the atmosphere (PMC3682748, The global nitrogen cycle in the twenty-first century).

This cycle has been fundamentally altered by industrial synthetic nitrogen fixation. The Haber-Bosch process, which combines atmospheric N₂ with hydrogen at high temperature and pressure over a catalyst to synthesize ammonia, now fixes on the order of 120 teragrams of nitrogen annually, roughly 80% of it destined for agricultural fertilizer (PMC3682748). Combined with fossil-fuel-combustion-related nitrogen oxide emissions, anthropogenic nitrogen fixation has more than doubled the pre-industrial rate of global nitrogen fixation, with annual anthropogenic inputs now estimated at 170–190 teragrams of nitrogen (PMC3682748; Grokipedia, Human impact on the nitrogen cycle). A global meta-analysis of sedimentary nitrogen stable isotopes from 173 sites identified 1910–1930 — the period of Haber-Bosch's invention and early industrial deployment — as the onset of an unprecedented, still-ongoing perturbation of the global nitrogen cycle, detectable in sediment isotope records worldwide (Nature Communications Earth & Environment, Global change pattern of sedimentary nitrogen isotope during the Anthropocene epoch). The oceanic signature of this perturbation is termed the "¹⁵N Haber-Bosch effect": anthropogenic atmospheric nitrogen deposition over the ocean has driven a measurable negative shift in the nitrogen isotope ratio of oceanic fixed nitrogen pools, with associated increases in denitrification (roughly 5%) and decreases in natural oceanic N₂ fixation (over 10%) as the ocean nitrogen cycle re-equilibrates to elevated external nitrogen inputs (Yang & Gruber, Global Biogeochemical Cycles, 2016). The terrestrial and coastal consequences of this perturbation — principally eutrophication and hypoxia — are examined in the Gulf of Mexico case study (Section 9.5).

4.3 Oxygen and the Great Oxidation Event

Molecular oxygen, now roughly 21% of the atmosphere by volume and the terminal electron acceptor for aerobic respiration (Section 3.1), was essentially absent from the early atmosphere — probably less than 10⁻⁵ of present atmospheric levels — until the Great Oxidation Event (GOE) approximately 2.45 billion years ago, when atmospheric O₂ rose abruptly as a consequence of oxygenic photosynthesis by expanding cyanobacterial populations outpacing the reduced substrates (volcanic gases, reduced iron and sulfur) that had previously consumed photosynthetically produced oxygen (ScienceDirect, Review: The Continuing Puzzle of the Great Oxidation Event; PMC8238953, The Great Oxygenation Event as a consequence of ecological dynamics modulated by planetary change). Recent work indicates the GOE was mechanistically coupled to the phosphorus cycle: co-variation between marine phosphorus proxies and the inorganic carbon isotope record suggests synchronized fluctuations in marine productivity, phosphorus availability, and atmospheric oxygenation (PMC12528410, Marine phosphorus and atmospheric oxygen were coupled during the Great Oxidation Event). By fundamentally reshaping the redox chemistry of the atmosphere and, eventually, the oceans, the GOE cascaded into the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and iron, and set the stage for aerobic metabolism and, ultimately, the evolutionary emergence of large, energy-intensive multicellular life (ScienceDirect, Review: The Continuing Puzzle of the Great Oxidation Event).

4.4 The sulfur cycle

Sulfur enters the ocean from continental runoff, atmospheric deposition, and hydrothermal vent fluids, where it is assimilated by marine algae into organosulfur compounds such as dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP); microbial and algal processing of DMSP releases dimethylsulfide (DMS) to the atmosphere, where it oxidizes to sulfate aerosol that nucleates cloud condensation nuclei, thereby feeding back on cloud albedo and regional climate (LibreTexts Geosciences, The global sulfur cycle). Anthropogenic combustion of sulfur-containing fossil fuels, particularly coal, has substantially altered this natural cycle by injecting large quantities of sulfur dioxide into the atmosphere, which oxidizes to sulfuric acid and contributes to both acid deposition and (via aerosol formation) a net cooling radiative forcing that partially offsets greenhouse warming — one of several ways the sulfur and carbon cycles are climatically entangled (LibreTexts Geosciences, The global sulfur cycle; Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, The history of Earth's sulfur cycle, 2024).

4.5 Aeolian transport: dust, nutrients, and ocean fertilization

Wind is also a direct transport agent for solid mineral matter. Mineral dust lofted from arid and semi-arid regions — most prominently the Sahara, which is thought to be the single largest dust source on Earth — is transported thousands of kilometers across ocean basins, undergoing chemical transformation en route (reaction with atmospheric sulfuric and nitric acid, humidity-driven mineral phase changes) that increases the solubility and bioavailability of the iron it carries (Frontiers in Marine Science, Long-range transport of dust enhances oceanic iron bioavailability, 2024). Because iron is a limiting micronutrient for phytoplankton growth across large regions of the open ocean, this aeolian iron flux exerts direct control over marine primary productivity and, through the biological carbon pump, the exchange of CO₂ between the ocean and atmosphere (Frontiers in Marine Science, 2024; Jickells et al., Global Iron Connections Between Desert Dust, Ocean Biogeochemistry, and Climate). Studies of Saharan dust deposition in the tropical Atlantic have used this relationship as a paleoclimate proxy, reconstructing past dust-driven export production from sedimentary geochemical signatures (PMC10881577, Coccolith-calcite Sr/Ca as a proxy for transient export production related to Saharan dust deposition). Beyond iron, aeolian transport also disperses biological propagules — pollen, fungal spores, and microorganisms — over similarly vast distances, linking atmospheric circulation directly to biogeographic and ecological processes.

4.6 Wind-driven ocean mixing and upwelling

Wind stress on the ocean surface drives Ekman transport, which in eastern boundary current systems (the California, Humboldt/Peru, Canary, and Benguela currents) displaces surface water offshore and draws cold, nutrient-rich water up from depth in a process called coastal upwelling. These upwelling zones, though occupying a small fraction of the ocean's surface area, support a disproportionate share of global marine fisheries because the upwelled water resupplies nitrate, phosphate, and silicate that photic-zone phytoplankton would otherwise exhaust. Wind-driven upwelling is thus a second, independent mechanism (alongside aeolian dust deposition) by which atmospheric circulation directly regulates ocean primary productivity and, through it, the ocean's capacity to draw down atmospheric CO₂.

4.7 Air quality and respiratory/cardiovascular health

The same atmospheric circulation that distributes dust and volcanic and biogenic gases also distributes anthropogenic air pollutants, with direct consequences for human health that are examined in detail in Section 8.1. Because wind determines the dispersion, concentration, and transboundary transport of combustion-derived pollutants, atmospheric circulation is not merely a physical backdrop to air quality but an active determinant of who is exposed to how much pollution — a point with direct bearing on the environmental justice literature discussed in Section 8.3.

4.8 Synthesis

"Wind," in this review's expanded sense, comprises both the physical circulation of the atmosphere and the biogeochemical gas cycles — nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur — that circulation transports and that, in turn, shape atmospheric composition and climate. Like water and fire, wind is a domain in which human industrial activity (Haber-Bosch nitrogen fixation, fossil fuel combustion, land-use-driven dust emission) has produced perturbations detectable at the global, geological-record scale within a single century — a theme taken up systematically in Section 7.

5. Rocks and Minerals: The Geochemical Substrate

5.1 The rock cycle and weathering as a biochemical interface

Igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks are continuously interconverted through the rock cycle: crystallization of magma forms igneous rock; weathering, erosion, and lithification form sedimentary rock; heat and pressure form metamorphic rock; and melting returns material to magma. Chemical weathering — the dissolution, hydrolysis, hydration, and oxidation of rock and mineral surfaces on contact with water, atmospheric gases, and biological agents — is the process where the "rock" domain most directly interfaces with the biochemical and atmospheric domains treated above. Weathering is influenced jointly by the presence of water (Section 2.4), oxygen availability (Section 4.3), atmospheric and soil CO₂ concentration (Section 3), and biological catalysis by plant roots and microorganisms (Springer, Chemical Weathering of Rocks and Its Controlling Factors; ScienceDirect Topics, Chemical Weathering).

Two broad classes of weathering are relevant to biogeochemistry. Carbonate weathering — dissolution of limestone and other carbonate rocks by carbonic acid — operates on relatively short timescales (years to centuries) and, over a full weathering-to-burial cycle, is roughly carbon-neutral. Silicate weathering, in contrast, consumes atmospheric CO₂ net (converting it to dissolved bicarbonate that is eventually sequestered as marine carbonate) and is therefore the reaction responsible for the long-term carbonate-silicate thermostat described in Section 3.3 (arXiv:1805.06943).

5.2 Biological weathering and the rhizosphere

Weathering is not a purely abiotic geochemical process. The rhizosphere — the thin volume of soil immediately surrounding plant roots — is among the most biogeochemically active environments on the planet, characterized by a locally depressed pH (relative to bulk soil) driven by root respiration and the exudation of organic acids, and by dense microbial colonization that further accelerates mineral dissolution (Frontiers in Microbiology, Root exudates contribute to belowground ecosystem hotspots: A review, 2022). Plant roots and their associated microbiome can account for the release of up to 40% of photosynthetically fixed carbon into the soil as exudates, a substantial metabolic investment that pays off by mobilizing mineral-bound nutrients (particularly phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients) that would otherwise be inaccessible (Frontiers in Microbiology, 2022). Diazotrophic (nitrogen-fixing) bacteria colonizing the rhizosphere play a particularly important role in pioneer soil formation, supplying fixed nitrogen to support the weathering activity of early-successional plants on nitrogen-poor parent material (Frontiers in Microbiology, 2022; ScienceDirect, Contribution of rhizospheric processes to mineral weathering in forest soils). Certain soil bacteria can also directly solubilize mineral-bound nutrients through the production of organic acids and siderophores, a capacity that has attracted agronomic interest as a potential biofertilizer strategy to reduce reliance on synthetic fertilizer inputs (PMC7759553, Use of Mineral Weathering Bacteria to Enhance Nutrient Availability in Crops: A Review).

5.3 The phosphorus cycle: a lithosphere-dominated nutrient cycle

Unlike carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur, phosphorus has no significant gas-phase form under normal environmental conditions and therefore lacks an atmospheric leg entirely — its biogeochemical cycle runs exclusively through rock, soil, water, and biota (Wikipedia contributors, Phosphorus cycle). Phosphorus is released to the biosphere almost entirely through the weathering of phosphate-bearing minerals, especially apatite, a process governed by temperature, biological activity, tectonic uplift rates, and atmospheric oxygen and CO₂ partial pressures, and typically requiring thousands to millions of years to complete at the mineral-grain scale (Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, Drivers of the global phosphorus cycle over geological time, 2024; Fiveable, Weathering and Phosphorus Release). Because phosphorus availability frequently limits primary production in both terrestrial and marine ecosystems, and because — unlike nitrogen — there is no atmospheric reservoir from which deficits can be replenished biologically, the phosphorus cycle is tightly coupled to tectonic and weathering processes over geological time and is understood to have co-evolved with the oxygen cycle across major transitions such as the Great Oxidation Event (Section 4.3) (PMC12528410).

5.4 Enhanced weathering as a climate mitigation strategy

The recognition that silicate weathering constitutes a natural, if slow, atmospheric CO₂ removal mechanism (Section 3.3) has motivated a growing field of applied research into "enhanced weathering" (EW): spreading crushed, fast-weathering silicate rock — most commonly basalt, or industrial byproducts such as steel slag — onto agricultural soils to accelerate the natural weathering reaction and generate bicarbonate that is ultimately exported to rivers, the ocean, and long-term geological storage, while simultaneously buffering soil acidity and releasing plant-available nutrients (base cations, silicon, and trace elements) as co-benefits (PNAS, Enhanced weathering in the US Corn Belt delivers carbon removal with agronomic benefits, 2024).

Field trial results to date are mixed but informative. A large-scale, multi-year trial in the U.S. Corn Belt (Illinois) applying basalt to a maize-soybean rotation documented reproducible evidence of carbon removal alongside measurable increases in soil pH, fertility, and crop yield (PNAS, 2024; arXiv:2307.05343). A three-year Midwestern U.S. field trial comparing basalt and steel slag amendments found that steel slag — applied to initially acidic soil — produced strong, detectable evidence of weathering-driven alkalinity generation and carbon dioxide removal, whereas basalt's signal was weaker under the trial's soil and climate conditions (Frontiers in Climate, Evidence for carbon dioxide removal via enhanced rock weathering with steel slag, though not basalt, 2025). A separate three-year trial reported a sustained but quantitatively limited enhanced-weathering signal, underscoring that measured CO₂ removal rates in real field conditions often fall well short of laboratory-derived theoretical maxima, with substantial site-to-site variability driven by soil mineralogy, hydrology, and climate (ACS Environmental Science & Technology, Three Years of Field Trials Indicate a Sustained Enhanced Rock Weathering Signal with Limited CO2 Removal, 2025). Complementary work on vineyard soils in Switzerland similarly detected a weathering signal but with low associated CO₂ removal over a 1,000-day observation period, and research into iron-chelating soil amendments has explored whether weathering rates can be further accelerated biotechnologically (PMC11238546, Iron Chelation in Soil: Scalable Biotechnology for Accelerating Carbon Dioxide Removal by Enhanced Rock Weathering). Collectively, this body of field research indicates that enhanced weathering is measurable and can deliver genuine agronomic co-benefits, but that its per-hectare carbon removal efficiency is highly context-dependent and, at present, more modest than early techno-economic assessments assumed — a case study developed further in Section 9.3.

5.5 Soil formation and the lithosphere–biosphere nutrient interface

Soil itself is the emergent product of the interaction between rock (parent material), climate (via weathering, principally water- and temperature-mediated), organisms (via biological weathering, organic matter input, and bioturbation), topography, and time — the classical "CLORPT" framework of soil genesis. Because soil is simultaneously a weathering product of the lithosphere and the rooting medium for terrestrial primary production, its geochemical composition directly determines the elemental composition of the food crops grown in it, providing the physical link between planetary-scale rock chemistry and human nutritional health that is developed in Section 8.1.

5.6 Synthesis

Rock and mineral weathering is the connective tissue of this review's four-part scheme. It is where water performs its geochemical work (Section 2.4), where oxidative "fire" chemistry operates on geological timescales via the carbonate-silicate cycle (Section 3.3), where biology actively accelerates otherwise sluggish inorganic reactions via the rhizosphere, and where the phosphorus cycle — absent an atmospheric leg — most starkly illustrates the dependence of the biosphere on lithospheric supply. It is also, via enhanced weathering, the domain in which geochemical understanding is being most directly translated into a proposed climate mitigation technology.

6. Integration: Coupled Earth-System Cycles and Feedbacks

6.1 Why the cycles cannot be treated in isolation

Sections 2 through 5 treated water, fire (carbon/redox), wind (atmospheric gases and circulation), and rock (weathering) as separate domains for expository convenience. In the real Earth system they are a single, tightly coupled dynamical network. Earth system science formalizes this network as a set of interconnected reservoirs (atmosphere, ocean, biosphere, crust, mantle) linked by fluxes, and treats the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and water as mutually interdependent rather than independently tractable (ScienceDirect Topics, Biogeochemical Cycle; Frontiers in Environmental Science, 2025). A recent synthesis explicitly frames the research challenge of the coming decades as integrating these elemental cycles with anthropogenic pressures and the planetary boundaries framework into a single predictive picture, rather than continuing to model each cycle in isolation (Frontiers in Environmental Science, 2025).

6.2 Illustrative coupling mechanisms

Several coupling mechanisms recur throughout Sections 2–5 and merit explicit restatement:

Water–carbon coupling. Photosynthesis and respiration both consume or release water alongside carbon (Section 2.1, Section 3.1); at the ecosystem scale, evapotranspiration from vegetation is mechanistically linked to carbon uptake through stomatal regulation, so that changes in the carbon cycle (e.g., elevated CO₂ altering stomatal conductance) feed back on the water cycle, and vice versa — a coupling made concrete in the Amazon Basin case study (Section 9.1), where forest loss disrupts both local rainfall generation and regional carbon storage simultaneously.

Water–rock–carbon coupling. Silicate weathering (Section 5.1) requires liquid water to proceed and consumes atmospheric CO₂, directly linking the hydrological cycle to the long-term carbon cycle and planetary thermostat (Section 3.3). Regions or eras with more precipitation and runoff weather silicate rock faster, drawing down more CO₂ — meaning that hydrological cycle intensification under warming (Section 2.3) could, in principle, provide a partial negative feedback on climate over very long timescales, though this operates far too slowly to offset near-term anthropogenic emissions.

Nitrogen–oxygen–phosphorus coupling. The Great Oxidation Event (Section 4.3) appears to have been mechanistically coupled to marine phosphorus availability (Section 5.3), illustrating that the oxygen and phosphorus cycles co-evolved rather than developing independently; today, industrial nitrogen fixation (Section 4.2) interacts with phosphorus loading to jointly drive coastal eutrophication (Section 9.5), since both nutrients typically must be co-limiting for algal blooms to be fully suppressed.

Wind–water–rock coupling. Aeolian dust transport (Section 4.5) delivers lithospheric material (iron-bearing mineral dust) directly into the ocean, where it interacts with the water-mediated biological carbon pump, linking atmospheric circulation, rock-derived nutrients, and the marine carbon cycle in a single flux pathway.

Sulfur–carbon–climate coupling. Marine sulfur cycling generates cloud-nucleating aerosol (Section 4.4) that affects regional albedo and precipitation, while anthropogenic sulfate aerosol from fossil fuel combustion (the same combustion driving CO₂ emissions, Section 3.2) exerts a net cooling effect that partially — and temporarily — masks greenhouse warming, a coupling with direct policy relevance since reducing sulfur emissions for air-quality reasons can, in the near term, unmask additional warming (Section 7.1).

6.3 Feedback loops: stabilizing and destabilizing

The carbonate-silicate cycle (Section 3.3) is the paradigmatic negative (stabilizing) feedback loop in Earth system biogeochemistry, operating over hundreds of thousands of years. Over shorter, human-relevant timescales, several positive (destabilizing) feedback loops are of greater concern. Permafrost thaw releases previously frozen organic carbon as CO₂ and methane, amplifying warming that further accelerates thaw. Amazon deforestation reduces evapotranspiration and moisture recycling (Section 9.1), which dries the regional atmosphere and further stresses remaining forest, a self-reinforcing dynamic that a substantial modeling literature now frames explicitly as a potential ecosystem "tipping point" (IOP Science, Deforestation triggering irreversible transition in Amazon hydrological cycle, 2022). Wildfire-driven emissions (Section 3.4) can similarly self-reinforce under warming and drying trends, converting terrestrial carbon sinks into sources. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report explicitly identifies this asymmetry between fast-acting positive feedbacks and slow-acting negative feedbacks as central to climate risk: "each additional increment of global warming" intensifies concurrent, compounding hazards, and some future changes are already committed to be unavoidable or irreversible on human timescales even as others remain avoidable through emissions reductions (IPCC, AR6 Synthesis Report: Climate Change 2023).

6.4 Synthesis

No single classical element — water, fire, or wind — nor the connective rock/lithosphere domain, can be understood in isolation from the others once the analysis moves from molecular mechanism to planetary system. The remainder of this review (Sections 7–9) examines what happens when this coupled system is subjected to a rapid, geologically anomalous anthropogenic perturbation, and Section 10 considers the research and policy implications.

7. Anthropogenic Perturbation and Climate Change

7.1 Fossil fuel combustion and the carbon cycle

The dominant anthropogenic perturbation to the coupled cycles described above is fossil fuel combustion, which each year transfers tens of billions of tonnes of carbon that had been sequestered in the lithosphere for millions of years into the fast, atmosphere-ocean-biosphere carbon cycle over a timescale of decades — a rate of transfer with no clear analogue in at least the last several hundred thousand years of the geological record. As detailed in Section 3.2, 2024 fossil CO₂ emissions were estimated at 37.4 billion tonnes, continuing a decades-long upward trend with "no sign" of a global peak (Global Carbon Budget 2024). The atmospheric CO₂ concentration reached roughly 422.5 ppm in 2024, 52% above the pre-industrial baseline (Global Carbon Budget 2024). Because the natural removal processes capable of drawing this CO₂ back down — ocean uptake (operating over centuries) and silicate weathering (operating over hundreds of thousands of years, Section 3.3) — are far slower than the rate of anthropogenic emission, atmospheric CO₂ concentration and its associated radiative forcing will continue to rise for as long as emissions exceed these slow removal fluxes.

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report (AR6 SYR), representing the consensus of several hundred contributing scientists across three working groups, concludes that the window to secure a "liveable and sustainable future" is rapidly closing, that some further warming and associated changes are already committed and unavoidable, but that the most severe impacts remain avoidable through deep, rapid, and sustained emissions reductions that could begin to slow warming within roughly two decades of implementation (IPCC, AR6 Synthesis Report: Climate Change 2023; World Resources Institute, Top Findings from the IPCC Climate Change Report 2023).

7.2 Industrial nitrogen fixation and eutrophication

As detailed in Section 4.2, the Haber-Bosch process and fossil-fuel-related nitrogen oxide emissions have more than doubled the pre-industrial rate of global nitrogen fixation. The agricultural benefit — synthetic nitrogen fertilizer underpins a substantial fraction of global food production — is real and well established, but the efficiency of nitrogen use in most agricultural systems is poor: crops typically take up only around 40% of applied nitrogen fertilizer in intensively farmed watersheds such as the U.S. Corn Belt, with the remainder running off into waterways or volatilizing to the atmosphere (Mississippi River Collaborative, Dead Zone). This excess nitrogen, together with phosphorus from agricultural and wastewater sources, drives coastal eutrophication and hypoxia, exemplified by the Gulf of Mexico dead zone examined in Section 9.5.

7.3 Land-use change and hydrological alteration

Deforestation, wetland drainage, and large-scale irrigation development alter regional and, in the case of large forest biomes, potentially global hydrological cycling by changing evapotranspiration rates, surface albedo, and moisture recycling pathways (Section 2.3, Section 4.1). The Amazon Basin — examined in detail in Section 9.1 — is the paradigmatic case: two decades of accumulated deforestation have measurably dried the regional atmosphere by reducing evapotranspiration-driven moisture recycling, with researchers now describing a possible approach toward an irreversible hydrological "tipping point" at an estimated 20–25% cumulative deforestation combined with 1.5–2.0°C of global warming (IOP Science, 2022; MAAP, The Amazon Tipping Point).

7.4 Ocean acidification

Roughly 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions are absorbed by the ocean, where dissolution re-equilibrates the seawater carbonate system, releasing hydrogen ions and lowering pH — a process termed ocean acidification (Annual Reviews, The Impacts of Ocean Acidification on Marine Ecosystems and Reliant Human Communities). Because many marine organisms — corals, mollusks, some plankton groups, and other calcifiers — construct shells and skeletons from biogenic calcium carbonate, the reduced carbonate saturation state associated with acidification directly impairs calcification, and the current rate of seawater chemistry change is understood to be without precedent in comparison to past mass-extinction-associated ocean acidification events preserved in the geological record (Annual Reviews; National Academies, Effects of Ocean Acidification on Marine Ecosystems). Ocean acidification is thus a direct mechanistic link between the "fire" domain (fossil combustion emissions) and marine biochemistry, mediated entirely by ocean water chemistry (the "water" domain) — a clean illustration of the cross-domain coupling discussed in Section 6.

7.5 Mitigation geochemistry

Several of the mitigation strategies now under active research and deployment are, in effect, deliberate accelerations of natural biogeochemical processes described earlier in this review: enhanced silicate weathering (Section 5.4) accelerates the natural carbonate-silicate thermostat; afforestation and reforestation accelerate net biological carbon uptake (Section 3.2); and reduced fossil fuel combustion directly addresses the root cause common to Sections 7.1, 7.4, and much of Section 8. The IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report emphasizes that solutions are available and that beginning deep emissions reductions now could measurably slow warming within roughly two decades (IPCC, AR6 SYR).

7.6 Synthesis

Anthropogenic perturbation of the coupled water-fire-wind-rock system is not a single intervention but a set of simultaneous, mechanistically linked disruptions — fossil combustion, industrial nitrogen fixation, land-use change — that touch every biogeochemical cycle discussed in this review. Their consequences for human health specifically are the subject of Section 8; concrete illustrations of these dynamics operating in real systems are presented in Section 9.

8. Environmental and Health Dimensions

8.1 Combustion byproducts and cardiovascular/respiratory health

Fine and ultrafine particulate matter (PM2.5 and smaller), generated predominantly by the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and biomass (Section 3.5), is now recognized as the largest single environmental health risk globally and ranks among the top modifiable risk factors for disease overall (Frontiers in Endocrinology, Particulate Matter Air Pollution: Effects on the Cardiovascular System, 2018). Airborne particulate matter has been mechanistically linked to impaired vascular endothelial function, elevated blood pressure, cardiac arrhythmia, blood clotting abnormalities, coronary artery disease, and stroke, with both acute (hours to days) and chronic (years) exposure associated with increased cardiovascular mortality (PMC6250783, Particulate Matter Air Pollution: Effects on the Cardiovascular System; PMC8941724, The cardiovascular effects of air pollution: Prevention and reversal by pharmacological agents). Cardiovascular disease is estimated to account for roughly 80% of all premature deaths attributable to air pollution exposure (Frontiers in Endocrinology, 2018). Combustion-derived fine and ultrafine particles — most strongly associated with diesel engine emissions and other fossil-combustion sources — penetrate more deeply into the small airways and alveoli than coarser particulate matter and are correspondingly associated with the strongest adverse respiratory and cardiovascular effects (PMC6250783; JCI, Particulate matter air pollution: effects on the respiratory system, 2024; PMC9370264, The Impact of Fine Particulate Matter 2.5 on the Cardiovascular System: A Review of the Invisible Killer).

8.2 Water quality, geochemistry, and micronutrient health

Section 2.5 introduced water's role as a vector for both contaminants and, more positively, dissolved minerals. The geochemical composition of soils and the water and crops grown in them directly determines dietary micronutrient intake at the population level. Deficiencies in essential trace elements — selenium, zinc, copper, iron, manganese, molybdenum, and others — remain a major global health burden, often termed "hidden hunger" because deficiency can coexist with adequate caloric intake (jscimedcentral, Impact of Trace Elements in the Natural Environment and Public Health: A Medical Geology Perspective; MDPI, Soil Properties and Microelement Availability in Crops for Human Health: An Overview). Soil micronutrient status has been directly linked to measurable population health outcomes: a large study of Indian agricultural soils found that a one-standard-deviation improvement in soil zinc adequacy was associated with approximately eleven fewer stunted children per thousand, and that soil iron availability was positively associated with population hemoglobin levels — a direct, quantifiable link between lithospheric geochemistry (Section 5) and human biochemistry (PMC10442378, Soil micronutrients linked to human health in India). Because trace element bioavailability in the food chain is governed by soil geochemical behavior — pH, redox state, organic matter content, and mineralogy — agricultural and land-management practices that alter soil chemistry (including, potentially, enhanced weathering interventions, Section 5.4) carry nutritional as well as climatic consequences that merit joint evaluation.

8.3 Climate change and health

Beyond the specific combustion and micronutrient pathways above, climate change acts on human health through several additional, well-documented channels. Rising temperatures and more frequent, intense heatwaves are directly associated with excess mortality; recent attribution research estimates that approximately 37% of heat-related deaths are attributable to human-induced climate change, with heat-related mortality among people over 65 having risen by roughly 70% over two decades (WHO, Climate change and health; ScienceDirect, Deciphering the impact of heat wave in the global surge of infectious diseases). Altered temperature, precipitation, and humidity regimes are reshaping the geographic range and transmission season of vector-borne diseases transmitted by ectothermic arthropod vectors (mosquitoes, ticks, sandflies), with vector-borne disease mortality currently exceeding 700,000 deaths annually worldwide and projected to increase without intensified prevention efforts (Nature Reviews Microbiology, Effects of climate change and human activities on vector-borne diseases, 2024; PMC6378404, Impact of recent and future climate change on vector-borne diseases). Water scarcity driven by heat and altered precipitation (Section 2.3, Section 7.3) forces some communities toward contaminated water sources, elevating risk of waterborne infectious disease (WHO, Climate change and health). Consistent with the coupling logic of Section 6, these climate-health pathways are not independent: heat, water stress, air pollution, and vector-borne disease risk frequently co-occur and interact within the same vulnerable populations and regions.

8.4 Environmental justice and disparities in biogeochemical exposure

The health burdens described above are not evenly distributed. A substantial and growing literature documents that air pollution exposure — and, by extension, the cardiovascular and respiratory disease burden it drives — is systematically higher in lower socioeconomic-status and racial/ethnic minority communities, a pattern most robustly documented in North America and increasingly examined, with more heterogeneous findings, in European, Asian, and African contexts (PMC4626327, Socioeconomic Disparities and Air Pollution Exposure: A Global Review; PMC11727678, Alignment of Air Pollution Exposure Inequality Metrics with Environmental Justice and Equity Goals in the United States). Because atmospheric circulation and industrial siting jointly determine pollutant dispersion and exposure (Section 4.7), environmental justice concerns are directly downstream of the "wind" domain of this review — the same circulation that transports Saharan dust to the Amazon or sulfate aerosol across ocean basins also concentrates locally emitted pollutants disproportionately over specific, often marginalized, communities. Climate-driven and vulnerable populations — including women, children, older adults, people with pre-existing health conditions, and displaced or migrant populations — are also disproportionately exposed to the heat, water scarcity, and disease-transmission risks discussed in Section 8.3 (WHO, Climate change and health).

8.5 Synthesis

Every health outcome discussed in this section traces back to one or more of the biogeochemical processes described in Sections 2–7: particulate matter to combustion chemistry (Section 3), micronutrient status to rock and soil geochemistry (Section 5), and climate-driven disease risk to the coupled hydrological, atmospheric, and carbon cycles (Sections 2, 4, 7). The biochemistry of the individual human body is, in this sense, continuous with — not separate from — the biogeochemistry of the planet that supplies its air, water, and food.

9. Case Studies

9.1 The Amazon Basin: water-carbon coupling and tipping-point risk

The Amazon rainforest generates a substantial fraction of its own rainfall through evapotranspiration-driven moisture recycling: air masses arriving from the Atlantic release moisture as rain, which is transpired back into the atmosphere by the forest canopy and carried further west, recycling roughly five to six times as it crosses the basin in what has been termed the system of "flying rivers" (MAAP, The Amazon Tipping Point; Amazon Aid, The Hydrological Cycle). This self-generated rainfall — estimated to constitute roughly a tenth of all land-vegetation moisture recycling globally, more than the daily discharge of the Amazon River itself — is directly dependent on forest cover (MAAP). Two decades of accumulated deforestation have already measurably dried the regional atmosphere: reduced evapotranspiration weakens the moisture-recycling feedback, while deforestation-driven surface warming enhances buoyant atmospheric updrafts that reduce the downward mixing of moisture from the tropical Atlantic that would otherwise replenish the forest (IOP Science, Deforestation-induced immediate and delayed shifts in moisture recycling, 2025; IOP Science, Deforestation triggering irreversible transition in Amazon hydrological cycle, 2022). Modeling work has proposed a tipping-point threshold at approximately 20–25% cumulative deforestation combined with 1.5–2.0°C of global warming, beyond which large areas of remaining rainforest could transition irreversibly to a drier savanna-like state (MAAP; IOP Science, 2022). The Amazon case thus illustrates, at basin scale, the water-carbon coupling and positive-feedback dynamics introduced in Section 6.2–6.3: forest loss simultaneously releases stored carbon (the "fire"/carbon domain) and undermines the hydrological cycle that sustains the remaining forest (the "water" domain), each reinforcing the other.

9.2 Wind-driven ocean upwelling and dust fertilization

Eastern boundary current upwelling systems (Section 4.6) and Saharan dust deposition over the tropical Atlantic (Section 4.5) together illustrate how atmospheric circulation directly regulates ocean biogeochemistry via two independent mechanisms — physical nutrient resupply from depth, and aeolian delivery of the limiting micronutrient iron. Long-range dust transport measurably increases the bioavailability of the iron it carries as it ages in the atmosphere, with iron transported farther from the Sahara showing greater bioreactivity upon deposition (Frontiers in Marine Science, 2024). This iron fuels phytoplankton primary production and, through the biological carbon pump, links wind-driven mineral transport (a "rock" and "wind" process) directly to the ocean's capacity to sequester atmospheric carbon (a "fire"/carbon process) — a clean illustration of the cross-domain coupling emphasized throughout this review.

9.3 Enhanced rock weathering field trials: geochemistry as climate intervention

As detailed in Section 5.4, multi-year enhanced weathering field trials in the U.S. Corn Belt, the U.S. Midwest more broadly, and European vineyard soils have moved the carbonate-silicate thermostat concept (Section 3.3) from theory to field-testable practice. Results to date demonstrate that the underlying weathering chemistry is real and measurable under field conditions and can deliver genuine co-benefits — improved soil pH, fertility, and, in the Corn Belt trial, crop yield (PNAS, 2024) — but that quantitative carbon removal efficiency varies substantially with rock type, soil chemistry, and climate, with steel slag outperforming basalt in at least one acidic Midwestern soil trial and per-hectare removal often falling below laboratory-derived projections (Frontiers in Climate, 2025; ACS ES&T, 2025). This case study exemplifies both the promise and the current empirical limitations of translating geochemical theory directly into climate policy.

9.4 Wildfire-affected ecosystems: fire biogeochemistry under climate change

Wildfire regimes are shifting toward greater frequency and severity across numerous fire-prone biomes under warming and drying trends, converting the pyrogenic carbon dynamics described in Section 3.4 from a background biogeochemical process into an increasingly dominant driver of regional carbon budgets. Because pyrogenic carbon is both unusually persistent in soils and sediments (owing to its condensed aromatic structure) and a source of dissolved organic matter and altered nutrient dynamics once mobilized into river networks, shifting fire regimes carry compound consequences for terrestrial carbon storage, water quality, and downstream aquatic ecosystems simultaneously (Frontiers in Environmental Science, 2017; ACS ES&T, 2024) — again illustrating how a single perturbation (fire regime shift) propagates across the water, fire, and rock domains of this review at once.

9.5 The Gulf of Mexico dead zone: nitrogen-driven eutrophication

The Gulf of Mexico hypoxic ("dead") zone, the second-largest such zone globally, forms annually where nutrient-laden Mississippi River discharge enters the Gulf. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff across the Mississippi River Basin — drawing from major farming states including Minnesota, Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, and others — triggers rapid algal blooms; decomposition of the resulting biomass consumes dissolved oxygen faster than it can be replenished, creating hypoxic conditions lethal or repellent to most marine life (Mississippi River Collaborative, Dead Zone; Mississippi River Delta, Explaining the Gulf of Mexico Dead Zone). NOAA estimated the 2023 dead zone at approximately 6,705 square miles, with documented impacts on over 40% of the U.S. fishing industry and estimated annual economic losses of roughly $82 million (Mississippi River Collaborative). Because crops typically assimilate only around 40% of applied nitrogen fertilizer, with the remainder available for runoff and leaching, the Gulf dead zone is a direct, quantifiable downstream consequence of the industrial nitrogen fixation perturbation described in Section 4.2 and Section 7.2 — arguably the clearest single-watershed illustration in this review of how an atmospheric-cycle perturbation (excess fixed nitrogen) propagates through the water domain to produce a marine biogeochemical and economic outcome.

9.6 Cross-case synthesis

These five cases, though geographically and mechanistically distinct, share a common structure: in each, a perturbation nominally confined to one classical-element domain (deforestation in the water/carbon domain, dust and wind-driven upwelling in the wind domain, enhanced weathering in the rock domain, fire regime shift in the fire domain, nitrogen fertilization in the wind/nitrogen domain) propagates measurably into at least one other domain, generating consequences — climatic, ecological, economic, or health-related — that would not be predicted from a single-domain analysis. This is the empirical core of the integration argument developed conceptually in Section 6.

10. Future Directions and Conclusion

10.1 Research priorities

Several research gaps recur across the sections of this review and merit explicit priority. First, quantitative uncertainty in cycle-coupling estimates remains substantial: the observed amplification rate of the global hydrological cycle under warming (Section 2.3) appears, in at least one salinity-based reconstruction, to exceed simple thermodynamic (Clausius-Clapeyron) predictions, and reconciling observational, reanalysis, and model-based estimates of water cycle intensification remains an open problem (PMC5146653). Second, the long-term fate of pyrogenic carbon (Section 3.4) — whether it functions predominantly as a durable carbon sink or is gradually degraded and returned to active cycling — remains debated and has first-order implications for whether shifting global fire regimes should be modeled as a net carbon source or a partially offsetting sink (PMC10867845). Third, enhanced weathering field trials (Sections 5.4, 9.3) have so far been conducted across a limited range of soil types, climates, and rock feedstocks; broader, longer-duration trials with standardized measurement protocols are needed before per-hectare carbon removal estimates can be extrapolated with confidence to global mitigation potential. Fourth, the mechanistic coupling between the nitrogen, phosphorus, and oxygen cycles first identified over Earth's deep geological history (Section 4.3, Section 5.3) may hold lessons for anticipating how modern, human-driven nutrient cycle perturbations will propagate over centennial timescales — a research direction that would benefit from closer dialogue between geobiologists working on Precambrian Earth and biogeochemists working on the contemporary Anthropocene.

10.2 Policy implications

Because fossil fuel combustion is the common driver of anthropogenic carbon cycle disruption (Section 7.1), ocean acidification (Section 7.4), and a large share of the cardiovascular and respiratory disease burden attributable to air pollution (Section 8.1), interventions that reduce combustion — renewable electrification, transportation electrification, industrial decarbonization — offer joint climate and public-health returns rather than requiring a tradeoff between the two goals. Similarly, because nitrogen use efficiency in major agricultural systems remains low (with roughly 40% crop uptake of applied fertilizer in intensively farmed watersheds, Section 7.2, Section 9.5), precision agriculture and improved fertilizer management represent a policy lever capable of simultaneously reducing input costs for farmers, mitigating coastal eutrophication, and reducing nitrous oxide emissions (a potent greenhouse gas not discussed in depth here but directly linked to excess nitrogen fixation). The IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report's central conclusion — that solutions exist and that beginning deep, sustained emissions reductions now could measurably slow warming within roughly two decades — applies with particular force to these combustion- and nitrogen-centered interventions, which act on multiple biogeochemical cycles simultaneously (IPCC, AR6 SYR).

10.3 Conclusion

This review set out to use the classical elements — water, fire, and wind — as an organizing frame for a genuinely interdisciplinary synthesis spanning geochemistry, atmospheric and ocean science, biochemistry, and biomedicine, with rock and mineral weathering treated as the connective lithospheric substrate. The exercise reveals that the metaphor, while chemically obsolete, tracks a real structural feature of the Earth system: water, redox ("fire"), and gas-phase/atmospheric ("wind") processes are not merely analogous across scales from the molecular to the planetary — they are mechanistically continuous, linked by shared physical carriers (water as solvent and climate regulator; electrons as the common currency of combustion and respiration; wind as the transport medium for gases, dust, and moisture) that operate identically whether the relevant "reactor" is a mitochondrion, a wildfire, or a river basin. Human industrial activity over the past two centuries — and especially the past seventy years — has perturbed every one of these coupled cycles simultaneously and at a rate that appears to be without clear precedent in the geological record, with consequences that are now measurable in atmospheric chemistry, ocean chemistry, regional hydrology, and human disease burden alike. The same coupling that makes these perturbations propagate so extensively across domains, however, also means that a comparatively small number of targeted interventions — chiefly, reducing fossil fuel combustion and improving nitrogen use efficiency — carry disproportionately large, multi-domain benefits. Understanding water, fire, and wind together, rather than as separate disciplinary silos, is accordingly not merely an intellectual convenience but a practical necessity for both the science and the policy of the Earth system going forward.
