Abstract

This review synthesizes three literatures that are conventionally treated separately: the molecular embryology of the neural tube; the biochemistry of the male endocrine system, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG), hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA), hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT), and growth hormone-IGF-1 axes and the enzymology of steroidogenesis; and the cellular and molecular mechanisms by which gonadal hormones act upon neural tissue to produce a sexually differentiated brain. Beginning with neural induction and neurulation and proceeding through dorsoventral and rostrocaudal patterning, neural crest formation, and the pathogenesis of neural tube defects, the review establishes the anatomical substrate on which later hormonal signals act. It then details testicular steroidogenesis and the kisspeptin-dependent regulation of the HPG axis, before turning to the genetic and hormonal determinants of testis differentiation and genital masculinization, and the clinical syndromes — complete androgen insensitivity, 5α-reductase deficiency, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia — that arise from specific failures of this cascade. The organizational/activational hypothesis and the aromatization hypothesis are examined as the conceptual core of brain sexual differentiation, alongside the cellular (apoptosis, dendritic remodeling) and epigenetic (DNA methylation and demethylation) mechanisms that render a transient perinatal hormone signal into a durable change in neural architecture. Adult activational effects of testosterone on cognition and aggression, sex-biased neurodevelopmental disorders, and fetal glucocorticoid programming are considered as translational and clinical correlates, and the review closes with a systems-level synthesis of cross-talk between hormonal axes and a discussion of open questions for future research. Every empirical claim below is anchored to a specific, verifiable citation, compiled in the reference list at the end of the article.

Keywords: neuroembryology, neural tube, neurulation, hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, steroidogenesis, testosterone, aromatization, sexual differentiation of the brain, sexually dimorphic nucleus, epigenetics, disorders of sex development

PART I — INTRODUCTION AND CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

1. Introduction: The Man as a Biochemical Problem

To speak of "the Man" in biochemical terms is to speak of a cascade: a fertilized zygote carrying a Y chromosome, a bipotential gonadal ridge nudged toward testicular fate by a single transcription factor, a testis that begins to secrete hormones before the fetus is large enough to fit in an adult's palm, and a nervous system — beginning as a flat sheet of ectoderm, folding into a tube, patterning itself along three axes — that spends the following two decades being organized, then reorganized, then activated by those same hormones and their descendants. Masculinity, from a biochemical standpoint, is not a single event but a layered history of molecular decisions: which enzyme is expressed in which cell at which hour of gestation, which receptor is occupied by which ligand, which gene is methylated or demethylated in response to a transient hormonal signal that may never recur.

This review takes as its organizing thread three intertwined stories that are conventionally treated in separate literatures: (i) the molecular embryology of the neural tube — the structure from which the entire central nervous system, including every neuron that will later respond to androgens and estrogens, is built; (ii) the biochemistry of the male endocrine system — the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis and its companion axes (HPA, HPT, GH-IGF-1), together with the enzymology of steroidogenesis; and (iii) the mechanisms by which hormones act on neural tissue to produce a sexually differentiated brain, from the classical organizational/activational hypothesis first articulated by Phoenix, Goy, Gerall, and Young in 1959 [1] to the modern molecular understanding of aromatization, receptor signaling, and epigenetic remodeling [2,3,4,5].

The phrase "a multitude of hormones and different neural tubes" captures something important about the subject: there is no single hormone and no single neural tube. Steroid hormones (testosterone, dihydrotestosterone, estradiol, cortisol, allopregnanolone), peptide hormones (GnRH, kisspeptin, LH, FSH, ACTH, TRH, TSH, GH, AMH), and their many receptors and metabolizing enzymes form a network, not a pathway. Likewise, the neural tube is not a single structure but a rostrocaudally and dorsoventrally patterned column of tissue that gives rise to regionally distinct derivatives — forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, spinal cord — each with its own patterning code and its own subsequent sensitivity to circulating and locally synthesized steroids. This review is organized to honor that plurality while building, cumulatively, toward an integrated picture of the biochemistry that produces and maintains the male phenotype, in body and brain, across the lifespan.

1.1 Scope and Structure

The review proceeds in nine parts. Part II reconstructs the neuroembryology of the neural tube — the physical substrate on which every subsequent hormonal signal will act — from neural induction through neurulation, dorsoventral and rostrocaudal patterning, neural crest formation, and the pathogenesis of neural tube defects. Part III turns to the biochemistry of the male endocrine system: hormone classes and receptor mechanisms, the enzymology of steroidogenesis in the Leydig cell, the HPG axis and its kisspeptin-dependent regulation, puberty, and the companion HPA, HPT, and GH-IGF-1 axes, closing with the emerging biochemistry of locally synthesized neurosteroids. Part IV addresses genetic and hormonal sex determination and differentiation of the reproductive tract, and the clinical syndromes — androgen insensitivity, 5α-reductase deficiency, congenital adrenal hyperplasia — that arise when specific enzymatic or receptor steps fail. Part V is the fulcrum of the review: the mechanisms, cellular and molecular, by which the hormones described in Part III act upon the neural tissue described in Part II to produce a sexually differentiated brain. Part VI considers the activational, adult-life biochemistry of testosterone with respect to cognition and social behavior. Part VII surveys clinical and translational correlates — sex-biased neurodevelopmental disorders, fetal glucocorticoid programming, and endocrine disruption. Part VIII attempts a systems-level synthesis of cross-talk between axes, and Part IX closes with a summary and directions for future research.

Every specific empirical claim in this review is anchored to a real, citable source, identified by numbered reference and compiled in the reference list. 2. Principles of Hormonal Signaling in the Nervous System

Before turning to the specific axes and the specific developmental events that are the substance of this review, it is worth establishing the general biochemical vocabulary of hormone action, because the same handful of mechanistic motifs recur throughout: ligand-receptor binding, allosteric conformational change, nuclear translocation, DNA-binding at hormone response elements, coactivator recruitment, and — increasingly appreciated — rapid, membrane-initiated signaling that never touches the genome at all.

2.1 Steroid Hormones: Structure and Classical Genomic Action

All steroid hormones relevant to this review — testosterone, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), estradiol, progesterone, cortisol, and the various neurosteroids — derive from a single precursor, cholesterol, via a shared cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene ring system. Because they are lipophilic, steroids cross the plasma membrane by passive diffusion and, in the classical model, bind intracellular receptors of the nuclear receptor superfamily: the androgen receptor (AR), estrogen receptors ERα and ERβ, and the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), among others. Ligand binding induces a conformational change that releases the receptor from chaperone proteins (notably heat-shock protein 90), permits receptor dimerization, and drives translocation to the nucleus, where the receptor dimer binds palindromic or half-site hormone response elements in gene promoters and enhancers, recruiting coactivator or corepressor complexes that remodel chromatin and alter RNA polymerase II recruitment. This genomic pathway is slow (hours) and produces changes in transcription and, ultimately, in protein expression and cell phenotype.

2.2 Non-Genomic and Membrane-Initiated Signaling

Alongside this classical pathway, steroid receptors — and dedicated membrane-associated receptors such as GPER1 for estrogen — mediate rapid (seconds-to-minutes) non-genomic signaling from the plasma membrane or from pools of receptor associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. In neurons, membrane-initiated estrogen signaling triggers calcium mobilization, activation of MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt cascades, and modulation of ion channel gating, and interacts extensively with genomic signaling to shape synaptic plasticity. The androgen receptor likewise possesses non-genomic actions, with cytoplasmic AR able to initiate kinase signaling cascades that influence cell migration and proliferation independently of its transcriptional function. The coexistence of genomic and non-genomic pathways is functionally important for the developing nervous system, where rapid, transient hormone signals during discrete developmental windows must produce durable, "organized" changes in neural circuitry — a puzzle addressed at length in Part V.

2.3 Peptide Hormones and Second-Messenger Signaling

In contrast to steroids, peptide hormones — GnRH, kisspeptin, LH, FSH, ACTH, TRH, TSH, GH, AMH — are hydrophilic and act at cell-surface receptors, predominantly G-protein-coupled receptors (GnRH receptor, kisspeptin receptor KISS1R) or receptor tyrosine kinases and their relatives (the GH receptor signals through JAK2-STAT5; the AMH receptor is a serine/threonine kinase receptor related to the TGF-β receptor superfamily). These receptors transduce extracellular hormone binding into intracellular second-messenger cascades — cyclic AMP/protein kinase A, phospholipase C/inositol trisphosphate/diacylglycerol/protein kinase C, and JAK/STAT signaling — that in endocrine target cells frequently converge on the regulation of steroidogenic enzyme transcription, linking the peptide-hormone and steroid-hormone signaling worlds into the integrated axes described in Part III.

2.4 Historical Foundations of Endocrine Thinking

The conceptual apparatus summarized above — a chemical messenger, secreted by one tissue, traveling through the circulation, and acting on a distant target — is barely a century and a half old, and its establishment is worth recounting briefly because the logic of those founding experiments recurs throughout this review. The first rigorous demonstration that a gonad exerts its influence through the bloodstream, rather than through direct neural connection, was Arnold Berthold's 1849 experiment in roosters: Berthold castrated young cockerels, observed the expected failure of comb, plumage, and behavioral maturation, and then showed that re-implanting a testis at a distant, non-innervated site in the body cavity was sufficient to restore normal male development, proving that a diffusible testicular product, not a neural connection, was responsible [31]. This single experiment anticipated, by more than a century, the logic of the Jost castration-and-replacement experiments described in Section 19 and Part IV. The word "hormone" itself, and the general concept of chemical messengers acting via the bloodstream, was established somewhat later: William Bayliss and Ernest Starling demonstrated in 1902 that acidified duodenal contents release a substance — secretin — into the blood that stimulates pancreatic secretion at a distance, without requiring intact nervous connections between the two organs, and Starling subsequently coined the term "hormone" in 1905 to describe this general mode of physiological regulation [32]. Every subsequent axis considered in Part III — HPG, HPA, HPT, GH-IGF-1 — is a direct intellectual descendant of this basic conceptual move: physiological coordination between distant organs achieved by a diffusible chemical signal rather than exclusively by nervous connection.

2.5 Why This Matters for "The Man" as an Object of Study

The male phenotype — testicular differentiation, Wolffian duct virilization, external genital masculinization, and the sexual differentiation of the brain — is not produced by a single hormone acting through a single mechanism, but by a temporally choreographed sequence in which peptide hormones (from placenta, then hypothalamus and pituitary) drive steroidogenic enzyme expression in the testis, the resulting steroids act genomically and non-genomically on multiple target tissues including brain, and the transcriptional and epigenetic consequences of that action are, in the nervous system, retained long after the initiating hormone pulse has cleared. Every subsequent section of this review returns, in different anatomical and developmental contexts, to this same basic logic.

PART II — NEUROEMBRYOLOGY: BUILDING THE NEURAL TUBE

Before it can be shaped by hormones, the nervous system must first be built. This part reconstructs, in biochemical and molecular detail, the process by which a flat sheet of ectodermal cells becomes a closed, regionally patterned neural tube — the common origin of every brain structure discussed later in this review, including the hypothalamic nuclei that will prove exquisitely sensitive to testosterone and its metabolites.

3. Neural Induction and the Neural Plate

Neurulation is preceded by gastrulation, during which the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm — are established and the notochord, a rod of axial mesoderm, forms beneath the dorsal midline ectoderm. Neural induction is the process by which a subset of dorsal ectodermal cells is instructed to adopt neuroepithelial identity rather than epidermal identity. The classical "default model" of neural induction holds that ectodermal cells adopt a neural fate unless instructed otherwise by bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling; the organizer tissue (notochord and underlying axial mesoderm) secretes BMP antagonists — noggin, chordin, and follistatin — that locally inhibit BMP signaling in the overlying dorsal ectoderm, permitting the default neural program to proceed and defining the neural plate, a thickened, pseudostratified epithelium that runs the length of the embryonic dorsal midline [12].

4. Neurulation: Primary and Secondary

Neurulation heralds the development of the central nervous system, and in mammals it proceeds by two distinct mechanisms operating at different axial levels. Primary neurulation, which forms the brain and most of the spinal cord, converts the flat neural plate into a closed tube through a coordinated sequence of cellular events: the neural plate first bends at defined hinge points (the median hinge point, overlying the notochord, and paired dorsolateral hinge points), the neural folds elevate and converge toward the dorsal midline, and the folds fuse, converting the open neural plate into a closed neural tube whose lumen becomes the future ventricular system and central canal. Secondary neurulation, which forms the most caudal spinal cord (the lower sacral and coccygeal segments), instead proceeds by condensation of a solid medullary cord that subsequently cavitates to form a tube — a mechanism that does not require the same fold-and-fuse process and is consequently not susceptible to the same class of closure defects [12].

4.1 Cellular Mechanics of Closure

Modern accounts of neural tube closure, synthesized comprehensively by Nikolopoulou and colleagues [12], emphasize that closure is not a passive folding event but an actively driven morphogenetic process involving several coordinated cellular behaviors. Apical constriction — the actomyosin-dependent narrowing of the apical (luminal) surface of neuroepithelial cells, driven by RhoA/ROCK-dependent phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chain — helps to wedge cells into the characteristic shape required at the hinge points. Convergent extension, a cell-intercalation process controlled by the non-canonical Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway (involving core PCP proteins such as Vangl2, Celsr1, and Scribble), narrows and elongates the neural plate along the mediolateral and rostrocaudal axes respectively, bringing the neural folds into apposition. Interkinetic nuclear migration, in which the nuclei of neuroepithelial cells move apicobasally in register with the cell cycle, contributes to the pseudostratified architecture and apical crowding that facilitate bending. Superimposed on these cellular mechanisms, recent work has identified biomechanical inputs — hydrostatic pressure within the neural tube lumen and forces exerted by adjacent tissues (surface ectoderm, somites) — as additional determinants of successful closure [12].

4.2 Molecular Control: Transcription Factors and Failure of Closure

Neural tube closure requires the coordinated action of a set of transcription factors, prominently Grhl2, Grhl3, Pax3, Cdx2, and Zic2, which regulate the expression of cytoskeletal and adhesion genes required for hinge-point bending and fold fusion, in concert with Shh/BMP signaling and the non-canonical Wnt/PCP pathway described above [12]. Failure of closure at different axial levels produces the clinically important spectrum of neural tube defects (NTDs): failure of closure at the rostral neuropore produces anencephaly (absence of the forebrain and cranial vault), failure at intermediate spinal levels produces open spina bifida (myelomeningocele), and failure of the caudal neuropore or of secondary neurulation contributes to closed spinal dysraphisms. NTDs are among the most common structural birth defects, with a heterogeneous, multifactorial etiology involving both genetic susceptibility and environmental risk factors, prominently including maternal folate status [13].

5. Dorsoventral Patterning of the Neural Tube: Sonic Hedgehog and BMP

Once closed, the neural tube must be patterned along two principal axes so that different progenitor domains generate the correct classes of neurons in the correct positions. Dorsoventral (DV) patterning is imposed by two opposing morphogen sources: sonic hedgehog (Shh), secreted first by the notochord and then by the floor plate it induces, forms a ventral-to-dorsal concentration gradient, while BMP family ligands (BMP4, BMP7) secreted by the roof plate and overlying surface ectoderm form a complementary dorsal-to-ventral gradient. Progenitor cells interpret local Shh concentration through a specific gene-regulatory network of homeodomain and basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors (e.g., Nkx2.2, Olig2, Nkx6.1, Pax6, Irx3), each expressed within a threshold-defined domain, which cross-repress one another to sharpen boundaries and specify discrete progenitor domains — and ultimately discrete neuronal subtypes (motor neurons, distinct classes of interneurons) — at reproducible dorsoventral positions. Dorsally, BMP and Wnt signals cooperate to specify sensory relay neurons and other dorsal interneuron classes, completing a coordinate system that patterns the entire spinal cord and much of the hindbrain [9,12].

6. Rostrocaudal Patterning: Hox Genes, Retinoic Acid, and FGF

Orthogonal to dorsoventral patterning, the neural tube is regionalized along its rostrocaudal (anteroposterior) axis into forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord, and — within the hindbrain and spinal cord — into finer segmental units whose identity is specified by the combinatorial, nested expression of Hox genes, arranged in clusters (HoxA–D) whose 3′-to-5′ genomic order corresponds colinearly to increasingly posterior domains of expression. Retinoic acid (RA), synthesized from maternally and fetally derived retinol by aldehyde dehydrogenases (notably Raldh2) in paraxial mesoderm, is a principal instructive signal for this posterior Hox code: RA diffuses from its mesodermal source into the neural tube, activates retinoic acid receptors (RAR/RXR heterodimers) that bind RA response elements in Hox gene enhancers, and thereby induces expression of intermediate Hox paralogs (Hox1–Hox5) in the hindbrain and rostral spinal cord [14,17]. Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling, high at the posterior-most tip of the elongating embryo (the tailbud) and declining rostrally, opposes RA and promotes expression of more posterior Hox paralogs in the caudal spinal cord, so that the interaction of an RA gradient and an FGF gradient establishes the overall rostrocaudal Hox code. Recent chromatin-level studies show that RA signaling acts in part by triggering domain-wide, saltatory removal of repressive histone modifications (H3K27me3) across Hox gene clusters, converting broad chromatin domains from a Polycomb-repressed to a transcriptionally competent state in a manner that is itself regionally restricted — neural progenitors isolated from rostral versus caudal neural tube respond differently to identical RA exposure, reflecting a pre-established chromatin landscape [17]. Retinoic acid signaling additionally plays roles beyond initial patterning, contributing to motor neuron differentiation, axon outgrowth, and, in the mature nervous system, to neuronal maintenance and regeneration, with disrupted RA signaling implicated in adult neurodegeneration [14].

7. Neural Crest: A Fourth Germ Layer

At the border of the neural plate and adjacent surface ectoderm, a distinct multipotent cell population — the neural crest — is induced by combinatorial Wnt, BMP, and FGF signaling at the neural plate border. Neural crest cells are specified within the dorsal neural tube (or, in the case of cranial neural crest, the neural folds) and subsequently undergo an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, delaminate, and migrate extensively throughout the embryo along stereotyped pathways, guided by a combination of chemotactic cues (ephrin/Eph, semaphorin/neuropilin signaling) and permissive or restrictive extracellular matrix corridors. The developmental trajectory of the neural crest — induction, specification, delamination, migration, and terminal differentiation — is governed by a hierarchical gene regulatory network (GRN), the general architecture of which has recently been reviewed in detail by Bronner [15]. Cranial neural crest gives rise to most of the craniofacial skeleton and connective tissue, in addition to neurons and glia of the peripheral and enteric nervous systems; trunk neural crest gives rise to the dorsal root ganglia, sympathetic ganglia, adrenal medullary chromaffin cells, and melanocytes. Because chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla and sympathetic neurons are themselves neural crest derivatives, and because the adrenal cortex (a separate, mesodermally derived structure adjacent to the medulla) is itself an essential node in the HPA axis discussed in Part III, the neural crest establishes a direct embryological link between "neural tube" biology and the endocrine biochemistry that is the second major subject of this review. Neural crest gene regulatory networks are also increasingly implicated in a range of human "neurocristopathies," and the same core GRN components recur across vertebrate diversification and in disease contexts, underscoring the deep conservation of this developmental module [15].

8. Neural Tube Defects: Folate, Homocysteine, and One-Carbon Metabolism

The clinical importance of correct neural tube closure is illustrated starkly by neural tube defects, and by the biochemistry of their most important modifiable risk factor: maternal folate status. Up to 70% of NTDs can be prevented by maternal periconceptional folic acid supplementation, yet the precise metabolic mechanism underlying this protective effect remained, for decades, incompletely understood [13]. Folate, in its metabolically active tetrahydrofolate forms, serves as a one-carbon donor/acceptor in a set of interlinked metabolic cycles: the folate cycle proper, which interconverts different one-carbon-substituted tetrahydrofolate species; the methionine/homocysteine remethylation cycle, in which 5-methyltetrahydrofolate donates a methyl group (via methionine synthase, a vitamin B12-dependent enzyme) to homocysteine, regenerating methionine, which is subsequently converted to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor for DNA, histone, and protein methylation reactions; and the transsulfuration pathway, which catabolizes homocysteine to cysteine. A key regulatory enzyme in this network is methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), which irreversibly commits 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to the 5-methyltetrahydrofolate pool used for homocysteine remethylation; common polymorphisms in MTHFR (C677T and A1298C) reduce enzyme activity, elevate circulating homocysteine, and are associated with increased NTD risk in both epidemiological and mechanistic studies [13]. Moderately elevated maternal homocysteine has itself been proposed as a risk factor for NTDs, independent of folate status, implicating disturbed methylation capacity — and consequently impaired epigenetic regulation of genes required for neural tube closure — as a plausible unifying mechanism linking folate insufficiency, homocysteine elevation, and failure of neurulation [13]. Beyond folate-homocysteine metabolism, genetic screens have implicated components of the same signaling pathways described above — the non-canonical Wnt/PCP pathway, Shh/BMP signaling — as additional, and sometimes interacting, causes of NTDs, indicating that the "genetic" and "nutritional" accounts of neural tube defect pathogenesis converge on a shared set of molecular closure mechanisms [30].

8A. Neurogenesis and Gliogenesis: Cellular Architecture of the Patterned Tube

Once the neural tube has closed and been regionally patterned along its dorsoventral and rostrocaudal axes, the pseudostratified neuroepithelium that lines it must generate, in the correct numbers and at the correct times, every neuron and glial cell of the mature nervous system. This process is executed by radial glial cells, elongated progenitor cells whose cell bodies reside in the ventricular zone adjacent to the tube's lumen and whose basal processes extend to the outer (pial) surface, providing both a proliferative stem-cell compartment and a physical scaffold along which newly generated neurons migrate to their final laminar or nuclear position [33]. Neuroepithelial cells of the earliest neural tube transition into radial glia through characteristic transcriptional and morphological changes that establish neurogenic competence while preserving the apical-basal polarity required for interkinetic nuclear migration (introduced in Section 4.1); radial glia then proceed through an initial, expansive phase of symmetric, proliferative division to expand the progenitor pool, followed by a neurogenic phase of asymmetric division producing one self-renewing radial glial daughter and one differentiating neuronal daughter, and finally, in most regions, a later gliogenic phase in which the same progenitor population switches to generating astrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursors [33]. This proliferative germinal compartment persists, in modified and reduced form, into postnatal and even adult life in specific regions (notably the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles), representing a continuum between the embryonic ventricular zone described here and the comparatively restricted zones of adult neurogenesis [33]. The regional identity established by Shh/BMP dorsoventral patterning (Section 5) and Hox/retinoic-acid rostrocaudal patterning (Section 6) is inherited by radial glia and, through them, imposed upon the specific neuronal subtypes they generate — meaning that the hypothalamic and extended-amygdala neurons discussed at length in Part V, with their eventual capacity for androgen and estrogen receptor expression and steroid-dependent apoptosis, are lineal descendants of radial glial progenitors whose regional identity was fixed during the earliest phases of neural tube patterning described in Sections 5 and 6.

9. Summary of Part II

By the time the neural tube has closed and become rostrocaudally and dorsoventrally patterned, it has already established the basic anatomical scaffold — forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, spinal cord, and the migratory derivatives of the neural crest — onto which the hormonal signals discussed in the remainder of this review will act. Critically, several of the structures that will later prove central to the story of male brain sexual differentiation — the hypothalamus, an unpaired ventral forebrain derivative patterned in part by Shh signaling, and the preoptic area and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis discussed at length in Part V — are direct descendants of this early patterning process, and their capacity to respond to gonadal steroids later in development is itself dependent on the correct prior execution of the neuroembryological program described in this Part. ## PART III — THE MALE ENDOCRINE SYSTEM: AXES AND BIOCHEMISTRY

Having established the neuroembryological substrate in Part II, this Part turns to the second pillar of the review: the biochemistry of the male endocrine system, understood as a set of interlocking hypothalamic-pituitary-target organ axes, together with the enzymology of steroid hormone biosynthesis and the newly appreciated biochemistry of steroids synthesized locally within the brain itself.

10. Steroidogenesis: From Cholesterol to Testosterone

10.1 Cellular Localization

In the adult male, testosterone biosynthesis occurs almost exclusively in the Leydig cells, an interstitial cell population of the testis situated between the seminiferous tubules. Cholesterol substrate is derived predominantly from de novo synthesis from acetyl-CoA within the Leydig cell, supplemented by uptake of circulating lipoprotein-derived cholesterol via the LDL receptor and scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-B1) pathways, and by mobilization of stored cholesteryl esters via hormone-sensitive lipase [10].

10.2 The Steroidogenic Pathway

The rate-limiting and hormonally regulated step of steroidogenesis is the transport of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane, mediated by the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), followed by the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone by the cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme (CYP11A1, also called P450scc), located on the inner mitochondrial membrane. This step is under the direct control of luteinizing hormone (LH) acting through the LH receptor, a Gs-protein-coupled receptor that raises intracellular cyclic AMP and activates protein kinase A, which in turn phosphorylates StAR and upregulates its transcription [10]. Pregnenolone then exits the mitochondrion and undergoes further processing in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, where testosterone can be synthesized via two parallel routes: the Δ5 (or "backdoor" progenitor) pathway, in which pregnenolone is converted to 17α-hydroxypregnenolone and then dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) by the combined 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase activities of CYP17A1, and DHEA is subsequently converted to androstenediol and then testosterone; and the Δ4 pathway, in which pregnenolone is first converted to progesterone by 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSD3B2), then to 17α-hydroxyprogesterone and androstenedione by CYP17A1, with androstenedione finally reduced to testosterone by 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 3 (HSD17B3). Both routes converge on testosterone, the principal circulating androgen in the adult male. Recent work using HSD17B3-deficient mouse models has clarified that alternative 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase isoforms can partially compensate for loss of HSD17B3 activity, revealing redundancy within this final reductive step and refining the classical linear model of the pathway.

10.3 Downstream Metabolism: DHT and Estradiol

Testosterone itself is a prohormone for two biochemically critical downstream products. In androgen-target tissues expressing 5α-reductase (two principal isozymes, SRD5A1 and SRD5A2), testosterone is irreversibly reduced to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a more potent androgen receptor ligand with higher receptor-binding affinity and slower dissociation kinetics than testosterone itself; DHT is essential for virilization of the external genitalia and prostate, as detailed in Part IV. Separately, in tissues expressing aromatase (CYP19A1) — including adipose tissue, and, critically for the subject of Part V, specific neurons and glia of the developing and adult brain — testosterone is converted to 17β-estradiol. This local, intracellular conversion of an androgen precursor into an estrogen is the biochemical basis of the aromatization hypothesis of brain masculinization, discussed at length in Part V.

10.4 Regulation Beyond LH

While LH is the principal regulator of Leydig cell steroidogenesis via the cAMP/PKA/StAR axis described above, a broader signaling network modulates Leydig cell function, including growth hormone, prolactin, and locally acting growth factors (insulin, IGF-1, FGF, EGF) and, more recently identified, the osteoblast-derived hormone osteocalcin, which acts on a Leydig cell G-protein-coupled receptor (GPRC6A) to potentiate LH-stimulated testosterone production — an unexpected link between bone and gonadal endocrinology [10]. This multiplicity of inputs means that Leydig cell steroidogenesis, though nominally a single-axis output, is in practice a point of convergence for several distinct endocrine and paracrine signals.

10A. The Molecular Architecture of the Androgen and Estrogen Receptors

The genomic actions of testosterone, DHT, and estradiol described throughout this review are executed through nuclear receptors whose modular domain architecture is worth making explicit, since the same architecture recurs, with variations, across the entire nuclear receptor superfamily. The androgen receptor comprises an N-terminal transactivation domain (NTD), a central, zinc-finger-containing DNA-binding domain (DBD), a flexible hinge region, and a C-terminal ligand-binding domain (LBD) of roughly 260 residues that forms the hydrophobic pocket accommodating testosterone or DHT. The DBD's two zinc fingers position a "P-box" of residues that contact the major groove of DNA at androgen response elements, conferring sequence-specific binding, while the LBD, upon ligand binding, repositions its C-terminal helix (helix 12) to seal the ligand-binding pocket and create the activation function-2 (AF-2) surface that recruits transcriptional coactivators. The estrogen receptors ERα and ERβ share this same four-domain architecture; structural work has shown that the DBD and LBD of ERα are not independent modules but communicate allosterically across an inter-domain interface, such that DNA binding at the DBD influences the conformation and coactivator-binding surface of the distant LBD, providing a structural mechanism by which different estrogen response elements can bias the transcriptional output of a single bound ligand. This shared domain logic — NTD, zinc-finger DBD, hinge, ligand-binding LBD with a mobile activation-function helix — underlies both the genomic androgen and estrogen signaling described in Section 2.1 and its developmental deployment in the sexual differentiation of neural tissue described in Part V.

11. The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal (HPG) Axis

11.0 The Glycoprotein Gonadotropins: Shared Subunits, Distinct Functions

LH and FSH, together with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, Section 14) and placental human chorionic gonadotropin, belong to a single glycoprotein hormone family built on a shared structural logic: each is a heterodimer of a common α-subunit (encoded by a single gene and identical in amino acid sequence across all four hormones) non-covalently associated with a hormone-specific β-subunit that confers receptor-binding specificity and, therefore, biological activity. The LH β-subunit and FSH β-subunit are distinct gene products of differing length, and neither subunit alone is biologically active — functional receptor engagement requires the intact αβ heterodimer, extensively glycosylated at several asparagine residues in a manner that further modulates circulating half-life and bioactivity. This shared-subunit, unique-specificity architecture explains both the evolutionary relatedness of the gonadotropins and thyrotropin, and the biochemical basis for LH and FSH's divergent actions on, respectively, Leydig cell steroidogenesis and Sertoli cell-supported spermatogenesis despite their common pituitary gonadotrope origin and shared upstream GnRH stimulus.

11.1 GnRH and Gonadotropins

The HPG axis is the central regulatory circuit of male reproductive endocrinology. Hypothalamic neurons in the arcuate nucleus and preoptic area synthesize and release gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in a pulsatile manner into the hypophyseal portal circulation; GnRH acts on its cognate Gq-coupled receptor on anterior pituitary gonadotrope cells to stimulate synthesis and pulsatile release of the gonadotropins luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH, as described above, drives Leydig cell testosterone production; FSH acts on Sertoli cells within the seminiferous tubules to support spermatogenesis and to stimulate Sertoli cell production of inhibin B, which in turn feeds back to suppress pituitary FSH secretion, while testosterone (and its aromatized product estradiol) feeds back at both hypothalamic and pituitary levels to suppress GnRH and LH secretion, closing the classical negative-feedback loop [9].

11.2 Kisspeptin and the KNDy Neuron

The critical upstream regulator of GnRH pulsatility, identified over the past two decades, is kisspeptin, a neuropeptide encoded by the KISS1 gene and acting through its receptor KISS1R (GPR54) expressed on GnRH neurons [9]. Kisspeptin neurons of the arcuate nucleus co-express neurokinin B and dynorphin and are accordingly termed KNDy neurons; the coordinated autosynaptic and paracrine interactions among neurokinin B (excitatory, acting via NK3R), kisspeptin (excitatory, driving GnRH release), and dynorphin (inhibitory, acting via κ-opioid receptors) within this neuronal population are now understood to constitute the pulse-generating circuit responsible for the characteristic pulsatile pattern of GnRH — and consequently LH — secretion that is essential for normal male reproductive function, including Leydig cell steroidogenesis, spermatogenesis, and reproductive behavior [9]. Kisspeptin neurons themselves are targets of steroid-hormone feedback, of metabolic signals (leptin, insulin), and of circadian input, situating the KNDy circuit as a genuine integrative hub linking the organism's overall physiological state to reproductive hormone output. Mutations in KISS1 or KISS1R produce idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism or, conversely, central precocious puberty, underscoring the non-redundant importance of this signaling node in human reproductive endocrinology [9,27].

11.3 Inhibin-Activin Feedback

Alongside the classical steroid-hormone negative feedback loop described in Section 11.1, the testis exerts an additional, steroid-independent layer of feedback control through inhibin B, a Sertoli-cell-derived member of the TGF-β superfamily (structurally related to AMH, described in Section 19) that selectively suppresses pituitary FSH secretion, largely without affecting LH, by antagonizing the actions of the structurally related peptide activin at the gonadotrope. Because inhibin B production tracks Sertoli cell mass and functional status, circulating inhibin B is used clinically as a biochemical marker of spermatogenic and Sertoli cell function, complementing testosterone as a marker of Leydig cell function, and illustrating that the HPG axis carries at least two semi-independent feedback channels — one steroidal (testosterone/estradiol onto GnRH and LH), one peptide-hormonal (inhibin B onto FSH) — rather than a single unified loop.

11.4 GnRH Neuron Ontogeny and Kallmann Syndrome

A distinctive and clinically important feature of the HPG axis, easily overlooked in accounts that begin with the adult hypothalamus, is that GnRH neurons do not originate within the brain at all. During embryogenesis, GnRH neurons are specified within the nasal placode — the same ectodermal thickening that gives rise to the olfactory epithelium — and subsequently migrate as a discrete population along olfactory and vomeronasal axon scaffolds, escorted by olfactory ensheathing cells, from the nasal region across the nasal-forebrain junction and into the developing hypothalamus, where they take up their final, scattered distribution predominantly within the preoptic area and mediobasal hypothalamus [35]. This migratory route places GnRH neuron development under the control of the same axon guidance and cell-adhesion signaling systems (including semaphorin-neuropilin-plexin signaling) that pattern the broader olfactory system, and explains the existence of Kallmann syndrome, a genetic disorder combining congenital GnRH deficiency (and consequent hypogonadotropic hypogonadism) with anosmia or hyposmia, arising from mutations in genes required for normal olfactory axon outgrowth and GnRH neuron migration (including ANOS1/KAL1, FGFR1, and components of the semaphorin pathway) [35]. Kallmann syndrome is, in effect, a natural experiment demonstrating that the entire downstream cascade of male reproductive endocrinology described in this Part — Leydig cell steroidogenesis, testosterone secretion, and the sexual differentiation processes of Part V — is contingent on the successful completion, during fetal life, of a specific and independently characterized neurodevelopmental migration event, linking the neuroembryological themes of Part II directly and concretely to the endocrine physiology of Part III.

11.5 Sertoli Cell Biochemistry and the Support of Spermatogenesis

While Leydig cell steroidogenesis (Section 10) is the principal focus of this review's endocrine biochemistry, it is worth noting briefly that FSH and testosterone act jointly, through largely distinct but converging biochemical pathways, on the Sertoli cell to support spermatogenesis, since this dual regulation illustrates the same general principle — convergence of peptide-hormone and steroid-hormone signaling on a single target cell — introduced in Section 2. FSH acts through the FSH receptor (FSHR), a Gs-coupled receptor on the Sertoli cell plasma membrane, to elevate cAMP and drive Sertoli cell proliferation (particularly during the prepubertal period, when Sertoli cell number is established and directly determines adult testis size and sperm output) and to stimulate production of androgen-binding protein, transferrin, and inhibin B [38,39]. Testosterone, diffusing from adjacent Leydig cells and acting on androgen receptors expressed by the Sertoli cell itself (rather than by germ cells, which do not express AR), is independently required for the completion of spermatogenesis, particularly the meiotic and spermiogenic phases; FSH and testosterone signaling are understood to act redundantly, additively, and synergistically at different stages of germ cell development, with FSH predominating in the prepubertal establishment of Sertoli cell number and testosterone predominating in the postpubertal maintenance of qualitatively normal, quantitatively adequate spermatogenesis [38,39].

12. Puberty: Neuroendocrine Reawakening of the HPG Axis

The HPG axis is active in fetal and early neonatal life (a period sometimes called "mini-puberty"), before entering a state of relative quiescence through childhood, and reawakening at puberty through a coordinated increase in the frequency and amplitude of GnRH pulses. Classical work established that the prepubertal quiescence is maintained in part by tonic inhibitory glutamatergic signaling through NMDA receptors, with puberty onset associated with a sequential reduction in inhibitory NMDA receptor-mediated tone and unmasking of a facilitatory NMDA receptor-mediated drive to GnRH neurons [28]. More recent work has elaborated the role of the kisspeptin/neurokinin B/dynorphin (KNDy) circuit itself in driving the pubertal increase in GnRH pulse generator activity, and has additionally identified neurotrophic signaling — notably brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) acting through its receptor TrkB — as a modulator of KNDy neuron synaptic remodeling and excitatory/inhibitory balance during the peripubertal transition, contributing to the robust reactivation of pulsatile GnRH secretion that defines puberty onset [29]. Disorders of pubertal timing, in both directions (precocious and delayed puberty), frequently trace to perturbations of this same kisspeptin-dependent pulse-generating circuitry.

12.1 Adrenarche: A Biochemically Distinct Prelude

Distinct from the gonadarche described above — the HPG-axis-driven process addressed in Section 12 — human puberty is additionally preceded, typically around age six to eight, by adrenarche: a selective increase in adrenal secretion of the weak androgens dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and its sulfated form (DHEA-S), arising from the postnatal development of a distinct adrenal cortical zone, the zona reticularis, which acquires biochemical properties reminiscent of the fetal adrenal cortex [42]. The zona reticularis achieves its characteristic high DHEA output through a specific enzymatic configuration: abundant expression of cytochrome b5 and P450 oxidoreductase, which allosterically favor the 17,20-lyase activity of CYP17A1 over its 17α-hydroxylase activity, combined with low expression of 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSD3B2), the enzyme that would otherwise divert steroidogenic intermediates toward cortisol and away from DHEA [42]. This enzymatic bias — the mirror image of the CYP21A2-driven shunt toward androgen synthesis seen in congenital adrenal hyperplasia (Section 21.3) — allows the zona reticularis to generate substantial adrenal androgen output without a corresponding rise in cortisol, and the phenotypic consequence, pubarche (axillary and pubic hair development), typically precedes the visible physical changes of gonadarche by one to two years, even though the two processes are regulated by substantially independent hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal and hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal mechanisms that happen to mature in temporal proximity during normal human development [42].

12.2 Tanner Staging: Anchoring Biochemistry to Observable Development

The hormonal events described in Sections 11–12.1 are conventionally correlated, in clinical and developmental-endocrinology practice, with the Tanner staging system (sexual maturity rating), an observational, five-stage classification of external genital, testicular, and pubic hair development originally established through longitudinal anthropometric study [45]. Tanner Stage 1 denotes the prepubertal state; the transition to Stage 2 — marking the clinical onset of puberty — corresponds biochemically to the reawakening of pulsatile GnRH secretion described in Section 12 and the resulting initial rise in testicular volume driven by FSH-dependent Sertoli cell proliferation and LH-dependent Leydig cell steroidogenesis; Stages 3 and 4 track the progressive amplification of gonadotropin pulsatility and testosterone output: and Stage 5 denotes attainment of adult genital and pubic hair morphology, coincident with adult-range testosterone concentrations and completed spermatogenic capacity [45]. Because the hormonal changes of puberty, as with adrenarche, measurably precede their physical (Tanner-stage) correlates by a period of months to years, Tanner staging functions clinically as a delayed, observable proxy for the underlying biochemical cascade detailed throughout this Part, rather than as a direct real-time readout of HPG axis activity.

13. The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) Axis

Distinct from, but extensively cross-talking with, the HPG axis is the HPA axis, the principal neuroendocrine mediator of the stress response. Hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus neurons synthesize corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which is released into the hypophyseal portal circulation and stimulates anterior pituitary corticotrope cells, via the CRH receptor type 1, to synthesize proopiomelanocortin (POMC) and cleave it to adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH acts on the melanocortin 2 receptor of adrenal cortex zona fasciculata cells to stimulate cortisol biosynthesis via a steroidogenic cascade analogous in overall logic (StAR-mediated cholesterol transport, CYP11A1-mediated side-chain cleavage) to that described for testicular Leydig cells, though diverging at the level of CYP17A1/CYP21A2/CYP11B1 to yield cortisol rather than testosterone. Cortisol feeds back at hypothalamic and pituitary levels to suppress CRH and ACTH secretion. The HPA axis is of direct relevance to the subject of this review both because it shares upstream hypothalamic architecture and regulatory logic with the HPG axis, permitting extensive functional cross-talk (elaborated in Part VIII), and because glucocorticoid exposure during fetal life is itself a major, biochemically well-characterized influence on brain development, discussed in Part VII [19,20,21].

14. The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid (HPT) Axis

The HPT axis follows the same three-tier organizational logic: hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates pituitary thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) secretion, and TSH stimulates thyroid follicular cell synthesis of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), with T3/T4 exerting negative feedback at hypothalamic and pituitary levels. Thyroid hormone is essential for normal fetal and early postnatal neurodevelopment, and the HPT axis interacts with both the GH axis (growth hormone replacement can unmask latent hypothyroidism in patients with combined pituitary hormone deficiency) and, at the level of the hypothalamus, with broader integrative circuits that also process reproductive and stress-related signals.

15. The Growth Hormone–IGF-1 Axis

Growth hormone (GH) secretion from the anterior pituitary somatotrope is governed by the antagonistic actions of hypothalamic growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH, stimulatory) and somatostatin (SST, inhibitory), producing a pulsatile secretory pattern. GH acts both directly, via the GH receptor and JAK2-STAT5 signaling, and indirectly, by stimulating hepatic (and local tissue) production of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which mediates most of the growth-promoting actions of GH and provides feedback inhibition at both hypothalamic and pituitary levels [11]. Beyond somatic growth, IGF-1 has recognized roles within the central nervous system in neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and neuroprotection, and the GH-IGF-1 axis interacts with gonadal steroid signaling during puberty, when a coordinated rise in GH/IGF-1 and gonadal steroid secretion together drive the pubertal growth spurt [11].

16. Neurosteroids: Local Steroidogenesis Within the Brain

A biochemically distinct and increasingly important dimension of the endocrine biology relevant to "the Man" is the capacity of the brain itself to synthesize steroids de novo from cholesterol, independent of peripheral gonadal or adrenal input — so-called neurosteroids. This local steroidogenic capacity, conserved across vertebrate species, was first localized to glial cells (oligodendrocytes and astrocytes) but has since been demonstrated also in specific neuronal populations, including glutamatergic neurons of cortex, hippocampus, and basolateral amygdala, and in long-projecting GABAergic interneurons [16]. The best-studied neurosteroid, allopregnanolone, is synthesized from progesterone by the sequential action of 5α-reductase (producing 5α-dihydroprogesterone) and 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3α-HSD), the same 5α-reductase enzyme family responsible for peripheral conversion of testosterone to DHT, here repurposed for a distinct local, brain-specific biochemical role [16]. Allopregnanolone and related neurosteroids act as potent positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors, distinct in binding site from benzodiazepines, and thereby enhance both phasic (synaptic) and tonic (extrasynaptic) inhibitory neurotransmission; they possess marked anticonvulsant, anxiolytic, and sedative-hypnotic properties, and clinical evidence links reduced neurosteroid levels to a range of stress-related and affective disorders, including depression, PTSD, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder [16]. For the purposes of this review, the existence of local, cell-autonomous brain steroidogenesis is important for two reasons: first, it demonstrates that the biochemical machinery of steroidogenesis described in Section 10 for the testis is not confined to classical endocrine glands but is redeployed, using several of the same enzymes, within neural tissue itself; and second, it provides a mechanistic bridge to the sexual differentiation processes discussed in Part V, in which local, intracellular conversion of circulating testosterone to estradiol by neuronal aromatase is the central biochemical event.

17. Summary of Part III

The male endocrine system, considered as a whole, is not a single hormone or a single gland but an integrated set of hypothalamic-pituitary-target organ loops (HPG, HPA, HPT, GH-IGF-1) sharing common upstream hypothalamic architecture and a common downstream enzymatic logic — cholesterol-derived steroidogenesis, mediated by cytochrome P450 and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzyme families that recur, with tissue-specific variations, in gonad, adrenal cortex, and brain alike. The HPG axis, and in particular its kisspeptin-dependent pulse-generating circuitry, is of special importance for this review because it is both the source of the testosterone whose downstream metabolites (DHT, estradiol) drive the sexual differentiation processes discussed in Parts IV and V, and itself a target of the very brain structures (hypothalamic nuclei, built via the neuroembryological processes of Part II) that those hormones go on to shape. ## PART IV — SEX DETERMINATION AND DIFFERENTIATION OF THE REPRODUCTIVE TRACT

Before hormones can act on the brain, they must first be produced — and their production depends on the prior, genetically triggered differentiation of the bipotential gonad into a testis. This Part traces that process and its downstream consequences for internal and external genital differentiation, and reviews the clinical syndromes that result when specific steps fail — syndromes that are themselves of central importance to the study of hormone-brain relationships, since they constitute natural experiments in the dissociation of genetic sex, gonadal sex, hormonal exposure, and phenotypic (including neurobehavioral) outcome.

18. Genetic Sex Determination: SRY and the Bipotential Gonad

Genetic sex is fixed at fertilization, but the gonad itself begins development in a bipotential state, capable of forming either a testis or an ovary. The gene responsible was identified in 1990, when Sinclair and colleagues isolated, from a critical 35-kilobase region of the human Y chromosome, a testis-specific transcript encoding a protein with a DNA-binding motif homologous to the high-mobility-group (HMG) box family, which they named SRY (sex-determining region Y) and proposed — correctly, as subsequent functional studies confirmed — as the long-sought testis-determining factor [34]. At approximately six weeks of human gestation, this sex-determining region Y (SRY) gene, located on the Y chromosome, is transiently expressed in the somatic cells of the bipotential gonad and triggers the male developmental pathway by upregulating SOX9, a transcription factor that in turn drives differentiation of Sertoli cells, the somatic cells responsible for organizing the seminiferous cords and supporting germ cell development. Sertoli cell differentiation is the pivotal event of testis determination: without it, the gonad defaults to ovarian development, in keeping with the classical Jost model in which the female phenotype represents the constitutive, unless-instructed-otherwise developmental pathway [18].

19. Hormonal Control of Internal Genital Differentiation

Jost's mid-twentieth-century castration experiments in the fetal rabbit established the fundamental hormonal logic of internal genital differentiation: fetuses castrated in utero before sexual differentiation uniformly developed female-pattern internal ducts regardless of genetic sex, and male-pattern development could be selectively restored in castrated fetuses by local implantation of testosterone, demonstrating that testicular hormones — not the presence of the Y chromosome per se — are the proximate drivers of male internal genital differentiation [18]. Modern biochemistry has resolved this into two discrete, testis-derived hormonal signals. Leydig cell-secreted testosterone, acting locally (in a paracrine, largely non-diffusible fashion) on the adjacent mesonephric (Wolffian) duct, drives its differentiation into the epididymis, vas deferens, and seminal vesicle. Independently, Sertoli cells secrete anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH, also called Müllerian-inhibiting substance), a member of the TGF-β superfamily acting through a dedicated type II serine/threonine kinase receptor, which provokes regression of the paramesonephric (Müllerian) duct — the primordium that would otherwise form the fallopian tubes, uterus, and upper vagina. Because both testosterone's virilizing action and AMH's regressive action are locally restricted, each testis governs duct differentiation only on its own ipsilateral side, a feature that remains clinically informative in cases of asymmetric gonadal disorders. Mutations in the AMH gene or its receptor produce persistent Müllerian duct syndrome, in which XY individuals with otherwise normal testicular and virilized external phenotype retain Müllerian derivatives (uterus, fallopian tubes) due to failure of this specific regressive signal, illustrating the independence of the testosterone-dependent (Wolffian) and AMH-dependent (Müllerian) arms of internal genital differentiation.

19A. INSL3 and Testicular Descent

A second, biochemically distinct Leydig cell secretory product, beyond testosterone, is required for normal male reproductive anatomy: insulin-like factor 3 (INSL3), a member of the relaxin-insulin peptide hormone family, is produced by fetal and postnatal Leydig cells in a developmental pattern that broadly parallels testosterone secretion, with a second-trimester peak, an early postnatal surge coincident with "mini-puberty," and rising levels again at puberty [41]. INSL3, acting through its receptor RXFP2 (also called LGR8/GREAT) on the gubernaculum, drives the transabdominal phase of testicular descent, and loss-of-function mutations in either INSL3 or RXFP2 produce cryptorchidism (undescended testis) in the mouse and are implicated in a subset of human cryptorchidism cases [41]. Because INSL3 production is itself sensitive, in animal models, to disruption by estrogenic or anti-androgenic environmental compounds, INSL3 biology provides a second, hormonally distinct (from the testosterone/DHT axis of Sections 19–20) mechanistic route by which endocrine-disrupting chemical exposure (introduced in Section 34) could plausibly affect male reproductive tract development [41].

20. External Genital Differentiation: The Role of Dihydrotestosterone

While Wolffian duct differentiation is testosterone-dependent, differentiation of the external genitalia and prostate depends specifically on dihydrotestosterone (DHT), generated locally within genital tissue by 5α-reductase-mediated reduction of testosterone. DHT binds the androgen receptor with higher affinity and a slower dissociation rate than testosterone, producing more potent and stable receptor activation appropriate to the sustained transcriptional program required for masculinization of the genital tubercle, urogenital sinus, and urogenital folds into the penis, scrotum, and prostate. This biochemical distinction between testosterone-dependent (Wolffian) and DHT-dependent (external genital) virilization is not merely academic: it is the precise basis of the clinical syndrome of 5α-reductase deficiency described below, in which internal male duct structures form normally (being testosterone-dependent) while external genitalia remain undervirilized (being DHT-dependent).

21. Disorders of Sex Development: Biochemistry and Clinical-Neurobehavioral Correlates

21.1 Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (CAIS)

Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome results from loss-of-function mutations in the androgen receptor gene itself, rendering target tissues wholly unresponsive to both testosterone and DHT despite normal or elevated circulating androgen levels in an XY individual with intact testes. Because AMH secretion (Sertoli-cell-dependent, not androgen-receptor-dependent) proceeds normally, Müllerian derivatives regress, but in the absence of any androgen receptor signaling, Wolffian duct virilization and external genital masculinization both fail, and affected individuals develop a female external phenotype and are, in the overwhelming majority of cases, raised and identify as female.

21.2 5α-Reductase Type 2 Deficiency

A biochemically distinct and clinically illuminating condition is 5α-reductase type 2 deficiency, an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in SRD5A2 that impair peripheral conversion of testosterone to DHT. Because Wolffian duct differentiation depends on testosterone directly (not DHT), affected 46,XY individuals develop normal internal male duct structures (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles), but because external genital virilization requires DHT specifically, they are born with a spectrum of undervirilized external genitalia ranging from isolated micropenis to severe hypospadias to a female-appearing phenotype, and are often assigned female at birth or in early infancy, sometimes being confused clinically with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome; genetic and biochemical testing (testosterone:DHT ratios, though these can be unreliable, and direct genetic analysis) is required to distinguish the two conditions definitively. The condition was clinically and biochemically characterized in detail in a large, extensively studied Dominican kindred, in which affected individuals — described locally by the Spanish-language term "guevedoces" — were typically raised as girls in early childhood but underwent pronounced virilization at puberty (driven by the pubertal testosterone surge acting through residual, DHT-independent androgen receptor signaling and by increasing conversion via the residual SRD5A1 isozyme), with the majority subsequently adopting a male gender identity and role [17]. This natural history has been of substantial importance to debates about the relative contributions of prenatal androgen exposure versus postnatal social assignment to the development of gender identity, since it demonstrates that a robust prenatal androgen milieu (present in 5α-reductase deficiency, notwithstanding the peripheral DHT deficit) is compatible with later-life male gender identification despite a female-typical sex-of-rearing in childhood — a pattern that stands in informative contrast to complete androgen insensitivity syndrome, in which the prenatal androgen milieu is rendered biologically ineffective and female gender identity is the near-universal outcome.

21.3 Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia, most commonly due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency (mutations in CYP21A2), disrupts the normal adrenal steroidogenic pathway: impaired conversion of 17α-hydroxyprogesterone to 11-deoxycortisol reduces cortisol output, releasing the HPA axis from negative feedback and driving compensatory ACTH hypersecretion, which in turn drives adrenal hyperplasia and shunts accumulating steroidogenic precursors into the androgen synthesis pathway, producing substantial adrenal androgen excess. In 46,XX fetuses, this prenatal androgen excess can virilize the external genitalia to varying degrees, and has been studied intensively as a human model system for the effects of atypical prenatal androgen exposure on subsequent brain and behavioral development. Observational studies have reported associations between CAH and altered rates of specific neuropsychiatric conditions, and a recent Mendelian randomization analysis reported that adrenal-driven pathways associated with 21-hydroxylase deficiency may be causally associated with altered risk of specific neuropsychiatric outcomes, including a possible increase in autism spectrum disorder risk, while structural neuroimaging studies of total and regional brain volume in CAH have generally found less consistent differences from control populations than earlier behavioral studies of gender-typed play and spatial cognition might predict [25,26]. CAH thus serves, alongside CAIS and 5α-reductase deficiency, as one of the three classical "experiments of nature" through which the endocrinology community has sought to disentangle the separate contributions of genetic sex, gonadal hormone exposure, and androgen receptor sensitivity to human psychosexual and neurobehavioral development — a theme developed further in Part V and Part VII.

21.4 Klinefelter Syndrome: A Sex Chromosome Aneuploidy

Distinct in mechanism from the single-gene and single-enzyme disorders discussed above, Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) is a sex chromosome aneuploidy — the presence of an additional X chromosome in an otherwise male (SRY-positive) individual — and is the most common such aneuploidy, affecting approximately 1 in 600 male births [40]. Rather than blocking a specific step of the androgen biosynthetic or receptor-signaling cascade, the additional X chromosome disrupts testicular architecture more globally, producing small, fibrotic testes (microorchidism) with impaired Leydig cell function; the resulting biochemical profile is one of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism — low or low-normal circulating testosterone accompanied by elevated LH and FSH, reflecting loss of negative feedback restraint at the hypothalamic-pituitary level rather than any defect in the feedback-sensing machinery itself [40]. Clinically, Klinefelter syndrome is associated with infertility, tall stature, variable gynecomastia, and a range of cognitive, metabolic, and psychosocial features, and clinical trial and observational data suggest that circulating testosterone concentration correlates with specific neurobehavioral outcomes (including social anxiety) within the Klinefelter population, and that early-life testosterone replacement, timed to coincide with the physiological neonatal "mini-puberty" described in Section 12, may favorably influence subsequent neurodevelopmental trajectories — a translational application directly motivated by the organizational-hormone-exposure logic developed at length in Part V [40].

22. Summary of Part IV

The differentiation of the male reproductive phenotype proceeds through a specific, biochemically dissectible sequence — SRY-driven Sertoli cell differentiation, testosterone-driven Wolffian virilization, AMH-driven Müllerian regression, and DHT-driven external genital masculinization — and each step is vulnerable to distinct genetic and enzymatic failures whose clinical phenotypes (CAIS, 5α-reductase deficiency, CAH) have, in turn, become indispensable natural experiments for the neuroendocrinology of sexual differentiation addressed in the following Part.

PART V — HORMONES AND THE DEVELOPING BRAIN: SEXUAL DIFFERENTIATION OF NEURAL TISSUE

This Part is the fulcrum of the review: the point at which the neuroembryology of Part II and the endocrine biochemistry of Part III converge to explain how the mammalian, and specifically the male, brain becomes sexually differentiated.

23. The Organizational/Activational Hypothesis: History and Modern Status

In 1959, Phoenix, Goy, Gerall, and Young reported that prenatal administration of testosterone propionate to female guinea pigs produced permanent changes in the neural tissue mediating adult mating behavior, such that treated females, when subsequently given adult hormone treatment, displayed masculinized patterns of sexual behavior not seen in untreated females given identical adult hormones [1]. From this single, carefully designed experiment emerged the organizational/activational hypothesis, arguably the single most influential conceptual framework in behavioral neuroendocrinology: that steroid hormones act during an early, typically prenatal or perinatal, sensitive period to permanently "organize" neural circuitry into a male-typical or female-typical configuration, and that this organized substrate is subsequently "activated" — its behavioral and physiological potential unmasked — by the resurgence of gonadal steroids at puberty and in adulthood [1,2]. Over the following half-century, the hypothesis has been extensively amended without being supplanted: it is now understood that organizational effects are not strictly confined to a single prenatal critical period but can occur, for some traits and circuits, at multiple points across the lifespan; that "activation" itself can, in some circuits, produce lasting structural changes indistinguishable in kind from classical organizational effects; and that the hypothesis extends well beyond behavior to encompass the sexual differentiation of peripheral tissues generally, suggesting a unified framework for steroid-mediated sexual differentiation across the mammalian body [2]. Nonetheless, the core logic — an early, hormonally instructed, durable change in tissue organization, later revealed by activational hormone exposure — remains the organizing conceptual scaffold for the molecular mechanisms described in the remainder of this Part.

24. The Aromatization Hypothesis

24.1 The Central Paradox and Its Resolution

A puzzle confronted early researchers in this field: in rodents, estradiol — nominally the "female" hormone — was found to be as or more effective than testosterone itself at masculinizing neural tissue and behavior when administered to neonatal females, and the key sexually dimorphic brain regions were found to express high levels of estrogen receptors and of the enzyme aromatase. The resolution, now termed the aromatization hypothesis, holds that circulating testicular testosterone in the male fetus and neonate is taken up by target neurons and locally converted to estradiol by neuronal aromatase (CYP19A1), and it is this locally generated estradiol — not testosterone itself — that is the proximate masculinizing and defeminizing signal acting through neuronal estrogen receptors [2,3]. This local, intracellular conversion elegantly explains why exogenous estradiol can masculinize female neural tissue in experimental paradigms (it directly supplies the active ligand, bypassing the need for aromatization) and why testosterone, rather than estradiol itself, circulates at high levels systemically in the male (limiting peripheral estrogenic exposure while permitting brain-specific local activation) [2,3].

24.2 Protecting the Female Brain from Maternal Estrogens

The aromatization hypothesis raises an immediate corollary problem: if estradiol is so potently masculinizing, how is the female fetal brain protected from the substantial estradiol present in maternal and fetal circulation? In rodents, this problem is solved biochemically by alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), a serum protein synthesized by the fetal liver that binds circulating estradiol with high affinity, sequestering it from access to brain tissue, while testosterone — a comparatively poor AFP ligand — passes AFP-bound sequestration largely unimpeded and reaches the brain to be locally aromatized in target neurons [2]. This mechanism accounts for why male fetuses, despite gestating in the same maternal estrogenic environment as their female littermates, undergo brain masculinization via locally generated estradiol, while female littermates do not, notwithstanding equal systemic exposure to maternal estrogens. The precise degree to which this AFP-mediated protective mechanism applies in humans (in which placental aromatase activity and other differences in steroid handling are substantial) remains an area of ongoing comparative investigation, but the general principle — that access of circulating estrogen to the developing brain is tightly regulated, and that local, cell-autonomous steroid metabolism (aromatization, and, as discussed in Section 16, neurosteroidogenesis) rather than simple systemic hormone concentration is the proximate determinant of neural sexual differentiation — is a unifying theme of modern behavioral neuroendocrinology [2,3,4].

25. Sexually Dimorphic Nuclei: Anatomy and Hormonal Control

25.1 The Sexually Dimorphic Nucleus of the Preoptic Area (SDN-POA)

The single most extensively studied anatomical substrate of hormone-dependent brain sexual differentiation is the sexually dimorphic nucleus of the preoptic area (SDN-POA), first described in the rat and subsequently demonstrated, in modified form, across a wide range of mammalian species including hamster, gerbil, California mouse, ferret, guinea pig, and human [6,7]. In humans, Swaab and Fliers demonstrated, using post-mortem morphometric analysis, that the volume of this preoptic cell group is approximately 2.5-fold larger in men than in women, and contains proportionally more neurons, with the sex difference emerging developmentally and, notably, only becoming detectable relatively late (the nucleus does not show a clear sex difference until around 2–4 years of age) and subsequently regressing substantially in volume with advanced age in both sexes [6]. A subsequent, larger comparative morphometric study confirmed a 2.1- to 2.2-fold male-biased difference in both nuclear volume and total cell number, while finding no sex difference in cell density or mean nuclear diameter, indicating that the dimorphism reflects a difference in total cell number rather than cell packing or cell size [7].

25.2 The Bed Nucleus of the Stria Terminalis (BNST)

A second, extensively studied sexually dimorphic structure is the principal nucleus of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNSTpr), part of the extended amygdala, which — like the SDN-POA — is larger in volume and neuron number in males across multiple species, an effect attributable to perinatal androgen (via local aromatization to estradiol) action, and which is functionally implicated in the regulation of stress responsivity, anxiety-related behavior, and reproductive behavior [8]. Both the SDN-POA and BNSTpr are markers of a broader class of male-biased sexually dimorphic cell groups within the preoptic area and extended amygdala, several of which (including subregions positive for the calcium-binding protein calbindin D-28K) share the common feature of estradiol-dependent masculinization during a perinatal sensitive period [8].

25.2A The Interstitial Nuclei of the Anterior Hypothalamus (INAH)

A related, though anatomically and historically distinct, set of sexually dimorphic cell groups is the interstitial nuclei of the human anterior hypothalamus (INAH 1–4), of which the third (INAH-3) has received the most sustained empirical attention: post-mortem morphometric study established that INAH-3 occupies a volume more than twice as large in men as in women, a robust and independently replicated sexual dimorphism [43]. INAH-3 additionally became the focus of a much-debated 1991 report that its volume was smaller, on average, in a post-mortem sample of homosexual men than in heterosexual men, at a magnitude comparable to the male-female difference [43]. Subsequent, more methodologically stringent replication attempts — controlling more rigorously for HIV status (a major confound in the original, AIDS-era autopsy sample) and for cause of death — have found, at most, a non-significant trend toward reduced INAH-3 volume in homosexual men, with no corresponding difference in absolute neuron number, and no study has independently confirmed the original volumetric finding free of confounding illness effects [43,44]. INAH-3 is accordingly best understood, on current evidence, as a robustly male-larger sexually dimorphic nucleus of uncertain and unresolved relationship to sexual orientation — a caveat worth stating explicitly given how frequently the original finding is invoked in public discussion beyond what the replication literature actually supports.

25.3 Cellular Mechanisms of Nucleus Formation

Recent work by Tsukahara and Morishita has detailed the specific cellular mechanisms by which perinatal neuroestrogens (that is, estradiol locally aromatized from testicular androgen) sculpt the SDN-POA and BNST: neuroestrogens act on glial cells and on the local immune microenvironment to modulate dendritic spine and synapse patterning in a sex-specific manner, and additionally regulate the survival versus programmed cell death of specific neuronal populations within these nuclei, such that the net male-biased volume difference reflects a combination of estradiol-promoted cell survival (reduced apoptosis) in males and estradiol-independent, comparatively greater developmental cell loss in females [8]. This apoptosis-based mechanism — hormone-dependent rescue of neurons that would otherwise undergo programmed cell death — is now understood to be a general cellular mechanism of sexually dimorphic nucleus formation, operating alongside sex differences in cell genesis, migration, and dendritic elaboration documented in other sexually dimorphic circuits, including denser dendritic branching in specific hypothalamic neuron populations in males, itself attributable to elevated perinatal aromatized estradiol [2,4].

26. Genomic and Non-Genomic Receptor Signaling in Developing Neurons

The cellular effects described above — altered apoptosis, dendritic remodeling, synaptic patterning — are mediated through the same genomic and non-genomic estrogen and androgen receptor signaling mechanisms introduced in Section 2, now considered specifically in the context of the developing neuron. Estrogen receptors (ERα, ERβ) are expressed not only in the nucleus but throughout the neuronal cytoplasm, at the plasma membrane, and in association with the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, and membrane-initiated, non-genomic estrogen signaling — including rapid stimulation of intracellular calcium oscillations and activation of kinase cascades — cooperates extensively with slower genomic, transcription-dependent signaling in shaping synaptic plasticity and, during development, in translating a transient hormone pulse into durable structural change [3]. Androgen receptor signaling likewise possesses a non-genomic component, with cytoplasmic AR capable of initiating kinase signaling that influences cellular migration and process outgrowth independent of its role as a nuclear transcription factor [3]. The coexistence of rapid non-genomic and durable genomic signaling modes is of particular developmental importance: it provides a plausible mechanistic bridge between the brief, pulsatile nature of the perinatal testosterone surge and the permanent, "organized" changes in neural architecture that persist for a lifetime — a bridge that is completed, mechanistically, by the epigenetic remodeling discussed next.

27. Epigenetic Mechanisms: Making Organization Permanent

27.1 The Central Problem

The organizational/activational hypothesis requires that a transient hormonal signal — the perinatal testosterone/estradiol surge, which in humans and rodents alike occupies a comparatively narrow developmental window — produce changes in neural tissue that persist for the remainder of the organism's life, including through periods of low circulating gonadal steroids (childhood) and are only "activated," not re-instituted, by the pubertal steroid surge. This durability problem is now understood to be substantially solved by epigenetic mechanisms: heritable-at-the-cellular-level, but not DNA-sequence-based, modifications of chromatin, principally DNA methylation and histone post-translational modification, that can be established by a transient signal and then stably maintained by cellular machinery independent of the original signal's continued presence [5].

27.2 DNA Methylation and Demethylation

Genome-wide studies of the hypothalamus have shown that DNA methyltransferase and, notably, ten-eleven translocation (Tet) enzyme ("epigenetic eraser," DNA demethylation) expression is itself sexually dimorphic and developmentally regulated, peaking in the first postnatal week and showing greater expression in male than female neonates [5]. Critically, many of the transcriptional and methylomic consequences of neonatal testosterone exposure do not become fully apparent until adulthood, indicating that the perinatal hormone signal establishes a "primed" epigenetic state whose functional (transcriptional and behavioral) consequences are only revealed later, plausibly at puberty when activational hormone levels rise — a molecular instantiation of the organizational/activational distinction itself [5]. Comparative genome-wide analyses further reveal that sex differences in DNA methylation at a given locus do not always correspond to sex differences in the expression of the associated gene, and conversely that genes without steady-state sex differences in expression can nonetheless carry sex-differentiated epigenetic marks, indicating that the relationship between epigenetic sex differences and transcriptional sex differences is not a simple one-to-one mapping but reflects a more complex, context-dependent regulatory layer [5].

27.3 Histone Modification and MeCP2: A Second Chromatin Layer

DNA methylation does not act alone. A functionally important second layer of chromatin regulation involves the methyl-CpG-binding protein MeCP2, which binds methylated DNA and recruits corepressor complexes (including nuclear receptor corepressor, nCoR) that alter local histone acetylation and broader chromatin architecture. Sex differences in MeCP2 expression are themselves region- and stage-specific — female rats express higher MeCP2 (and higher DNMT3a and nCoR) than males in the neonatal amygdala, while in some other neuronal contexts the direction of the sex difference reverses — and experimentally reducing amygdala MeCP2 expression during the neonatal sensitive period disrupts the normal masculinization of juvenile social play behavior specifically in males, directly demonstrating that MeCP2-dependent chromatin remodeling is a functionally necessary component of the organizational mechanism, not merely a correlated marker of it [37]. More broadly, MeCP2 deficiency produces global changes in neuronal chromatin structure, including elevated histone acetylation, illustrating that DNA methylation and histone modification are mechanistically coupled rather than independent epigenetic channels, and that perinatal gonadal hormone exposure — by altering DNMT activity and MeCP2 expression in a region-specific manner — programs both layers simultaneously [37]. Clinically, the same MECP2 gene, located on the X chromosome, underlies two contrasting sex-differential neurodevelopmental disorders: loss-of-function MECP2 mutations cause Rett syndrome, which manifests almost exclusively in females, while MECP2 duplication syndrome, caused by gain of an extra functional copy, occurs almost exclusively in males — a pairing that itself illustrates how X-linked epigenetic regulators can produce starkly sex-differential clinical phenotypes depending on the direction (loss versus gain) of the underlying genetic lesion.

27.4 Sex Chromosome Contributions

Epigenetic and hormonal mechanisms of brain sexual differentiation do not act in isolation from genetic sex itself: cell-autonomous actions of sex chromosome genes (that is, genes on the X and Y chromosomes acting directly within individual cells, independent of gonadal hormone secretion) also contribute to phenotypic sex differences in the brain, indicating that the mammalian brain's sexual differentiation reflects the joint operation of gonadal-hormone-dependent and sex-chromosome-dependent, cell-autonomous mechanisms operating in parallel and, in some circuits, interactively [5].

28. Integration: From Neural Tube to Sexually Differentiated Brain

Considered together, Sections 23–27 describe a coherent multilevel mechanism linking the endocrine biochemistry of Part III to the neuroembryological substrate of Part II: (i) the fetal testis, differentiated via the SRY-SOX9 pathway of Part IV, secretes testosterone under LH stimulation; (ii) testosterone reaches specific neurons within nuclei — such as the preoptic area and BNST — whose regional identity was established during the dorsoventral and rostrocaudal patterning of the neural tube described in Part II; (iii) within those neurons, local aromatase converts testosterone to estradiol, which acts through genomic and non-genomic estrogen receptor signaling to alter rates of apoptosis, dendritic branching, and synaptogenesis; and (iv) these hormonally instructed cellular changes are stabilized by DNA methylation/demethylation and other chromatin modifications that persist long after the initiating hormonal pulse has cleared, to be functionally revealed at puberty when the HPG axis, reawakened by the mechanisms described in Section 12, drives a second, activational surge of the same steroid hormones acting now upon a tissue already structurally and epigenetically prepared to respond in a male-typical manner. This integrated account is the biochemical core of what it means, mechanistically, to speak of "the Man" as a hormonally and neuroembryologically constructed phenotype. ## PART VI — ADULT MALE NEUROENDOCRINOLOGY: ACTIVATIONAL BIOCHEMISTRY

Having traced the organizational events of prenatal and perinatal life in Part V, this Part turns to the activational side of the classical dichotomy: the biochemistry by which circulating testosterone acts on the adult male brain, already organized by the mechanisms described above, to modulate cognition and social behavior.

29. Testosterone, Cognition, and Spatial Ability

In androgen-deficient men, testosterone replacement produces measurable improvements in visuospatial cognitive performance, a domain in which men on average outperform women and in which performance correlates with circulating androgen status — an activational effect operating upon neural circuitry (including hippocampal and parietal networks) whose androgen and estrogen receptor expression patterns were themselves established, in part, during the organizational period described in Part V [2]. The relationship between testosterone and social cognition is considerably more nuanced than the relationship with spatial cognition: experimental testosterone administration studies in healthy men have found that exogenous testosterone does not consistently alter brain activity in the networks supporting cognitive or affective empathy, indicating that "activational" effects are circuit- and domain-specific rather than uniformly enhancing or suppressing all forms of social-cognitive processing.

30. Testosterone, Aggression, and the Amygdala-Prefrontal Circuit

30.1 Behavioral and Endocrine Correlates

A large observational and experimental literature links testosterone to aggressive and dominance-related behavior in men, though the relationship is probabilistic and context-dependent rather than deterministic: aggressive behavior attributable to testosterone in humans is substantially attenuated and channeled by social and familial inhibition relative to its expression in non-human animals, manifesting across a spectrum from aggressive cognition and verbal aggression to, in its most extreme forms, physical violence [24]. Testosterone concentrations are elevated in some studies of incarcerated individuals convicted of violent offenses, and field and laboratory studies consistently find that testosterone rises during competitive and dominance-related social interactions, particularly among the victors of such interactions, suggesting a bidirectional relationship in which testosterone both influences and is influenced by social competition [24].

30.2 Neural Mechanisms

Neuroimaging studies indicate that testosterone increases activity within the amygdala, a subcortical structure central to threat processing and the generation of aggressive and defensive responses, while simultaneously reducing the functional coupling between the amygdala and prefrontal cortical regions that ordinarily exert top-down inhibitory control over amygdalar reactivity [24]. This pattern — heightened subcortical threat/emotion processing coupled with reduced cortical restraint — provides a plausible neural circuit-level account of testosterone's behavioral association with aggression and dominance-seeking, complementing the amygdala's well-established role, described in Part V, as one of the principal targets of perinatal organizational hormone action; the adult amygdala's response to activational testosterone is thus itself likely conditioned by the organizational hormone exposure that shaped its androgen and estrogen receptor expression and its structural connectivity to prefrontal cortex during development [2,4,24].

31. Reproductive Aging in the Male: Biochemical Decline

Unlike the comparatively abrupt cessation of ovarian steroidogenesis at female menopause, the male HPG axis undergoes a gradual, partial decline in Leydig cell steroidogenic capacity with advancing age, sometimes termed late-onset hypogonadism or "andropause," characterized biochemically by a slow decline in total and free testosterone, a compensatory (though incomplete) rise in LH reflecting reduced testicular sensitivity and negative feedback, and clinical correlates including reduced bone density, altered body composition, and, in some individuals, changes in mood and cognitive function. The gradual nature of this decline, in contrast to the organizational sharpness of the perinatal hormone surge, illustrates that the same steroidogenic biochemistry described in Part III operates across a lifetime with markedly different temporal dynamics at different developmental stages.

PART VII — CLINICAL AND TRANSLATIONAL CORRELATES

32. Sex Bias in Neurodevelopmental Disorders

32.1 Epidemiological Pattern

Males are disproportionately affected by several major neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and, with a less pronounced but still notable bias, schizophrenia, while females show comparatively greater vulnerability to internalizing psychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depressive disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder [15,23]. This asymmetric pattern of sex-biased psychiatric vulnerability has motivated intensive investigation into whether the same hormonal and neurodevelopmental mechanisms responsible for normative male brain sexual differentiation, described in Part V, also confer differential risk for neurodevelopmental disorder.

32.2 The Extreme Male Brain Theory and Prenatal Androgen Exposure

The most prominent hypothesis linking prenatal hormone exposure to ASD risk is the "extreme male brain" theory, which proposes that unusually elevated prenatal testosterone exposure shifts brain development along the same organizational axis described in Part V towards an exaggeratedly male-typical pattern, and that this exaggeration itself confers elevated ASD risk or symptom severity [22]. Reviews of the prenatal hormone literature note that this testosterone-centered hypothesis coexists with several complementary or competing frameworks — the female protective effect (whereby female-typical developmental programs buffer against genetic and environmental risk factors for ASD), maternal immune activation, and prenatal stress — and that the specific mechanistic contribution of prenatal androgen exposure, likely acting via the same aromatization-dependent neural pathways described in Section 24, together with possible reductions in neuronal aromatase expression in at-risk individuals, remains an area of active investigation rather than settled fact [22]. Comparably, ADHD shows a pronounced male bias in prevalence, with boys exhibiting more externalizing symptomatology (hyperactivity, impulsivity) and girls more internalizing symptomatology (inattention, anxiety), a pattern that has similarly been linked, though not conclusively, to differential prenatal androgen exposure and its downstream neurodevelopmental consequences [22,23]. A comparative review across ASD, ADHD, and schizophrenia emphasizes both shared features (male bias, implicated roles for prenatal hormonal and immune factors) and important differences in the developmental timing and specific neurobiological substrates of male-biased risk across these three conditions, cautioning against a single unified account [23].

32.3 Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia as a Natural Experiment

As introduced in Part IV, congenital adrenal hyperplasia provides a further human natural experiment bearing on this question: recent Mendelian randomization analysis, which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to test for causal relationships more robustly than purely observational association studies, reported evidence that adrenal-driven biochemical pathways characteristic of 21-hydroxylase deficiency are causally associated with altered risk of specific neuropsychiatric outcomes, including increased autism spectrum disorder risk, even as structural brain imaging studies of CAH have generally found less pronounced anatomical differences than might be predicted from behavioral findings alone [25,26]. Taken together with the ASD and ADHD hormone-exposure literature above, this body of work situates CAH as an important, biochemically well-defined test case for the broader hypothesis that atypical prenatal androgen exposure, mediated by the same organizational mechanisms responsible for normative male brain sexual differentiation, contributes to sex-biased neurodevelopmental disorder risk.

33. Fetal Glucocorticoid Programming of the Developing Brain

A parallel and mechanistically distinct line of research concerns the HPA axis (introduced in Section 13) as an additional, non-gonadal hormonal influence on fetal brain development. Glucocorticoids are essential, in physiological concentrations, for the normal maturation of multiple fetal organ systems, but excess fetal glucocorticoid exposure — whether from maternal stress-induced cortisol elevation, reduced placental 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 activity (the enzyme that normally inactivates maternal cortisol before it reaches the fetal compartment), or exogenous synthetic glucocorticoid administration (for example, antenatal corticosteroids given to accelerate fetal lung maturation in threatened preterm labor) — produces durable "programming" of the fetal HPA axis itself and of broader neurodevelopmental trajectories [19,20,21]. This programming, mechanistically, proceeds through epigenetic modification (methylation of the glucocorticoid receptor gene promoter, among other loci) of hypothalamic and hippocampal tissue, altering the sensitivity and set-point of the HPA axis for the remainder of the organism's life, with human observational data linking repeated courses of antenatal synthetic glucocorticoids to ADHD-like symptomatology in exposed offspring, and animal and human data together implicating impaired brain growth, altered behavior, and increased vulnerability to later metabolic and cardiovascular disease as downstream consequences of this fetal programming process [19,20,21]. The parallel between glucocorticoid programming of the HPA axis and gonadal steroid organization of sexually dimorphic circuits (Part V) is instructive: in both cases, a transient prenatal hormonal signal produces a durable, epigenetically stabilized change in neural tissue that is only fully revealed by later-life physiological or environmental challenge — stress reactivity in the case of glucocorticoid programming, and gonadal steroid activation in the case of sexual differentiation.

34. A Note on Endocrine-Disrupting Chemicals

Beyond endogenous hormonal programming, exogenous environmental chemicals capable of interacting with steroid hormone receptors or steroidogenic enzymes — endocrine-disrupting chemicals such as bisphenol A and certain phthalates — have been investigated for their capacity to perturb the same aromatization-dependent and receptor-mediated organizational mechanisms described in Part V, with experimental studies reporting sex-specific effects of prenatal bisphenol A exposure on the expression of genes associated with autism spectrum disorder risk in the hippocampus, illustrating that the biochemical pathways of normative sexual differentiation described throughout this review are also the pathways through which exogenous chemical exposures can, at least in experimental models, produce atypical neurodevelopmental outcomes.

PART VIII — INTEGRATION: CROSS-TALK BETWEEN AXES

35. HPG-HPA Interaction

The HPG and HPA axes, despite governing ostensibly distinct physiological domains (reproduction and stress response respectively), share overlapping hypothalamic circuitry and engage in extensive reciprocal cross-talk. Glucocorticoids, acting at multiple levels of the HPG axis, can suppress GnRH pulse generator activity, pituitary gonadotropin responsiveness, and gonadal steroidogenic capacity, providing a plausible mechanism by which chronic stress suppresses reproductive function; conversely, gonadal steroids modulate HPA axis reactivity, with organizational, sexually differentiated patterning of HPA stress responsivity itself likely established, at least in part, by the same perinatal hormone-dependent mechanisms responsible for the sexual differentiation of the SDN-POA and BNST described in Part V, given the anatomical overlap and shared steroid-receptor expression of the hypothalamic and extended-amygdala circuits governing both stress and reproductive physiology. The KNDy neuron circuit described in Section 11, situated in the arcuate nucleus, is itself a documented target of stress-axis input, providing a specific cellular node at which HPA activity can suppress HPG output.

35A. A Comparative and Evolutionary Coda

The mechanisms detailed throughout this review — organizational gonadal steroid action, aromatization, epigenetic stabilization — have been established predominantly in laboratory rodents, and it is worth closing the integrative discussion with a brief note on their generality. Comparative and evolutionary analysis across vertebrates indicates that the establishment of enduring brain and behavioral sex differences occurs during pre- or perinatal development quite generally, though the precise developmental timing varies substantially by species and life-history strategy, and the specific molecular substrate differs in emphasis even where the broad organizational logic is conserved [36]. A significant recent revision to the field's historical rodent-centric, gonadal-steroid-centric model has been the recognition that sex chromosome complement itself contributes directly, via cell-autonomous mechanisms independent of gonadal hormone secretion, to brain sex differences (introduced already in Section 27.4), and that inflammatory signaling molecules and immune cells — not classically considered part of the endocrine system at all — play a consistently observed, if mechanistically diverse, role in the masculinization of brain and behavior across the specific neuroanatomical substrates examined to date [36]. This revised, multicausal view does not overturn the organizational/activational and aromatization hypotheses that remain the conceptual backbone of Part V; rather, it situates gonadal steroid hormone action as one of several convergent, interacting causal streams — alongside direct sex chromosome gene dosage and neuroimmune signaling — that jointly sculpt the sexually differentiated vertebrate brain, a revision consistent with the broader multi-factorial, cross-talking systems view developed throughout this review [36].

36. A Systems-Level Model

Synthesizing the material of Parts II through VII, a systems-level model of "the Man" as a biochemical construction can be summarized as follows. A rostrocaudally and dorsoventrally patterned neural tube (Part II), built via Shh/BMP/Wnt/retinoic-acid-dependent gene regulatory networks and vulnerable to folate-modifiable closure defects, provides the anatomical substrate — hypothalamus, extended amygdala, and their neural crest-derived autonomic and neuroendocrine adjuncts — on which a genetically triggered (SRY-SOX9), enzymatically executed (StAR-CYP11A1-CYP17A1-HSD3B2-HSD17B3) program of testicular steroidogenesis, itself governed by a hypothalamic-pituitary GnRH/kisspeptin/gonadotropin circuit (Part III), imposes a two-stage program of internal and external genital masculinization (Part IV, via testosterone and DHT respectively) and, in parallel, a two-stage (organizational, then activational) program of neural sexual differentiation (Part V and Part VI) mediated substantially by local neuronal aromatization of testosterone to estradiol and stabilized by DNA methylation/demethylation and other epigenetic mechanisms, with the entire system additionally modulated by cross-talk with the HPA, HPT, and GH-IGF-1 axes (Part III, Part VIII) and vulnerable, at multiple points, to perturbation by dysregulated adrenal androgen production (CAH), androgen receptor or 5α-reductase deficiency (Part IV), fetal glucocorticoid excess, and — per an active and not yet fully resolved area of research — atypical prenatal hormone exposure contributing to sex-biased neurodevelopmental disorder risk (Part VII).

PART IX — CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

37. Summary

This review has traced the biochemistry of "the Man" across three integrated registers: the neuroembryology of the neural tube as the physical substrate of the nervous system; the enzymology and neuroendocrinology of the male hormonal axes, principally the HPG axis and its testicular steroidogenic output; and the cellular and molecular mechanisms — aromatization, genomic and non-genomic receptor signaling, apoptosis-mediated nucleus formation, and epigenetic stabilization — by which those hormones act upon that substrate to produce a sexually differentiated brain, from prenatal organization through pubertal and adult activation. Clinical syndromes of sex development (CAIS, 5α-reductase deficiency, CAH) and of neurodevelopmental vulnerability (ASD, ADHD) have served throughout as natural experiments clarifying, and in some cases complicating, the basic organizational/activational framework first proposed in 1959 [1] and substantially elaborated since [2,3,4,5].

38. Unresolved Questions and Future Directions

Several questions remain genuinely open. The precise degree to which the rodent-derived aromatization hypothesis and AFP-mediated protective mechanism apply to human brain sexual differentiation remains incompletely resolved, given substantial differences between rodent and human placental and hepatic steroid handling. The causal architecture linking prenatal androgen exposure to sex-biased neurodevelopmental disorder risk remains contested, with the "extreme male brain" hypothesis, the female protective effect, maternal immune activation, and prenatal stress currently understood as complementary rather than mutually exclusive contributors, and with Mendelian randomization and other causally-oriented genetic epidemiological methods only recently beginning to adjudicate among these possibilities in a rigorous way [22,23,25]. Methodologically, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiling of the developing human hypothalamus and extended amygdala, organoid-based models of human neural tube and forebrain development capable of incorporating steroid hormone exposure paradigms, and expanded genome-wide epigenomic mapping across development and into aging all represent promising avenues for resolving these questions with a precision not available to the foundational studies reviewed here. What is already clear, however, is that the biochemistry of the male phenotype — in body and in brain — is not the product of a single hormone or a single gene, but of a temporally extended, multiply cross-talking, and epigenetically stabilized network connecting the earliest events of neural tube patterning to the lifelong activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. ## APPENDIX — GLOSSARY OF ABBREVIATIONS
