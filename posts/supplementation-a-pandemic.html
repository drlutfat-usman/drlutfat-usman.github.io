Abstract

Dietary supplement use has grown from a niche wellness practice into a global industry worth more than $200 billion annually, consumed by a majority of adults in the United States and marketed under a “natural” halo that suppresses ordinary clinical skepticism. This growth has occurred almost entirely outside the regulatory framework that governs pharmaceuticals: since 1994, US law has placed the burden of proving a supplement unsafe on the government rather than requiring manufacturers to prove safety before sale. This review examines that regulatory vacuum and its clinical consequences through four case studies spanning three decades of evidence. Large randomized trials of antioxidant megadosing (beta-carotene, vitamin E, selenium) intended to prevent cancer instead found no benefit or measurable harm in several cohorts. Herbal supplements, particularly St John's wort and kava, have produced documented drug interactions and hepatotoxicity through mechanisms most patients and many clinicians do not anticipate. Stimulant and weight-loss supplements, from ephedra to contemporary products still found to contain undeclared pharmaceuticals years after FDA warnings, have caused cardiovascular deaths and continue to reach poison control centers in rising numbers. Finally, the review examines nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) precursor supplementation — a flagship product of the emerging longevity medicine movement — as a contemporary case study in which the same molecule protects against cancer in one clinical context (chemoprevention) and, per a 2026 study, protects cancer cells from chemotherapy in another. Across all four case studies, patient non-disclosure of supplement use to clinicians recurs as a common failure point. This review concludes that “dietary supplement” is a regulatory category, not a safety designation, and that clinicians across every specialty should treat supplement use as an active medication history item rather than an assumed-benign lifestyle detail.

Keywords: Longevity Medicine, dietary supplements, DSHEA, herb-drug interactions, supplement adulteration, NAD+, nicotinamide mononucleotide, patient disclosure, public health, pharmacovigilance

Introduction and Background

More than half of US adults now report taking at least one dietary supplement, and use has risen steadily across every demographic group over the past two decades, driven by multivitamins in older adults and, increasingly, by single-ingredient products marketed for specific outcomes: immunity, weight loss, muscle gain, cognitive performance, and, most recently, longevity itself [1]. The global market for these products was valued at roughly $210 billion in 2025 and is projected to nearly double by the early 2030s, a growth curve that shows no sign of plateauing. The word “supplement” implies something additive and benign, a nutritional top-up rather than a pharmacologically active substance, and marketing leans heavily on that implication. The reality is closer to the reverse: the products colloquially called “supplements” span an enormous range of bioactivity, from inert fillers to compounds capable of inducing hepatic enzymes, altering cardiac conduction, or feeding malignant cell metabolism, and the regulatory system in the United States and many other countries makes almost no distinction among them at the point of sale.

This review treats that mismatch — between the perceived safety of “natural” products and their actual, sometimes serious, biological activity — as a public health problem worth naming plainly: a pandemic of supplementation, in the literal sense of a widespread, self-propagating exposure that clinicians have been slow to systematically screen for. It surveys four representative case studies spanning three decades of accumulated evidence: antioxidant vitamins tested against cancer in large randomized trials, herbal products with documented drug interactions and organ toxicity, stimulant and weight-loss supplements associated with cardiovascular harm and pharmaceutical adulteration, and NAD⁺ precursor supplementation, a flagship product of the contemporary longevity medicine movement, examined here as an in-depth case study of how the same molecule can help or harm depending on clinical context. A recurring theme connects all four: patients rarely tell their clinicians what they are taking, and clinicians rarely ask specifically enough to find out.

Review

The Regulatory Vacuum: Why “Dietary Supplement” Does Not Mean “Proven Safe”

The modern US supplement market exists in its current form because of a single piece of legislation. The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA) defined dietary supplements as a distinct regulatory category and, critically, placed the burden of proof on the government to demonstrate that a marketed supplement is unsafe, rather than requiring the manufacturer to demonstrate safety before marketing, as is required for pharmaceuticals [2]. Under DSHEA, a manufacturer may market a supplement containing ingredients sold before October 1994 without any premarket safety review at all; only genuinely “new dietary ingredients” trigger even a notification requirement, and that notification is not an approval. The FDA can act after the fact, but only by proving in its own investigation that a marketed product presents a significant or unreasonable risk — a much higher and slower bar than the premarket efficacy-and-safety requirement that governs every prescription drug oncologists and other physicians prescribe.

This asymmetry explains a pattern that recurs throughout the rest of this review: a supplement can remain on shelves for years, sometimes decades, generating case reports and even randomized-trial evidence of harm, before any regulatory action follows, if it follows at all. It also means that the manufacturer's own quality control, rather than an external premarket process, is what stands between a product label and its actual contents — a gap explored directly in the discussion of adulteration below.

Case Study 1: Antioxidant Megadosing and the Paradox of the Cancer Prevention Trials

If any category of supplement should have been safe by intuition, it was antioxidant vitamins: beta-carotene, vitamin E, and selenium were reasoned, on sound if incomplete biochemical grounds, to neutralize the oxidative damage implicated in carcinogenesis. That intuition was tested directly, and expensively, in three of the largest chemoprevention trials ever conducted, and in two of the three the intuition failed in a clinically important way. The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene (ATBC) Cancer Prevention Study randomized over 29,000 male smokers in Finland to vitamin E, beta-carotene, both, or placebo, and found that participants receiving beta-carotene had an 18% higher incidence of lung cancer than those who did not, along with higher total mortality [3]. The Beta-Carotene and Retinol Efficacy Trial (CARET), which enrolled smokers and asbestos-exposed workers, was stopped early when combined beta-carotene and vitamin A supplementation produced a 28% increase in lung cancer incidence and a 17% increase in total mortality relative to placebo [4]. More than a decade later, the Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT), a randomized trial of over 35,000 men, found that vitamin E supplementation was associated with a statistically significant increased risk of prostate cancer, with neither selenium nor the combination showing benefit [5].

These trials share a structure worth naming explicitly, because it recurs in the NAD⁺ case study later in this review: an antioxidant mechanism that is protective against oxidative damage in normal cells can, in a population already carrying field-cancerized or early-malignant tissue, instead protect the abnormal cells from the oxidative stress that would otherwise help eliminate them. The lesson from three decades of chemoprevention research is not that antioxidants are uniformly harmful — dietary antioxidant intake from food has not shown the same signal — but that isolated, high-dose supplementation of a single compound cannot be assumed safe merely because the same compound is beneficial at dietary levels or in a different population.

Case Study 2: Herbal Supplements and Hidden Drug Interactions

Herbal supplements pose a different hazard from antioxidant megadosing: rather than directly harming the tissue that consumes them, several of the most popular botanicals alter the metabolism of concurrently prescribed drugs through hepatic enzyme induction or inhibition, an interaction easy to miss because the supplement itself may cause no symptoms. St John's wort (Hypericum perforatum), among the best-selling herbal antidepressant alternatives worldwide, induces cytochrome P450 3A4 and P-glycoprotein, accelerating the clearance of a wide range of drugs that depend on those pathways. The clinical consequences have been documented in the most serious terms possible: Ruschitzka et al. reported acute rejection in a heart transplant recipient whose cyclosporine levels collapsed after self-initiated St John's wort use, tracing the mechanism directly to enzyme induction [6]. Piscitelli et al. showed the same phenomenon in HIV pharmacology, where St John's wort reduced plasma concentrations of the protease inhibitor indinavir by57% on average, a magnitude sufficient to risk virologic failure and resistance [7]. Because St John's wort is sold without any prescriber gatekeeping, patients on immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals, oral contraceptives, or anticoagulants can unknowingly undermine therapy that depends on stable drug levels.

Kava (Piper methysticum), marketed as an anxiolytic, illustrates a second mechanism of herbal harm: direct organ toxicity rather than drug interaction. Case series and regulatory reviews attributed dozens of instances of severe hepatotoxicity, including several cases of fulminant liver failure requiring transplantation, to kava use, prompting import restrictions or bans in multiple countries in the early 2000s. Teschke's clinical and pathogenetic review of the accumulated case literature concluded that idiosyncratic and dose-dependent hepatotoxic mechanisms both plausibly contribute, complicated by variability in extraction solvents and plant part used across commercial products — a reminder that “herbal” describes a taxonomic origin, not a standardized pharmaceutical preparation [8]. Unlike a regulated drug, two bottles labeled “kava” from different manufacturers may not contain comparable doses of the same active constituents at all.

Case Study 3: Stimulants, Weight-Loss Products, and Pharmaceutical Adulteration

The weight-loss and performance-enhancement supplement category has produced the clearest cardiovascular harm signal of any class reviewed here, and the clearest evidence that the underlying regulatory problem has not been solved by any single enforcement action. Ephedra alkaloids, once widely marketed for weight loss and athletic performance, were linked by Haller and Benowitz's case-series analysis of FDA adverse event reports to strokes, myocardial infarctions, seizures, and sudden deaths, including in young and previously healthy users, ultimately prompting an FDA ban in 2004 [9]. That ban did not end the underlying problem; it only removed one ingredient from the available toolkit.

What replaced ephedra is, in some respects, more concerning, because it is often undisclosed. Tucker et al. systematically reviewed FDA warnings for adulterated dietary supplements issued between 2007 and 2016 and identified 776 products from 146 companies that contained undeclared active pharmaceutical ingredients, most commonly marketed for sexual enhancement, weight loss, or muscle building; sibutramine, an appetite suppressant withdrawn from the market in 2010 for cardiovascular risk, was found in 85% of adulterated weight-loss products, and synthetic anabolic steroids were found in 89% of adulterated muscle-building products [10]. Strikingly, the same study found that most of these companies received more than one FDA warning, and that later warnings frequently involved newly substituted undeclared ingredients rather than compliance — evidence that enforcement, as structured, functions as a game of regulatory whack-a-mole rather than a deterrent. Consistent with a genuine and growing exposure rather than an artifact of surveillance, Rao et al. documented a significant year-over-year increase in dietary supplement exposures reported to US poison control centers over a 13-year period, with the sharpest increases in exposures among young children and in products marketed for weight loss and energy [11]. Because a patient taking an adulterated “all-natural” weight-loss product has no way of knowing it may contain an unlabeled stimulant, sedative, or steroid, standard medication reconciliation — which asks about prescribed and over-the-counter drugs — systematically misses this entire category of exposure unless a clinician asks about supplements by name.

Case Study 4: NAD⁺ Precursor Supplementation in Oncology — Longevity Medicine's Newest Frontier

The first three case studies describe supplement categories with decades of accumulated post-market evidence. The fourth is different in kind: it is a live, unfolding case study in a supplement category still gaining popularity, illustrating that the pattern described above — regulatory permissiveness outpacing safety evidence — is not a historical artifact but a continuing feature of the supplement market, now expressed through the emerging longevity medicine movement. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) precursors sit alongside rapamycin and senolytics at the center of that movement, marketed to restore a coenzyme that declines with age. Oncologists cannot treat this as someone else's problem: cancer survivors are, almost by definition, longevity-medicine consumers, and the same NAD⁺ metabolism being marketed as protective is also one of the most extensively studied metabolic dependencies of cancer cells.

NAD⁺ Biology and the Rationale for Supplementation

NAD⁺ exists in a constant cycle of consumption and resynthesis. It can be synthesized de novo from tryptophan or, more efficiently, salvaged from dietary and endogenous precursors — nicotinamide, nicotinic acid, nicotinamide riboside, and nicotinamide mononucleotide — through a pathway centered on the rate-limiting enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), which converts nicotinamide to NMN before its final conversion to NAD⁺ [13,14]. Sirtuins (NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases implicated in metabolic regulation and genomic stability), PARPs (activated by DNA damage to orchestrate repair), and CD38 (a major NAD⁺-consuming ectoenzyme expressed on immune cells) all compete for this same finite pool [14,15]. Camacho-Pereira et al. demonstrated that CD38 expression rises with age and is a principal driver of the age-related decline in tissue NAD⁺, providing much of the mechanistic rationale for NAD⁺ repletion as an anti-aging strategy [15].

This biology underlies the appeal of NMN and NR: both bypass rate-limiting steps of the salvage pathway to raise cellular NAD⁺ more efficiently than nicotinamide alone, and preclinical rodent studies summarized by Rajman, Chwalek, and Sinclair link NAD⁺ repletion to improved mitochondrial function, insulin sensitivity, and stress resistance across multiple organ systems [14]. It is this same repletion logic, however, that raises the oncologic concern explored below: a pathway that supports mitochondrial and DNA-repair capacity in aging normal tissue may, in a tumor or its microenvironment, support the same functions in malignant cells.

Clinical Safety Evidence for NAD⁺ Precursors in Non-Oncologic Populations

The human evidence base for NMN and NR is real but narrow. Martens et al. conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of NR (up to 1,000 mg/day for six weeks) in healthy middle-aged and older adults and found it well tolerated, with elevated blood NAD⁺ metabolome but no significant effect on blood pressure, arterial stiffness, or insulin sensitivity [16]. Dollerup et al. similarly found NR safe and tolerable in obese, insulin-resistant men over 12 weeks, again without a clinically meaningful metabolic benefit in that trial [17]. Irie et al. established single- and multiple-dose safety of oral NMN (up to 500 mg) in healthy Japanese men over four weeks, with mild elevations in some laboratory parameters but no serious adverse events [18]. In a more clinically striking result, Yoshino et al. reported that 250 mg/day of NMN for 10 weeks improved skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity in prediabetic postmenopausal women, one of the few trials to show a functional metabolic benefit rather than tolerability alone [19]. Even at doses well above typical retail supplements, Brakedal et al.'s NADPARK study of 1,000 mg/day NR in Parkinson disease patients over 30 days found the intervention safe, raised brain NAD⁺ metabolism measurably, and produced modest clinical improvement, though a signal toward accelerated non-motor symptom progression in a subgroup underscored that “safe” and “inert” are not synonyms [20].

Two limitations of this literature matter enormously for oncology. First, every one of these trials excluded patients with active or recent malignancy, so “safe” here means safe in the population studied, not safe in a patient undergoing chemotherapy or radiation. Second, none was designed or powered to detect an oncologic signal such as tumor growth, recurrence, or treatment resistance; the endpoints were metabolic and functional, observed over weeks to a few months, far short of the time horizon over which a cancer-promoting effect would plausibly manifest. Extrapolating these reassuring safety profiles to oncology patients is therefore an evidence gap, not a validated inference — precisely the same gap that separated dietary antioxidant intake from high-dose antioxidant supplementation in the cancer-prevention trials discussed above.

Mechanistic Concerns: NAD⁺ Metabolism in Cancer Biology

Independent of the supplement-safety literature, NAD⁺ metabolism has been studied in oncology for over a decade as a therapeutic vulnerability, precisely because malignant cells are often more NAD⁺-dependent than normal tissue. Chiarugi et al., in an early and influential review, described the “NAD metabolome” as a key determinant of cancer cell biology: elevated NAD⁺ turnover supports the aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) that fuels rapid proliferation, and many tumor types overexpress NAMPT, the same enzyme NMN and NR are designed to feed into [21]. Yaku et al. extended this point directly, noting that increased NAD⁺ availability enhances glycolytic flux and fuels cancer cells, and that NAMPT inhibitors (such as FK866) have been developed as anticancer agents specifically to deplete tumor NAD⁺ pools, though clinical development has been limited by hematologic toxicity in normal tissue that shares the same dependency [22]. Navas and Carnero's review reinforces this dual identity of NAD⁺ metabolism, describing how NAMPT upregulation supports cancer stem cell properties through activation of pluripotency pathways, in addition to its roles in immune regulation and DNA repair — the very features that make NAD⁺ boosting attractive for healthy aging are, in a tumor, the features that support stemness and persistence [23].

Two more specific mechanistic threads sharpen the concern. First, in melanoma, Ohanna et al. showed that NAMPT drives the switch of melanoma cells toward an invasive, drug-resistant phenotype, positioning NAD⁺ salvage-pathway activity as a direct contributor to metastatic progression and therapy resistance rather than merely a bystander metabolic effect [24]. Second, in the immune compartment, Chen et al. demonstrated that CD38 — the same enzyme that consumes NAD⁺ and declines in activity with successful anti-aging repletion — is upregulated on tumor cells and myeloid cells as a mechanism of acquired resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade, with CD38 blockade restoring anti-tumor immunity in preclinical models [25]. Because CD38 itself consumes NAD⁺, the net effect of exogenous NAD⁺ precursor supplementation on this immunosuppressive axis in an actual tumor microenvironment is not straightforward to predict from either the aging literature or the immuno-oncology literature in isolation, and it has not, to date, been directly tested in patients receiving checkpoint inhibitors.

New Direct Evidence: NAD⁺ Precursors and Chemotherapy Resistance in Pancreatic Cancer

Until recently, the connection between NAD⁺ supplementation and clinical cancer risk was inferred from separate bodies of aging and oncology literature rather than demonstrated directly. That changed with a 2026 study from Case Western Reserve University, in which Nakazzi et al. tested NMN, NR, and NAM directly against pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell lines and mouse models treated with three chemotherapy backbones used in pancreatic cancer: oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and gemcitabine [26]. All three NAD⁺ precursors protected pancreatic cancer cells from chemotherapy-induced death, through three converging mechanisms: enhanced tumor cell energy production that increased resilience to metabolic stress, reduced oxidative stress that blunted a primary mechanism by which these chemotherapy agents kill cancer cells, and suppressed DNA damage signaling that limited the apoptotic response chemotherapy is intended to trigger. NMN produced the most pronounced protective effect among the three compounds tested.

This study has important limitations that should temper, without dismissing, its clinical implications. It is preclinical, conducted in cell lines and murine xenograft or genetically engineered mouse models rather than in patients, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma is a single, particularly NAD⁺-avid tumor type that may not generalize to all malignancies. Even with these caveats, the study is significant because it closes an inferential gap: rather than relying on separate lines of evidence about NAMPT biology and aging supplementation safety, it directly tested the interaction between a commercially available longevity supplement and the chemotherapy regimens patients are actually receiving, and found a consistent, mechanistically coherent, and clinically concerning signal. The study's senior author was explicit about the clinical stakes, describing the findings as highlighting a potentially concerning role for NAD⁺-boosting supplements in the context of active cancer treatment and calling for improved screening of supplement use and further clinical investigation of these interactions [26].

A Counterexample: Nicotinamide as Skin-Cancer Chemoprevention

Any critical review of this topic must resist the temptation to flatten “NAD⁺ boosting” into a single uniformly hazardous exposure, because the best human oncology evidence on this coenzyme pathway points the opposite direction in a specific, well-studied context — echoing the same dose- and context-dependence seen with dietary versus supplemental antioxidants in Case Study 1. In the ONTRAC trial, Chen et al. randomized 386 patients with a history of at least two nonmelanoma skin cancers in the preceding five years to oral nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily or placebo for 12 months, and found a 23% reduction in new nonmelanoma skin cancers, including statistically significant reductions in both basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma, with no meaningful difference in adverse events between groups [27]. Nicotinamide is itself an NAD⁺ precursor, feeding the same salvage pathway as NMN and NR, yet here it reduced rather than promoted carcinogenesis.

Reconciling this with the pancreatic cancer findings requires attention to context rather than treating the two studies as contradictory. The ONTRAC trial enrolled immunocompetent patients without an existing malignancy, using nicotinamide to prevent the first oncogenic transformation in UV-damaged skin; the proposed mechanism is that nicotinamide restores cellular ATP and NAD⁺ needed for nucleotide excision repair of UV-induced DNA damage and mitigates UV-induced immunosuppression, functioning as a DNA-repair enhancer in normal, damaged-but-not-yet-malignant keratinocytes [27]. The pancreatic cancer study, by contrast, examined the effect of NAD⁺ precursors on cells that had already undergone malignant transformation and were undergoing cytotoxic chemotherapy, where the same restored energetic and DNA-repair capacity instead protected established cancer cells from treatment-induced death [26]. The lesson for longevity medicine, as for the antioxidant trials before it, is that the clinical effect of a pathway-modulating supplement is not a fixed property of the molecule but depends on disease stage, tissue type, dose, and formulation — a distinction that a single blanket marketing claim cannot capture.

The Disclosure Gap: A Cross-Cutting Problem

Every case study above shares a common point of failure that is neither pharmacologic nor regulatory but behavioral: patients do not reliably tell clinicians what supplements they are taking, and clinicians do not reliably ask specifically enough to find out. Kantor et al.'s national survey data show supplement use concentrated in the same populations — older adults, patients with chronic disease — who are most likely to be on interacting prescription medications, yet routine medication reconciliation in most clinical settings still treats “supplements” as an afterthought checkbox rather than a structured inquiry [1]. Within oncology specifically, Tank et al. surveyed ambulatory cancer patients and found dietary supplement use to be common, driven substantially by a desire to “do something” for their own prognosis and general health, with a meaningful proportion of patients not discussing this use with their oncologist at all [28]. The poison-control surveillance data described above, showing rising pediatric and adult supplement exposures over more than a decade, is itself indirect evidence that supplement use is occurring at a scale and with a degree of clinical invisibility that outpaces current screening practice [11].

This disclosure gap is not simply a matter of patient forgetfulness. Supplements are sold without a prescription, displayed in the same retail aisles as food, and marketed with language — “natural,” “wellness,” “supports healthy aging” — deliberately distinct from pharmaceutical framing. A patient who would readily volunteer a prescribed statin or antidepressant may not think to mention an NAD⁺ powder, a St John's wort capsule, or a fat-burner purchased online, because none of it registers to them as “medication” in the first place.

Public Health and Clinical Recommendations

The case studies reviewed here point toward a consistent set of practical responses. Clinicians across every specialty, not only oncology, should incorporate specific, named supplement questions into medication reconciliation — asking about antioxidant megadoses, herbal products (naming St John's wort and kava explicitly), weight-loss or “fat-burner” products, and longevity/anti-aging products (naming NAD⁺, NMN, and NR explicitly) — rather than relying on an open-ended “any supplements?” prompt that patients do not reliably map onto the products they actually use. This is especially important before starting drugs with a narrow therapeutic index or that depend on stable CYP3A4-mediated clearance, and in any patient undergoing active cancer treatment, given the mechanistic and preclinical evidence reviewed above.

At the systems level, the FDA warning-letter approach to adulterated products, while necessary, has not proven sufficient on its own: the same manufacturers identified in Tucker et al.'s review frequently reappeared with new violations rather than achieving compliance, suggesting that post-market enforcement alone cannot substitute for premarket verification of label accuracy [10]. Poison control and adverse-event surveillance systems, meanwhile, remain the most sensitive tools currently available for detecting emerging harms in a category that largely escapes premarket safety testing, and their continued funding and clinician engagement (through voluntary adverse-event reporting) matters accordingly [11]. Finally, the specific research agenda outlined for NAD⁺ precursors in oncology — pharmacokinetic and safety studies in patients with active malignancy, correlative analysis within existing survivorship cohorts, and mechanistic work on CD38-mediated immune interactions with checkpoint inhibitor therapy — is a template that could reasonably be applied to any newly popularized supplement category before, rather than after, it reaches widespread use.

Conclusions

“Dietary supplement” is a regulatory category defined by an absence — the absence of premarket proof of safety or efficacy — not a clinical designation of benignity, and three decades of accumulated evidence bear this out repeatedly across unrelated product categories. Antioxidant vitamins, tested in some of the largest cancer-prevention trials ever conducted, increased cancer risk in specific populations rather than preventing it. Herbal supplements have caused organ transplant rejection, virologic treatment failure, and fulminant liver failure through mechanisms most patients never suspect. Stimulant and weight-loss products have caused cardiovascular deaths and, even after regulatory bans and repeated FDA warnings, continue to reach consumers adulterated with undeclared pharmaceuticals. And NAD⁺ precursor supplementation, the newest entrant from the longevity medicine movement, now carries direct preclinical evidence of protecting cancer cells from chemotherapy, set against equally real evidence that a related compound protects against skin cancer in a different clinical context. The unifying lesson across all four case studies is that context, dose, and disease state determine whether a bioactive compound helps or harms, and that this determination cannot be outsourced to a supplement label. Until regulatory frameworks require premarket safety evidence commensurate with a product's actual bioactivity, the clinical burden of catching these harms falls on routine, specific, and repeated supplement-use screening — a low-cost intervention that the evidence reviewed here suggests is not yet happening consistently enough to catch a genuinely widespread exposure.
