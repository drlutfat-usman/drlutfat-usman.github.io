A Review Article

Abstract

The female body is organized around an unusually dense and interactive endocrine architecture. Reproductive function alone requires the coordinated output of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, but that axis never operates in isolation: it is continuously cross-talking with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, the somatotropic (growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor-1) axis, the adipoinsular system of leptin and adiponectin, the calciotropic hormones that govern the skeleton, and neuropeptides such as oxytocin and vasopressin that extend endocrine signaling into social behavior and bonding. Across the female lifespan — from the kisspeptin-driven awakening of pulsatile gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion at puberty, through the cyclical remodeling of the endometrium each month, through the profound endocrine reorganization of pregnancy and lactation, to the depletion of ovarian follicular reserve at menopause — these systems rise, fall, and re-equilibrate together. This review synthesizes current biochemical and clinical literature on the major hormonal systems relevant to female physiology, with emphasis on steroidogenesis and steroid receptor pharmacology, gonadotropin regulation, placental endocrinology, thyroid-reproductive crosstalk, sex-differentiated stress biology, metabolic and adipokine signaling in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), adrenal androgen physiology, oxytocinergic signaling, the growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor-1 (GH/IGF-1) axis, and estrogen-dependent skeletal homeostasis. The aim is to provide clinicians, trainees, and advanced students with an integrated, mechanistically grounded, and properly referenced account of "a myriad of hormones" as they operate — individually and in concert — across a woman's life.

Keywords: estrogen; progesterone; hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis; menstrual cycle; menopause; thyroid; cortisol; insulin resistance; polycystic ovary syndrome; oxytocin; bone remodeling; growth hormone

1. Introduction

To describe a woman's physiology as governed by "a myriad of hormones" is not a figure of speech; it is a reasonably precise biochemical claim. At any given moment, a woman's tissues are being instructed simultaneously by steroid hormones diffusing from the ovary and adrenal cortex, glycoprotein hormones pulsing from the anterior pituitary, thyroid hormones circulating on carrier proteins, glucocorticoids following a circadian and stress-reactive rhythm, insulin and adipokines signaling nutritional status, and neuropeptides such as oxytocin coordinating reproduction with social attachment. These systems are not simply parallel; they are wired into one another through shared feedback loops, shared receptors, and shared upstream regulators such as kisspeptin neurons and the hypothalamic pulse generator. A change in one axis — weight loss severe enough to suppress leptin, for example, or the cortisol surge of chronic psychological stress — propagates measurably into the others, most visibly into the menstrual cycle itself, which functions as a sensitive bioassay of whole-body physiological state [2,53].

This review takes a systems-level approach to female endocrinology, treating the reproductive (HPG), stress (HPA), thyroid (HPT), metabolic/adipoinsular, somatotropic (GH/IGF-1), and calciotropic axes as an integrated network rather than a set of isolated organ systems. The scope is deliberately broad: it covers the biochemistry of steroidogenesis and steroid receptor pharmacology, the neuroendocrinology of puberty and the menstrual cycle, the unique endocrinology of pregnancy and lactation, the hormonal reorganization of the menopausal transition, sex-differentiated stress physiology, the metabolic hormone disturbances that underlie polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), the physiology of adrenal and ovarian androgens in women, the expanding role of oxytocin outside reproduction, sex differences in the growth hormone axis, and the estrogen-dependent biology of the skeleton. Each section is grounded in the primary and review literature, cited numerically in Vancouver style, with a consolidated reference list at the end of the document.

A note on scope and audience: this review is written at the level of an academic/clinical audience — physicians, endocrinology trainees, reproductive biologists, and advanced students — and assumes familiarity with basic biochemistry and cell signaling. Mechanistic detail (enzyme names, receptor isoforms, signal transduction cascades) is preserved throughout, because the central argument of this review is that female physiology cannot be understood hormone-by-hormone; it must be understood as a network, and the network can only be appreciated once its molecular nodes are named precisely.

2. General Principles of Endocrine Signaling

Before turning to specific axes, it is useful to establish the biochemical vocabulary that recurs throughout this review.

2.1 Classes of hormones

Hormones relevant to female physiology fall into three biochemical classes, each with distinct synthesis, transport, and receptor logic.

Steroid hormones (estrogens, progestogens, androgens, glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids) are lipophilic derivatives of cholesterol, synthesized in mitochondria and smooth endoplasmic reticulum through a shared cascade of cytochrome P450 and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzymes. Because they are lipid-soluble, they diffuse across the plasma membrane and act mainly through intracellular nuclear receptors that function as ligand-activated transcription factors, altering gene expression over a timescale of hours [4-6]. A second, faster tier of steroid signaling occurs through membrane-associated receptors (for example, membrane progesterone receptors of the PAQR family and G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor GPER) that trigger non-genomic, second-messenger-based effects within seconds to minutes [9].

Peptide and glycoprotein hormones (GnRH, LH, FSH, prolactin, oxytocin, insulin, leptin, hCG, parathyroid hormone) are synthesized as larger precursor proteins, cleaved, packaged into secretory granules, and released by regulated exocytosis. Being hydrophilic, they cannot cross the plasma membrane and instead act through cell-surface receptors — G-protein-coupled receptors for GnRH, oxytocin, and glycoprotein hormones; receptor tyrosine kinases for insulin and IGF-1; and cytokine-receptor superfamily members (JAK-STAT signaling) for leptin and prolactin.

Amine hormones (thyroid hormones, catecholamines) occupy an intermediate position. Thyroid hormones are tyrosine derivatives that, unusually for amines, are lipophilic enough to enter cells and act largely through nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, giving them a genomic mechanism more akin to steroids than to catecholamines [35].

2.2 Feedback architecture

Nearly every axis discussed in this review is organized as a hypothalamic-pituitary-peripheral gland loop with negative feedback: a hypothalamic releasing hormone drives a pituitary trophic hormone, which drives a peripheral gland to secrete an effector hormone, which feeds back — usually negatively, occasionally positively — on both the hypothalamus and pituitary. The HPG axis (GnRH → LH/FSH → estrogen/progesterone), HPA axis (CRH → ACTH → cortisol), and HPT axis (TRH → TSH → T4/T3) share this exact topology, which is why perturbation of one can so readily be mistaken, clinically, for perturbation of another, and why the axes are so easily coupled through shared central inputs [1,2,41].

A distinguishing feature of the HPG axis in women is that estrogen feedback is not fixed in sign: at low-to-moderate, sustained concentrations it is negative (suppressing GnRH/LH), but at the high, sustained concentrations reached late in the follicular phase it flips to positive, driving the LH surge that triggers ovulation. This switch is the single most important design feature that distinguishes cyclical female reproductive endocrinology from the comparatively tonic pattern seen in the male HPG axis [1,3].

2.3 Pulsatility as an information-encoding mechanism

Several hormones central to this review — GnRH and cortisol prominent among them — are secreted in a pulsatile, rather than a continuous, fashion, and the pituitary and peripheral targets are able to read pulse frequency and amplitude as an independent signal layered on top of mean concentration. GnRH pulse frequency, for instance, differentially controls the relative synthesis of LH versus FSH: rapid pulses favor LH transcription while slow pulses favor FSH transcription, which is one mechanism by which the same hypothalamic neuron population can steer the ovary through different phases of the cycle [2]. This principle recurs later in this review and explains, for example, why continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH agonist administration paradoxically suppresses rather than stimulates the axis — a pharmacological strategy exploited in fertility treatment and endocrine therapy.

3. The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis

The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis is the organizing system of female reproductive biochemistry. It comprises hypothalamic GnRH neurons, gonadotrope cells of the anterior pituitary that secrete luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and the ovary, which both responds to and feeds back upon the axis through estrogen, progesterone, inhibins, and activins [1-3].

3.1 GnRH neurons and pulsatile secretion

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) is a decapeptide synthesized by a small, developmentally unusual population of roughly a thousand neurons that originate in the olfactory placode and migrate into the hypothalamus during embryogenesis. Mature GnRH neurons project axon terminals to the median eminence, where they release GnRH into the hypophyseal portal circulation in discrete pulses rather than as a continuous secretion. Pulse frequency varies across the menstrual cycle — roughly one pulse per 60-90 minutes in the early-to-mid follicular phase, quickening further just before the periovulatory LH surge, and slowing to one pulse every several hours in the luteal phase under the restraining influence of progesterone [1,2]. Because pituitary gonadotropes decode this frequency information transcriptionally, GnRH pulsatility functions as a biochemical code: fast pulses upregulate LH-β transcription and favor LH secretion, while slow pulses favor FSH-β transcription and FSH secretion [2]. This frequency-decoding property explains a range of clinical phenomena, from the efficacy of pulsatile GnRH pumps in inducing ovulation in hypothalamic amenorrhea to the paradoxical suppressive effect of continuous GnRH agonist administration, which desensitizes and downregulates the GnRH receptor and is used therapeutically to achieve medical castration in endometriosis, uterine fibroids, and central precocious puberty.

GnRH neuronal activity is itself under the control of an upstream network of afferent inputs, discussed in section 3.2, and is also directly modulated by gonadal steroids, metabolic signals (leptin, insulin), and stress mediators (corticotropin-releasing hormone), which is the principal molecular route by which caloric restriction, obesity, and chronic stress each independently suppress reproductive cyclicity [2].

3.2 Kisspeptin and the neuroendocrine trigger of puberty

For more than three decades after GnRH itself was characterized, the identity of its immediate upstream regulator remained unclear. That gap was closed by the discovery that kisspeptin, the product of the KISS1 gene acting through its receptor KISS1R (formerly GPR54), is both necessary and, at the population level, largely sufficient to drive pulsatile GnRH release [13-15]. Kisspeptin neurons cluster in two hypothalamic nuclei with distinct roles: the arcuate nucleus population (often described, together with neurokinin B and dynorphin co-expression, as "KNDy" neurons) is now understood to function as the GnRH pulse generator itself, while the anteroventral periventricular nucleus population is implicated in generating the preovulatory GnRH/LH surge under positive estrogen feedback [2,13-15].

Puberty onset reflects the reawakening of this kisspeptin-GnRH circuit after a juvenile quiescent period. Kisspeptin neuron activity is itself gated by metabolic and adiposity signals — most importantly leptin — providing a direct biochemical link between nutritional status/body fat and reproductive maturation: chronically low leptin (as in significant undernutrition or very low body fat) delays or arrests pubertal onset and disrupts adult cyclicity, while the kisspeptin system is thought to integrate this metabolic information with genetic and developmental cues to determine pubertal timing [13-15]. There remains active debate over whether kisspeptin should be conceived of as the singular "trigger" of puberty or as an amplifier of a slower maturational process already underway in GnRH neurons themselves; current evidence favors the latter, more nuanced view [15].

3.3 Gonadotropins: LH and FSH

LH and FSH are heterodimeric glycoprotein hormones that share a common alpha subunit (identical to the alpha subunit of hCG and TSH) and a hormone-specific beta subunit that confers receptor specificity. Both are secreted by pituitary gonadotropes in response to GnRH acting through the GnRH receptor, a Gq-coupled receptor that activates phospholipase C, and both act on the ovary through distinct G-protein-coupled, Gs-linked receptors (LHCGR and FSHR) that stimulate cAMP-protein kinase A signaling in their respective target cells [1-3].

FSH acts principally on granulosa cells of the growing follicle: it upregulates the aromatase enzyme CYP19A1, drives granulosa cell proliferation, and induces LH receptor expression on granulosa cells as the dominant follicle matures — a change that renders the pre-ovulatory follicle newly responsive to the LH surge. LH acts principally on theca cells throughout the cycle, driving androgen precursor synthesis, and then, at the mid-cycle surge, acts on the mature granulosa cell to trigger resumption of meiosis, follicular rupture (ovulation), and luteinization of the residual follicle into the corpus luteum, which LH continues to support through progesterone secretion during the luteal phase [1,3,11]. The two-cell, two-gonadotropin cooperation between LH-driven thecal androgen synthesis and FSH-driven granulosa aromatization is the central biochemical event of ovarian estrogen production and is elaborated in section 3.4.

Ovarian feedback onto gonadotropin secretion is mediated not only by the steroids estrogen and progesterone but also by two structurally related glycoprotein hormones of the TGF-β superfamily: inhibin (predominantly inhibin B in the follicular phase and inhibin A in the luteal phase), which selectively suppresses FSH secretion, and activin, which stimulates it. This dual steroidal and peptide feedback allows differential, rather than yoked, control of LH and FSH secretion across the cycle [1,2].

3.4 Ovarian steroidogenesis: the two-cell, two-gonadotropin model and estrogen biosynthesis

All steroid hormones derive from cholesterol through a shared initial and rate-limiting step: transport of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane by steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), followed by cleavage of the cholesterol side chain by CYP11A1 (P450scc) to yield pregnenolone [4-6]. From pregnenolone, the pathway branches according to the enzyme complement expressed in a given cell: 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD) converts pregnenolone to progesterone; 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase (CYP17A1) converts pregnenolone or progesterone toward the androgen precursors dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and androstenedione; and, in cells expressing aromatase (CYP19A1), androstenedione and testosterone are converted to the estrogens estrone and estradiol, respectively, with 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (HSD17B1) interconverting estrone and the more potent 17β-estradiol [4-6].

Critically, no single ovarian cell type expresses the complete enzymatic pathway from cholesterol to estradiol. Theca interna cells express CYP17A1 and, under LH stimulation, convert cholesterol to androstenedione, but they lack significant aromatase activity. Granulosa cells express abundant FSH-induced aromatase but have limited access to androgen substrate on their own. The androgen product of the theca cell therefore diffuses across the basal lamina into the granulosa cell layer, where FSH-driven aromatase converts it to estrone, which HSD17B1 further reduces to estradiol — the two-cell, two-gonadotropin model of ovarian estrogen synthesis [4]. This compartmentalized division of labor is the biochemical reason that both LH (driving thecal androgen supply) and FSH (driving granulosa aromatization) are required for normal estradiol output, and it explains why disorders of either gonadotropin, or of the enzymes themselves (as in some forms of PCOS, discussed in section 8.3, where excess thecal androgen synthesis outpaces aromatization capacity), produce characteristic hormonal phenotypes.

Circulating estradiol concentrations rise through the follicular phase in proportion to the growth of the dominant follicle, peak roughly 24-36 hours before ovulation (providing the positive-feedback trigger for the LH surge described above), fall transiently after ovulation, and rise again — together with progesterone — during the luteal phase as a secondary product of the corpus luteum. After menopause, ovarian estradiol synthesis effectively ceases, and circulating estrogen derives almost entirely from peripheral aromatization of adrenal and ovarian stromal androgens in adipose tissue, skin, and other extragonadal sites — a pathway of considerable clinical importance in postmenopausal physiology and in estrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer [5,26].

Estrogen acts through two classical nuclear receptors, estrogen receptor alpha (ESR1) and estrogen receptor beta (ESR2), which have overlapping but distinct tissue distributions and, in some contexts, opposing transcriptional effects, as well as through the membrane-bound G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER/GPR30), which mediates rapid, non-genomic signaling [9]. The balance of ESR1 and ESR2 expression varies substantially by tissue — ESR1 predominates in the uterus and mammary epithelium, while ESR2 is relatively enriched in ovarian granulosa cells, bone, and parts of the central nervous system — and this differential expression underlies the tissue-selective pharmacology exploited by selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as tamoxifen and raloxifene.

3.5 Progesterone: synthesis, transport, and receptor pharmacology

Progesterone is synthesized from cholesterol via pregnenolone through the action of 3β-HSD and is the dominant secretory product of the corpus luteum during the luteal phase, and later of the placenta during pregnancy [7,8,10]. Beyond its role as a steroidogenic intermediate, progesterone is a hormone in its own right, acting principally through the nuclear progesterone receptor (PR), which exists as two major isoforms, PR-A and PR-B, transcribed from a single gene using alternate promoters. PR-A and PR-B have distinct, sometimes opposing, transcriptional activities, and the ratio between them varies by tissue and across the menstrual cycle, providing a mechanism by which the same circulating progesterone concentration can produce different effects in the endometrium, myometrium, breast, and brain [7,8].

Progesterone's principal physiological role in the reproductive tract is to convert an estrogen-primed, proliferative endometrium into a secretory, decidualizable endometrium receptive to implantation, and to maintain myometrial quiescence during the luteal phase and pregnancy by opposing estrogen-driven contractility — the mechanistic basis for progesterone and progestin use in luteal-phase support during assisted reproduction and in tocolysis. Beyond the reproductive tract, progesterone receptors are expressed in the mammary gland (where progesterone drives lobuloalveolar development in concert with prolactin), in bone, in the cardiovascular system, and extensively in the central nervous system, where progesterone and its neuroactive metabolite allopregnanolone modulate GABA-A receptor function [8,10,70].

In addition to the classical nuclear PR, progesterone signals through a family of seven-transmembrane membrane progesterone receptors (mPRα, mPRβ, mPRγ, structurally related to the PAQR family of receptors) that couple to inhibitory G proteins and mediate rapid, non-genomic progesterone effects, particularly in the ovary, oviduct, and central nervous system [70]. The existence of this parallel signaling system helps explain progesterone effects that occur too rapidly to be accounted for by classical transcriptional mechanisms, such as some of progesterone's acute neuromodulatory and smooth-muscle effects.

3.6 The menstrual cycle as an integrated hormonal program

The human menstrual cycle is best understood not as a sequence of isolated events but as a single, continuously unfolding hormonal program with four physiologically overlapping phases: the follicular phase, ovulation, the luteal phase, and menstruation, which is itself the terminal event of luteal regression [11,12].

During the early-to-mid follicular phase, a cohort of antral follicles recruited from the ovarian reserve competes under rising FSH stimulation; through the two-cell mechanism described in section 3.4, the cohort begins to secrete increasing amounts of estradiol, and, through a combination of FSH-induced LH receptor expression and FSH-suppressing inhibin B secretion, a single dominant follicle is typically selected by the mid-follicular phase while its companions undergo atresia. Estradiol from the dominant follicle rises steeply in the late follicular phase, and once concentrations exceed a threshold and are sustained for roughly 36-50 hours, the sign of estrogen's central feedback flips from negative to positive, triggering the massive, self-limited LH surge (accompanied by a smaller FSH surge) that is the immediate trigger for the resumption of oocyte meiosis, follicular rupture, and ovulation approximately 36 hours later [1,11].

The ruptured follicle then luteinizes under continued LH support to form the corpus luteum, a transient endocrine gland whose principal secretory product is progesterone, accompanied by continued, lower-level estradiol secretion and by inhibin A, which now assumes the FSH-suppressive role previously played by inhibin B. Progesterone during the luteal phase converts the endometrium to a secretory, receptive state, slows GnRH pulse frequency (contributing to the tonic gonadotropin pattern characteristic of this phase), and — if fertilization and implantation do not occur — the corpus luteum undergoes programmed regression (luteolysis) roughly 14 days after ovulation, in the absence of the rescue signal (hCG) that would otherwise be provided by an implanting embryo (section 4.1). The abrupt withdrawal of progesterone and estradiol that accompanies luteolysis triggers prostaglandin-mediated vasoconstriction and shedding of the functional endometrial layer — menstruation — and the consequent loss of steroidal negative feedback allows FSH to rise again, recruiting the next follicular cohort and restarting the cycle [11,12].

This entire program is exquisitely sensitive to perturbation from outside the HPG axis itself. Hypothalamic amenorrhea from energy deficit, thyroid dysfunction (section 6.3), hyperprolactinemia, hyperandrogenic states such as PCOS (section 8.3), and chronic activation of the HPA axis (section 7) can each independently suppress GnRH pulsatility or otherwise disrupt this program, which is why the menstrual cycle is frequently described in clinical endocrinology as a physiological "vital sign" — a readily observable output that reflects the integrated state of several converging hormonal systems [2,12,48].

4. Pregnancy and Lactation Endocrinology

Pregnancy converts the cyclical HPG program described in section 3 into a sustained, non-cyclical hormonal state dominated first by the corpus luteum, then by the placenta itself, which becomes the dominant endocrine organ of pregnancy [16].

4.1 Human chorionic gonadotropin and maternal recognition of pregnancy

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is a glycoprotein hormone structurally related to LH, sharing the identical alpha subunit and a beta subunit similar enough to LH's that hCG can bind and activate the LHCGR receptor. hCG is synthesized by the syncytiotrophoblast beginning remarkably early in development — its messenger RNA has been detected as early as the eight-cell stage, and the protein is produced by the blastocyst prior to implantation [17,18]. Its principal early function is to "rescue" the corpus luteum from the programmed regression that would otherwise occur roughly 14 days after ovulation (section 3.6): by binding LHCGR on luteal cells, hCG sustains progesterone (and estradiol) secretion by the corpus luteum until placental steroidogenesis becomes self-sufficient, typically around 8-10 weeks of gestation — the so-called luteal-placental shift [16-18].

hCG exists in several molecular isoforms — classical hCG, hyperglycosylated hCG (associated with early trophoblast invasion), the free beta subunit, and sulfated forms — each with somewhat distinct biological roles, and beyond corpus luteum rescue, hCG has pleiotropic actions that include promoting trophoblast invasion and syncytialization, immunomodulation at the maternal-fetal interface, and stimulation of fetal testicular testosterone production in male pregnancies during the window relevant to genital differentiation [17,18]. Serum hCG rises exponentially in early pregnancy, typically doubling every 48-72 hours, peaks around 8-10 weeks, and then declines to a lower plateau for the remainder of gestation — the biochemical basis of both urine/serum pregnancy testing and early pregnancy dating [16-18].

4.2 The placenta as an endocrine organ

Beyond hCG, the syncytiotrophoblast secretes a large repertoire of protein and steroid hormones, including human placental lactogen (hPL, also called chorionic somatomammotropin), which shares structural homology with growth hormone and prolactin and progressively shifts maternal metabolism toward relative insulin resistance and lipolysis in later pregnancy, thereby prioritizing glucose delivery to the fetus; placental growth hormone variant (hGH-V), which replaces pituitary growth hormone in the maternal circulation as pregnancy advances; placental corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), secreted in escalating amounts and implicated in a "placental clock" hypothesis of parturition timing; placental-derived ACTH; parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), important for fetal calcium transport; and progressively increasing quantities of progesterone and estriol (a relatively weak estrogen that becomes the dominant circulating estrogen of pregnancy and is synthesized cooperatively by the fetal adrenal, fetal liver, and placenta) [16,19]. This shift of the dominant maternal estrogen from estradiol toward estriol, and of overall progesterone production from the corpus luteum to the placenta, together constitute the endocrine signature that distinguishes established pregnancy from the luteal phase of a non-conception cycle [16].

The secretion of these placental hormones is compartmentalized within specific trafficking pathways of the syncytiotrophoblast, and disruption of normal placental hormone secretion — whether insufficient (as in early pregnancy loss) or dysregulated (as in some hypertensive disorders of pregnancy) — has been increasingly linked to specific defects in these secretory pathways rather than simple placental insufficiency alone [19].

4.3 Relaxin

Relaxin is a peptide hormone structurally related to insulin and insulin-like growth factors, produced principally by the corpus luteum and, during pregnancy, by the placenta and decidua [23,24]. Circulating relaxin rises sharply in the first trimester, peaking around the end of that trimester, declines through mid-pregnancy, and rises again just before labor. Its principal actions are on maternal connective tissue and the cardiovascular system: relaxin promotes remodeling of pelvic ligaments and softening of the cervix in preparation for delivery, and it is also a potent vasodilator that contributes to the substantial increase in renal and systemic blood flow and the fall in systemic vascular resistance characteristic of early pregnancy, in part through upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor and matrix metalloproteinase activity in the uterine vasculature [23,24]. Because of its vasoactive role, relaxin deficiency or dysregulation has been investigated as a contributor to uteroplacental vascular dysfunction and has been proposed as a first-trimester biomarker for preeclampsia risk, although clinical evidence for this application remains preliminary [23].

4.4 Prolactin and lactogenesis

Prolactin is a pituitary hormone, structurally related to growth hormone and placental lactogen, secreted by lactotrophs under predominantly inhibitory control by hypothalamic dopamine acting through D2 receptors — a regulatory arrangement distinct from every other anterior pituitary hormone, all of which are principally under stimulatory hypothalamic control [20-22]. During pregnancy, rising estrogen concentrations stimulate lactotroph proliferation and prolactin secretion, priming the breast for lactation; however, actual copious milk secretion (lactogenesis II) is held in check until delivery, because the high estrogen and progesterone concentrations of pregnancy antagonize prolactin's action at the mammary epithelium. The abrupt withdrawal of placental estrogen and progesterone at delivery removes this restraint, allowing prolactin to drive full lactogenesis over the subsequent 24-72 hours [20-22].

Once lactation is established, prolactin secretion becomes suckling-dependent: mechanical stimulation of the nipple-areolar complex during breastfeeding triggers a neuroendocrine reflex that transiently suppresses hypothalamic dopamine tone, producing a pulse of prolactin release that sustains milk protein and lactose synthesis in mammary epithelial cells for the subsequent feeding cycle [20-22]. Sustained, suckling-driven prolactin secretion also suppresses GnRH pulsatility — mediated in part by prolactin's inhibitory effect on kisspeptin neurons — which underlies the physiological, though not fully reliable, contraceptive effect of exclusive breastfeeding (lactational amenorrhea) [20].

4.5 Oxytocin and the milk-ejection reflex

While prolactin governs milk synthesis, the physical delivery of milk to the infant depends on oxytocin, a nonapeptide synthesized in magnocellular neurons of the hypothalamic paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei and released from the posterior pituitary [20,21]. Tactile stimulation of the nipple during suckling generates afferent signals that reach these hypothalamic nuclei and trigger pulsatile oxytocin release; oxytocin then acts on oxytocin receptors expressed on myoepithelial cells surrounding the mammary alveoli, causing their contraction and ejection of milk into the ductal system — the milk-ejection ("let-down") reflex [20,21]. Notably, this reflex can become classically conditioned to non-tactile cues (the sound or sight of an infant), reflecting central, learned modulation of the oxytocinergic circuit.

Prolactin and oxytocin interact bidirectionally during lactation: prolactin's tonic inhibition of oxytocin neuronal activity, present outside of lactation, is functionally suppressed during lactation, which is thought to facilitate the coordinated co-release of both hormones needed to sustain milk supply [20]. Oxytocin released during breastfeeding also acts centrally to promote maternal-infant bonding and a range of maternal caregiving behaviors, a theme that extends well beyond lactation and is treated more broadly in section 10, and postpartum oxytocin, vasopressin, and prolactin concentrations have been associated with maternal psychological and infant weight-gain outcomes in the early postpartum period, an area of active clinical research [21].

5. Menopause and Reproductive Aging

5.1 Ovarian reserve and anti-Müllerian hormone

Women are born with a finite, non-renewable pool of primordial follicles — the ovarian reserve — that declines progressively from before birth onward, initially through a rapid wave of prenatal and childhood atresia and subsequently through the more gradual, age-related attrition that determines the timing of reproductive senescence [27,29-31]. Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), a TGF-β superfamily glycoprotein secreted by granulosa cells of small growing (preantral and early antral, up to roughly 8 mm) follicles, has emerged as the most useful circulating biomarker of the size of this remaining follicle cohort, because — unlike FSH, which is a pituitary hormone and only an indirect readout of ovarian status — AMH is secreted directly by the structures being measured [29-31]. Serum AMH rises through childhood, peaks around the mid-twenties, and then declines progressively, becoming undetectable at a median age of approximately 50-51 years, coincident with menopause [30,31]. Because AMH secretion is relatively gonadotropin-independent and shows less within- and between-cycle variability than FSH or estradiol, it has become the preferred clinical marker for estimating ovarian reserve and for predicting, albeit imprecisely, the approximate timing of menopause and the likely response to ovarian stimulation in assisted reproduction, although its precision as a population-level predictor of age at spontaneous menopause remains limited [29-31].

5.2 The perimenopausal transition

Age-related decline in ovarian follicle number begins to produce compensatory hormonal changes well before overt cycle irregularity appears, typically starting in the mid-to-late thirties. As the growing follicle cohort shrinks, inhibin B secretion (section 3.3) falls, releasing FSH from tonic suppression; FSH concentrations rise gradually, which for a time compensates for the declining follicle pool by driving more vigorous stimulation of the remaining follicles, preserving estradiol output and cycle regularity despite falling ovarian reserve [27,28]. As this compensatory mechanism is progressively exhausted, the perimenopausal transition proper begins, characterized by increasingly erratic — rather than simply diminished — hormone secretion: cycles shorten as the follicular phase contracts under high FSH drive, anovulatory cycles become more frequent, and estradiol concentrations can swing between subnormal and supraphysiological (from sporadic, exuberant follicular responses to elevated FSH) in a pattern far more erratic than the gradual decline sometimes assumed in lay descriptions of menopause [25,27,28]. This hormonal volatility, rather than simple estrogen deficiency, is thought to underlie many of the vasomotor and mood symptoms characteristic of the perimenopausal years, which frequently precede the final menstrual period by several years [25,28].

5.3 Endocrinology of established menopause

Menopause is defined retrospectively as twelve consecutive months of amenorrhea attributable to the loss of ovarian follicular activity, reflecting depletion of the follicle pool below the threshold required to sustain cyclical estradiol production [28,72]. In the established postmenopausal state, ovarian estradiol secretion falls to very low concentrations, FSH and LH rise substantially (often ten-fold or more) as they are released from steroidal and inhibin-mediated negative feedback, and the dominant circulating estrogen shifts from ovarian 17β-estradiol to estrone derived from peripheral aromatization of adrenal and residual ovarian stromal androgens (principally androstenedione) in adipose tissue and other extragonadal sites [25,26]. Because this peripheral aromatization pathway is adiposity-dependent, postmenopausal estrone concentrations correlate positively with body fat, one of several mechanisms linking obesity to postmenopausal estrogen-sensitive disease risk [26].

Beyond the ovary itself, postmenopausal endocrine change is broader than sex-steroid loss alone: adrenal DHEA and DHEAS secretion (section 9) continues to decline with age independent of menopause per se, the loss of estrogen's restraining effect on bone resorption produces accelerated skeletal loss (section 12), and estrogen withdrawal is associated with unfavorable shifts in lipid profile, vascular function, and body fat distribution toward a more central pattern, changes that collectively account for much of the increase in cardiometabolic and osteoporotic risk observed after menopause [25,26].

5.4 Menopausal hormone therapy: pharmacology and risk-benefit framework

Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) aims to replace declining ovarian estrogen — with concomitant progestogen in women with an intact uterus, to protect the endometrium from unopposed estrogen-driven proliferation — and remains the most effective available treatment for vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats) and genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and is effective for prevention of postmenopausal bone loss and fracture in appropriately selected women [32-34]. Contemporary guidance from major professional bodies frames the benefit-risk balance of MHT as strongly dependent on the timing of initiation relative to menopause, the dose and route of administration, and individual cardiometabolic and thrombotic risk factors: initiation within roughly ten years of the final menstrual period (or before age 60) is generally associated with a more favorable benefit-risk profile than later initiation, and transdermal estradiol delivery, which avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism, appears to carry lower venous thromboembolic risk than oral formulations, making it a preferred option in women with elevated baseline cardiometabolic or thrombotic risk [32-34]. Beyond symptom control and fracture prevention, individualized MHT has also been associated with improvements in sleep, mood, and sexual function, reinforcing a broader, quality-of-life-oriented framework for treatment decisions rather than a narrow focus on any single outcome [33,34].

6. Thyroid Hormones and Female Physiology

6.1 Synthesis, transport, and activation of thyroid hormones

The thyroid gland, under stimulation by pituitary thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) itself driven by hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), synthesizes and secretes thyroxine (T4) and, to a lesser extent, triiodothyronine (T3) via iodination and coupling of tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin [35]. T4 is secreted in far greater quantity — the thyroid produces roughly nine parts T4 to one part T3 — but T4 is the comparatively inactive form; the majority (roughly 80%) of biologically active T3 is generated not in the thyroid itself but in peripheral tissues, through removal of an outer-ring iodine atom from T4 by deiodinase enzymes (type 1 deiodinase in liver and kidney; type 2 deiodinase in the brain, pituitary, and other tissues with high local T3 demand) [35]. This peripheral activation step is a major point of physiological and pharmacological regulation, since deiodinase activity — and therefore local T3 availability — can be modulated independently of circulating TSH or T4 concentrations, for example by illness, fasting, or certain medications (the "sick euthyroid" or non-thyroidal illness pattern).

T4's substantially longer half-life (approximately 7 days, versus roughly 1 day for T3) makes it a stable circulating reservoir from which tissues draw T3 according to local need, a design that buffers the organism against transient fluctuations in thyroidal output [35]. Like steroid hormones, both T3 and T4 act predominantly through nuclear thyroid hormone receptors that alter gene transcription, and elevated T3/T4 exert negative feedback at both the hypothalamus (suppressing TRH) and pituitary (suppressing TSH), completing the classical HPT feedback loop [35].

6.2 Sex differences in autoimmune thyroid disease

Autoimmune thyroid disease — both Hashimoto's thyroiditis (the leading cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions) and Graves' disease (the leading cause of hyperthyroidism) — disproportionately affects women, a disparity among the largest of any common autoimmune condition. Population estimates cited in recent reviews put the incidence of Graves' disease at roughly ten-fold higher in women than men (on the order of 80 per 100,000 person-years in women versus 8 per 100,000 in men in Western populations), and incidence of hypothyroidism has been estimated at over 200-fold higher in women in some cohorts, with the sex disparity emerging specifically around puberty rather than being present from birth [36,37,71]. Proposed mechanisms for this striking sex skew include direct immunomodulatory effects of estrogen on B- and T-lymphocyte function and antibody production, X-chromosome-related factors including skewed X-inactivation and haploinsufficiency of X-linked immune-regulatory genes, fetal microchimerism (persistence of fetal cells in maternal tissue after pregnancy), and possible roles for the microbiome and environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals; no single mechanism is considered fully sufficient, and the leading current view treats the disparity as multifactorial [36,37]. Clinically, women with autoimmune thyroid disease also show sex-specific symptom patterns and downstream effects, including documented associations between thyroid autoimmunity and impaired sexual function in young women [36].

6.3 Thyroid-reproductive axis interactions and pregnancy

Thyroid hormone has direct and indirect effects on reproductive function: thyroid hormone receptors are expressed in the ovary, endometrium, and along the HPG axis, and both overt hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are associated with menstrual irregularity, anovulation, and reduced fertility, with thyroid dysfunction identified in a meaningful proportion (on the order of 5-10%) of couples presenting with infertility [38]. Hypothyroidism, in particular, elevates TRH, which — beyond its stimulatory effect on TSH — also stimulates pituitary lactotrophs, producing hyperprolactinemia that can independently suppress GnRH pulsatility and cause anovulation, illustrating a direct biochemical crosstalk pathway between the HPT and HPG axes distinct from any effect of thyroid hormone on the ovary itself.

Pregnancy substantially reorganizes thyroid physiology and thyroid hormone requirements. Maternal thyroxine-binding globulin rises under estrogen stimulation, increasing total (though not necessarily free) T4 concentrations; hCG, which shares the LH/FSH/TSH common alpha subunit and has intrinsic thyrotropic activity through weak cross-reactivity at the TSH receptor, transiently stimulates thyroid hormone secretion and suppresses TSH in the first trimester, particularly at the peak of hCG secretion; and maternal thyroid hormone requirements rise overall to support both maternal metabolism and fetal neurodevelopment, since the fetal thyroid does not become fully functional until roughly mid-gestation and the fetus depends on transplacental maternal thyroid hormone, especially in the first trimester [38-40]. These physiological shifts necessitate trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and free T4 in pregnancy, since non-pregnant reference intervals systematically misclassify normal pregnant physiology as abnormal [39]. Both overt and, to a lesser but still clinically relevant extent, subclinical maternal thyroid dysfunction and isolated thyroid autoimmunity (positive thyroid peroxidase antibodies with normal thyroid function) have been associated with adverse obstetric and fetal outcomes, including pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and impaired offspring neurodevelopment, and treatment of overt maternal hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism has been shown to improve pregnancy outcomes, providing the rationale for thyroid function screening and monitoring protocols in pregnancy and the postpartum period, when autoimmune thyroiditis (postpartum thyroiditis) is also common [38-40].

7. The HPA Axis: Cortisol and Stress Biochemistry in Women

7.1 The CRH-ACTH-cortisol cascade

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is the principal endocrine mediator of the stress response and, like the HPG and HPT axes, is organized as a three-tier negative-feedback loop. Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), synthesized in parvocellular neurons of the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus, is released into the hypophyseal portal circulation (often together with arginine vasopressin, which potentiates CRH's effect) and stimulates corticotrope cells of the anterior pituitary to synthesize and release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), cleaved from the larger precursor proopiomelanocortin (POMC) [41,42]. ACTH acts on melanocortin-2 receptors in the adrenal cortex's zona fasciculata to stimulate cortisol synthesis from cholesterol via the same StAR-dependent, CYP11A1-initiated pathway described in section 3.4, with cortisol-specific steps mediated by 21-hydroxylase and 11β-hydroxylase [41]. Circulating cortisol acts through two distinct intracellular receptors — the high-affinity mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), which is substantially occupied even at low, basal (circadian trough) cortisol concentrations, and the lower-affinity glucocorticoid receptor (GR), which becomes progressively occupied as cortisol rises toward circadian peak or stress-induced concentrations — and exerts negative feedback at both the hypothalamus and pituitary, closing the loop [41]. Beyond acute stress reactivity, the HPA axis also generates a robust circadian rhythm, with cortisol peaking shortly after waking (the cortisol awakening response) and reaching a nadir around midnight, a rhythm that is itself entrained by the central circadian pacemaker in the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

7.2 Sex differences in HPA reactivity

The HPA axis is not sexually monomorphic. Reviews of human and animal literature converge on a broadly consistent, if mechanistically complex, pattern: gonadal steroids exert opposing effects on axis reactivity, with androgens generally dampening and estrogens generally sensitizing HPA responsiveness to acute stress [43-46]. In practical terms, this has been described as producing a more rapidly initiating, higher-output stress response in women compared with men under some experimental stress paradigms, alongside evidence that estrogen can, in other contexts and depending on which estrogen receptor subtype predominates, blunt physical/somatic components of the stress response — a body of literature reviewers describe as showing genuinely complex, context- and receptor-subtype-dependent effects rather than a single uniform direction of influence [44-46]. Androgen signaling through the androgen receptor has been specifically implicated as a restraining influence on HPA output, providing one plausible mechanistic contributor to average sex differences in axis reactivity, alongside organizational (developmental, largely irreversible) and activational (adult, reversible) actions of gonadal steroids on the CRH neuron population and on pituitary corticotrope excitability itself, which recent work suggests also differs by sex at the cellular level [45,46].

These differences are proposed to be clinically relevant well beyond acute stress physiology: women experience substantially higher lifetime rates of several stress-related psychiatric conditions, including major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder, and sexually differentiated HPA reactivity is one of several converging hypotheses offered to help explain this epidemiological disparity, alongside social, developmental, and other biological contributors [45]. HPA axis sensitivity to stress also appears to shift across major reproductive transitions in women — puberty, the perimenopausal transition, and the postpartum period among them — consistent with a broader pattern in which the HPG and HPA axes are neuroendocrinologically coupled rather than independent [2,45].

7.3 Cortisol across the menstrual cycle

Beyond long-run sex differences, cortisol secretion in women also varies systematically across the menstrual cycle itself. A meta-analysis of circulating cortisol by cycle phase found significantly higher cortisol concentrations in the follicular phase than in the luteal phase, a pattern proposed to reflect direct modulatory effects of progesterone, its neuroactive metabolites, and estradiol on HPA output and on corticosteroid-binding globulin, which governs the free, biologically active fraction of circulating cortisol [47]. Because progesterone and its metabolites (notably allopregnanolone) also interact with the same GABA-A receptor system implicated in anxiety regulation, and because progesterone itself can compete for glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptor binding at sufficiently high luteal-phase concentrations, the luteal phase represents a period of particularly dense biochemical crosstalk between the HPG and HPA axes [47,48]. This crosstalk has practical clinical relevance: stress-related mood symptoms in premenstrual dysphoric disorder, postpartum mood disorders, and perimenopausal mood instability are each hypothesized, in part, to reflect abnormal sensitivity to the qualitatively normal hormonal transitions described throughout this review, rather than abnormalities of hormone concentration per se — a "sensitivity," not "deficiency," model that has gained substantial support in the reproductive psychiatry literature. Cortisol has also been examined jointly with prolactin and other menstrual cycle hormones as a determinant of conception likelihood in infertility populations, underscoring that HPA tone is not merely a bystander to reproductive function but an active modulator of it [48].

8. Metabolic Hormones: Insulin, Leptin, Adiponectin, and Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

8.1 Insulin signaling and insulin resistance

Insulin, secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to rising glucose, acts through the insulin receptor, a receptor tyrosine kinase that, upon ligand binding, autophosphorylates and recruits insulin receptor substrate (IRS) proteins, activating downstream PI3K-Akt signaling (governing glucose uptake and metabolic effects) and Ras-MAPK signaling (governing mitogenic and growth-promoting effects). Insulin resistance — reduced tissue responsiveness to a given insulin concentration — produces compensatory hyperinsulinemia as beta cells increase output to maintain euglycemia. Critically for female reproductive endocrinology, insulin resistance and its accompanying hyperinsulinemia appear to be pathway-selective rather than global: metabolic (PI3K-Akt-dependent) insulin signaling is impaired in many insulin-resistant states, while mitogenic and steroidogenic (MAPK-dependent) insulin signaling in the ovary often remains intact or is even enhanced, allowing compensatory hyperinsulinemia to continue driving ovarian androgen production even as insulin's metabolic effects elsewhere in the body are blunted [49]. This selective-resistance model is central to understanding polycystic ovary syndrome, discussed in section 8.3.

8.2 Leptin and adiponectin in reproductive tissue

Adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ, and two of its principal secreted products, leptin and adiponectin, exert direct and largely opposing effects on reproductive function [52,53]. Leptin, secreted in proportion to fat mass, acts on hypothalamic receptors — including, as discussed in section 3.2, receptors upstream of or on kisspeptin neurons — to signal energy sufficiency to the reproductive axis; leptin concentrations below a physiological threshold, as occurs in significant energy deficit, low body fat, or eating disorders, are associated with suppressed GnRH pulsatility, hypothalamic amenorrhea, and delayed puberty, providing a direct molecular link between nutritional/energy status and fertility [52,53]. Leptin receptors are also expressed directly within the ovary, uterus, and other peripheral reproductive tissues, indicating that leptin's reproductive effects are not confined to the hypothalamus [53].

Adiponectin, in contrast to leptin, is secreted inversely to adiposity — concentrations fall as fat mass rises — and generally exerts insulin-sensitizing, anti-inflammatory effects; within the ovary, adiponectin has been shown to modulate granulosa cell steroidogenesis and is implicated in normal follicular development, and clinically, higher adiponectin concentrations have been associated with more regular menstrual cyclicity and improved outcomes in assisted reproduction [52,53]. In obesity, the combination of elevated leptin (with a degree of central leptin resistance) and suppressed adiponectin creates a hormonal milieu that independently promotes ovulatory dysfunction, superimposed on the direct effects of insulin resistance described above — an important mechanistic link between obesity and the anovulatory infertility seen in some, though not all, individuals with PCOS [52,53].

8.3 PCOS: hyperandrogenism, anovulation, and metabolic disease

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder of reproductive-age women and is defined, in its core Rotterdam-criteria form, by some combination of clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology on imaging [49-51,54-56]. Biochemically, PCOS is characterized by excess ovarian (and sometimes adrenal) androgen production; total and free testosterone are recommended by current evidence-based diagnostic guidance as the first-line laboratory measures of biochemical hyperandrogenism, with androstenedione, DHEAS, and dihydrotestosterone offering supplementary but less standardized diagnostic value [54]. A substantial majority of women with PCOS — estimates cited in recent reviews range from roughly 65% to 95%, including most overweight or obese women with the syndrome and more than half of women with PCOS who are of normal weight — demonstrate measurable insulin resistance [49].

The pathophysiological model that best accounts for the syndrome's characteristic biochemical phenotype links insulin resistance directly to hyperandrogenism through several convergent mechanisms: compensatory hyperinsulinemia acts, via the largely-preserved mitogenic/steroidogenic insulin signaling pathway described in section 8.1, directly on ovarian theca cells to amplify LH-stimulated androgen synthesis; insulin also suppresses hepatic synthesis of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), the principal carrier protein that binds and thereby limits the bioavailability of circulating testosterone, so that even a normal total testosterone concentration can translate into an elevated, biologically active free androgen fraction when SHBG is low [49,54,55]; and elevated androgen concentrations, in turn, can independently exacerbate insulin resistance and adipose dysfunction, creating a self-reinforcing biochemical cycle. Additional contributory mechanisms proposed in the recent literature include disordered GnRH pulsatility with a relative bias toward LH-favoring rapid pulse frequency (compounding thecal androgen drive, per section 3.1), low-grade chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, accumulation of advanced glycation end-products, and a substantial genetic and epigenetic contribution, with the syndrome now understood to reflect a multifactorial, rather than single-cause, disease process [50,55,56].

Clinically, this biochemical profile produces the syndrome's hallmark features: hirsutism and acne from peripheral androgen excess; oligo-ovulation or anovulation from disrupted intraovarian and central hormonal signaling, with attendant menstrual irregularity and infertility; and, because insulin resistance is central rather than incidental to the pathophysiology, a substantially elevated lifetime risk of type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and other metabolic syndrome components, positioning PCOS as fundamentally a metabolic-reproductive disorder rather than a purely gynecologic one [49-51].

9. Androgens in Women: DHEA, DHEAS, and Adrenal Contributions

Although androgens are conventionally framed as "male" hormones, they are physiologically indispensable in women, serving both as direct effectors and as obligate precursors for ovarian estrogen synthesis (section 3.4). Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and its sulfated storage form, DHEA-sulfate (DHEAS), are the most abundant circulating steroid hormones in the human body and are synthesized predominantly by the adrenal zona reticularis, with additional ovarian and peripheral tissue contributions [57-60]. In premenopausal women, combined adrenal, ovarian, and peripheral synthesis produces roughly 6-8 mg of DHEA daily; because DHEAS has a long circulating half-life (in contrast to DHEA's rapid turnover) and shows minimal diurnal variation, DHEAS is the preferred clinical marker of overall adrenal androgen output [57-60].

DHEA and DHEAS are, in a strict biochemical sense, pro-hormones rather than hormones with strong direct receptor activity: their principal physiological importance lies in serving as substrate for peripheral, tissue-specific conversion into more potent androgens (androstenedione, testosterone, dihydrotestosterone) and, via aromatization, into estrogens, through locally expressed steroidogenic enzymes — a process termed "intracrinology," in which target tissues regulate their own local androgen and estrogen exposure largely independent of circulating gonadal hormone concentrations [57,58]. This intracrine system is thought to be particularly important in postmenopausal women, in whom ovarian estrogen synthesis has ceased but DHEA-driven peripheral steroidogenesis continues to supply local estrogenic and androgenic activity to tissues such as bone, skin, and the vaginal epithelium [58].

Circulating DHEAS follows a distinctive lifetime trajectory, rising through childhood and peaking around the mid-twenties (adrenarche and its aftermath), then declining progressively — by roughly 10% per decade — through the remainder of adult life, a phenomenon termed "adrenopause" that is distinct from, and proceeds on a different timetable than, ovarian menopause [57]. Beyond their steroidogenic precursor role, DHEA and testosterone acting directly in women have documented physiological roles in reproductive tissue function, mood and cognition, mammary gland biology, bone density maintenance, skeletal muscle mass, and vascular function, and clinical interest in low-dose testosterone or DHEA supplementation for indications such as hypoactive sexual desire disorder or adrenal insufficiency-related androgen deficiency reflects this broader physiological role, though such use requires careful individualized risk-benefit assessment [58-60].

10. Oxytocin and Vasopressin: Neuropeptide Hormones Beyond Reproduction

Oxytocin and arginine vasopressin are structurally related nonapeptides, differing by only two amino acids, synthesized in overlapping populations of hypothalamic magnocellular neurons and released both peripherally (from posterior pituitary terminals, acting as classical circulating hormones) and centrally (from dendritic and axon-collateral release within the brain, acting as a neuromodulator) [61-63]. Peripherally, oxytocin's best-characterized roles are the two already described in this review: stimulation of uterine smooth muscle contraction during labor (via oxytocin receptors that are markedly upregulated in the myometrium near term) and the milk-ejection reflex of lactation (section 4.5).

Centrally, oxytocin receptors are densely expressed in limbic structures — including the amygdala, ventromedial hypothalamus, nucleus accumbens, and other regions of the mesolimbic reward system — where oxytocin modulates the salience and reward value of social stimuli, facilitates social recognition, and is strongly implicated in the formation and maintenance of selective social attachments, including the maternal-infant bond and pair-bonding between adult partners [61-63]. Because oxytocin release accompanies several of the most socially and physiologically significant events in a woman's reproductive life — coitus and orgasm, parturition, and breastfeeding — it has been proposed as a biochemical thread linking reproductive physiology directly to affiliative social behavior, trust, and empathy, effects mediated through modulation of synaptic plasticity and neuronal firing patterns within these limbic circuits rather than through any single, unitary "bonding" mechanism [61-63]. Reviews caution that oxytocin's social effects are context-dependent and modulatory rather than uniformly prosocial — the same peptide can under some conditions heighten vigilance toward out-group or threatening social stimuli — underscoring that oxytocin functions as a modulator of social salience broadly construed, rather than a simple "love hormone" [62,63].

Vasopressin, oxytocin's close structural relative, functions peripherally as the primary regulator of renal water reabsorption (via V2 receptors) and as a vasoconstrictor (via V1 receptors), but also acts centrally, often in a sexually dimorphic fashion, to modulate social behaviors including pair-bonding, territoriality, and — as noted in section 7.1 — as a potentiator of CRH-driven ACTH release, providing a further point of direct biochemical crosstalk between the neuropeptide systems governing social behavior and the HPA stress axis [21,61].

11. The Growth Hormone/IGF-1 Axis: Sex-Specific Regulation

Growth hormone (GH), secreted by pituitary somatotrophs under stimulatory control from hypothalamic GH-releasing hormone and inhibitory control from somatostatin, acts both directly on target tissues and indirectly by stimulating hepatic (and local tissue) synthesis of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which mediates many of GH's anabolic and growth-promoting effects [68,69]. The GH/IGF-1 axis shows well-documented sex differences that are directly attributable to circulating estrogen, illustrating another point of direct crosstalk between the HPG axis and a nominally separate endocrine system.

Women secrete GH in a pattern of higher overall pulsatile output than age-matched men — on the order of two- to three-fold greater GH secretion — yet circulating IGF-1 concentrations in women are similar to, or in some studies lower than, those in men, indicating reduced hepatic GH sensitivity in women despite greater GH drive [69]. The principal mechanism is estrogen-dependent: estrogen, particularly via first-pass hepatic exposure with oral administration, suppresses hepatic GH receptor signaling and downstream IGF-1 generation, an effect confirmed clinically in studies of GH-deficient adults, where women required substantially higher replacement GH doses than men to achieve equivalent IGF-1 responses, and where estrogen-treated women required higher GH doses than women not receiving estrogen therapy [68,69]. This estrogen-mediated hepatic GH resistance has direct clinical implications for dosing GH replacement therapy in hypopituitary women, particularly those using oral versus transdermal estrogen, and more broadly illustrates that the "sex difference" in a given hormonal axis often does not reside in that axis's own central regulation but in downstream modulation by an entirely separate hormone system — a recurring theme throughout this review [68,69].

12. Estrogen and Skeletal Homeostasis: the RANKL/RANK/OPG and Wnt Pathways

Bone is in a continuous state of remodeling, coupling resorption by osteoclasts to formation by osteoblasts, and estrogen is among the most important physiological regulators of the balance between these two processes in women [64-67]. The central molecular switch governing osteoclast differentiation and activity is the RANKL/RANK/osteoprotegerin (OPG) system: osteoblasts and bone lining cells produce receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), which binds its receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors to drive their differentiation, fusion, and activation, while the same osteoblastic lineage cells also secrete osteoprotegerin, a soluble decoy receptor that binds RANKL and prevents it from engaging RANK, thereby restraining osteoclastogenesis; the ratio of RANKL to OPG, rather than the absolute concentration of either alone, is the proximate determinant of net bone resorptive activity [64,66,67].

Estrogen acts directly on this system, suppressing RANKL expression by osteoblasts and bone lining cells while stimulating OPG secretion, and thereby restraining osteoclast-mediated resorption; estrogen also independently limits osteoblast and osteocyte apoptosis and modulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway that governs osteoblastic bone formation, giving estrogen a dual restraining-and-supportive role across both arms of the remodeling cycle [64,65]. The abrupt fall in circulating estradiol at menopause (section 5.3) removes this restraint: RANKL expression rises, the RANKL:OPG ratio increases, osteoclastic resorption accelerates and outpaces formation, and the result is the rapid phase of postmenopausal bone loss — most pronounced in the first 5-10 years after the final menstrual period — that underlies the sharply elevated postmenopausal incidence of osteoporosis and fragility fracture [64-67]. This mechanistic pathway is also the direct pharmacological target of denosumab, a monoclonal antibody that mimics OPG by binding and neutralizing RANKL, and helps explain why menopausal hormone therapy (section 5.4) remains an effective, if not first-line in all cases, means of preventing postmenopausal bone loss [32,64]. Androgen deficiency contributes to bone loss through parallel, partially overlapping mechanisms, underscoring that skeletal homeostasis in women reflects integrated input from multiple gonadal steroid pathways rather than estrogen action in isolation [65].

13. Fetal Sexual Differentiation and Developmental Programming of Later Hormonal Function

The hormonal systems described in the preceding sections do not begin at puberty; their anatomical substrate is laid down in utero, and the process of fetal sexual differentiation is itself a hormonally directed event worth reviewing on its own terms, since several of its molecular actors reappear, repurposed, throughout adult female physiology [73-75].

In the genetically female (46,XX) embryo, the gonad develops as an ovary by default, in the absence of the Y-chromosome-encoded SRY gene that would otherwise trigger testicular differentiation via SOX9 activation around the seventh week of gestation in a male embryo [73]. Because the fetal ovary does not, at this stage, secrete significant anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH, the same glycoprotein hormone that reappears in section 5.1 as an adult ovarian reserve marker, here performing an entirely different developmental function) or fetal androgen, the paired Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts are not suppressed and differentiate, in the absence of AMH signaling, into the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper vagina, while the Wolffian (mesonephric) ducts, deprived of androgen support, regress [73-75]. In other words, female internal genital development is fundamentally a default, hormone-absence pathway, in contrast to male development, which requires active hormonal signaling (AMH-driven Müllerian regression and androgen-driven Wolffian development and external virilization) to proceed away from a common bipotential starting anatomy [73,74].

This developmental logic has direct clinical relevance to disorders of sex development, in which loss-of-function or gain-of-function variants affecting AMH, the AMH receptor, androgen synthesis, or the androgen receptor produce discordance between chromosomal, gonadal, and phenotypic sex, and in which measurement of AMH remains a clinically useful diagnostic tool precisely because of its developmental specificity [74,75]. Disorders of sex development of adrenal origin — most commonly congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency — illustrate a distinct mechanism, in which impaired cortisol synthesis removes negative feedback on ACTH, driving adrenal hyperplasia and shunting steroidogenic precursors toward androgen synthesis; in a 46,XX fetus, this excess prenatal androgen exposure can virilize the external genitalia despite normal ovarian and Müllerian development, distinguishing it clinically from primary gonadal disorders of sex development [75].

Beyond the anatomical patterning reviewed here, a substantial body of work in developmental endocrinology — the "organizational-activational" hypothesis referenced briefly in section 7.2 — proposes that the hormonal milieu of fetal and early postnatal life exerts permanent, organizational effects on the later sensitivity of adult neuroendocrine circuits, including the HPA axis, to activating hormones in adulthood, providing one candidate explanatory mechanism, still under active investigation, for some of the adult sex differences documented throughout this review [45,46].

14. The Mammary Gland Across the Lifespan: Hormonal Regulation of Breast Development

The breast is unusual among human organs in that the great majority of its structural development occurs postnatally, under direct and continuously changing hormonal control, making it a useful capstone illustration of how the axes reviewed throughout this article converge on a single peripheral tissue across the female lifespan [76-79].

At birth, the mammary gland consists of only a rudimentary ductal structure. It remains largely quiescent through childhood until puberty, when rising ovarian estradiol, acting in concert with growth hormone and locally generated IGF-1 (section 11), drives ductal elongation and branching morphogenesis through the surrounding fat pad, a process requiring estrogen receptor alpha signaling in the terminal end buds at the growing tips of the ductal tree [76-79]. This pubertal expansion window has attracted particular research interest because the terminal end buds are thought to be especially susceptible, during this period of rapid estrogen- and GH-driven proliferation, to environmental carcinogen exposure, a susceptibility-window hypothesis relevant to lifetime breast cancer risk [79].

Progesterone, largely absent in meaningful concentration before the onset of ovulatory cycling, becomes a second major architect of breast structure once cyclical ovulation is established: acting through progesterone receptor signaling on the ductal epithelium, progesterone drives side-branching and the formation of rudimentary lobuloalveolar structures during the luteal phase of each cycle, a proliferative effect that regresses, along with the corpus luteum itself, if pregnancy does not occur — meaning that the adult, non-pregnant breast undergoes a small-scale version of the proliferation-regression cycle each month, mirroring the endometrium [76-78].

Pregnancy converts this cyclical, limited progesterone effect into a sustained, much larger-scale developmental program: the combined, high, sustained concentrations of estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, and placental lactogen (sections 4.1-4.4) drive extensive alveologenesis, converting the ductal tree into a secretory organ fully populated with milk-producing alveolar units, though — as discussed in section 4.4 — actual copious secretion is held in check by high progesterone and estrogen until their withdrawal at delivery triggers lactogenesis [76,77]. After weaning, involution returns the gland to a resting, non-secretory state through a hormonally regulated program of programmed epithelial cell death, completing a developmental cycle that can repeat with each subsequent pregnancy and that ultimately terminates, at menopause, in glandular atrophy and replacement with adipose tissue as estrogen and progesterone support is withdrawn [76,77]. Because estrogen and progesterone receptor signaling is so central to normal breast development, the same receptors are, unsurprisingly, the dominant molecular drivers and therapeutic targets in the most common molecular subtype of breast cancer, and the pubertal- and pregnancy-associated windows of hormone-driven proliferation described here are directly relevant to epidemiological models of lifetime breast cancer risk [9,79].

15. Neurosteroids and Mood: Allopregnanolone, GABA-A Signaling, and Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder

Section 3.5 introduced allopregnanolone as a progesterone-derived neurosteroid capable of direct, non-genomic modulation of the GABA-A receptor, the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter receptor in the central nervous system; this mechanism deserves closer treatment because it is now understood to be central to the pathophysiology of premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), a severe, cyclical mood condition affecting a meaningful minority of reproductive-age women [80-82].

Allopregnanolone is synthesized from progesterone via sequential reduction by 5α-reductase and 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, and, unlike classical progesterone signaling through the nuclear progesterone receptor (section 3.5), it acts as a potent positive allosteric modulator at the GABA-A receptor, binding at a site distinct from the benzodiazepine site and, at physiological concentrations, enhancing chloride channel conductance in a manner that produces anxiolytic, sedative, and mood-stabilizing effects broadly analogous to those of benzodiazepines [80,81]. Because allopregnanolone concentrations track luteal-phase progesterone (rising through the luteal phase and falling sharply premenstrually, in parallel with progesterone itself, section 3.6), most women experience a monthly rise and fall in this endogenous GABA-A modulator without adverse effect.

The leading pathophysiological model of PMDD, however, proposes that affected women are not experiencing abnormal allopregnanolone concentrations but an abnormal central nervous system sensitivity to the same, qualitatively normal luteal-phase fluctuation — a change in GABA-A receptor subunit composition or responsiveness that converts what is, in most women, a mood-neutral or mood-stabilizing hormonal signal into a dysphoria-provoking one [80,81]. Neuroimaging work has correlated abnormal emotion-related brain activation across the menstrual cycle in women with PMDD with measured concentrations of progesterone-derived neurosteroids, lending direct support to this sensitivity-based model over a simple hormone-excess or hormone-deficiency account [82]. This framework is directly analogous to, and consistent with, the "sensitivity, not deficiency" model of reproductive-transition mood disorders introduced in section 7.3, and it has produced a specific and successful therapeutic corollary: pharmacological agents that block or normalize allopregnanolone's action at the GABA-A receptor, including a synthetic isomer developed specifically to antagonize allopregnanolone's effect, have shown efficacy in PMDD, as has synthetic allopregnanolone itself (brexanolone and its oral prodrug) in the mechanistically related condition of postpartum depression, in which the abrupt postpartum withdrawal of pregnancy-level progesterone and allopregnanolone is hypothesized to play an analogous precipitating role [80,81].

16. Calcium Homeostasis: Parathyroid Hormone, Vitamin D, and Calcitonin in Women

Skeletal health, introduced in section 12 from the perspective of estrogen-dependent bone remodeling, depends equally on a second, largely estrogen-independent hormonal system that governs systemic calcium homeostasis: the parathyroid hormone (PTH)-vitamin D axis [83-85].

Parathyroid hormone, secreted by the parathyroid glands in response to falling ionized serum calcium (sensed via the calcium-sensing receptor), acts to restore calcium concentration through three coordinated mechanisms: it mobilizes calcium from bone by stimulating osteoblastic RANKL expression (directly intersecting the pathway described in section 12), it enhances renal tubular calcium reabsorption while promoting phosphate excretion, and it stimulates renal 1α-hydroxylase, which converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D — the major circulating, storage form of vitamin D produced by hepatic hydroxylation of vitamin D from skin synthesis or diet — into 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the biologically active hormone that increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption [83-85]. Calcitriol, in turn, exerts negative feedback on PTH secretion, completing a classical feedback loop analogous in structure to those already described for the HPG, HPA, and HPT axes.

This system interacts directly with the estrogen-dependent bone physiology of section 12: because vitamin D insufficiency (commonly defined as serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D below 30 ng/mL) is highly prevalent — affecting roughly three-quarters of postmenopausal women in some population studies — many postmenopausal women experience secondary hyperparathyroidism superimposed on estrogen-deficiency bone loss, in which chronically elevated PTH, driven by inadequate calcium absorption, compounds estrogen-deficiency-driven RANKL upregulation to further accelerate bone resorption [83,84]. This compounding effect is the biochemical rationale for routine calcium and vitamin D sufficiency as baseline therapy alongside, rather than instead of, estrogen or antiresorptive therapy in postmenopausal osteoporosis prevention and treatment, and clinical trial evidence supports correction of vitamin D insufficiency as an effective means of normalizing PTH and improving downstream markers of bone health in postmenopausal women [84,85]. Calcitonin, secreted by thyroid parafollicular (C) cells in response to rising serum calcium, acts as a direct physiological counterweight to PTH by inhibiting osteoclast activity, though its quantitative contribution to day-to-day human calcium homeostasis is considerably smaller than that of the PTH-vitamin D axis and it is of greater pharmacological than physiological significance in adult women.

17. Sex Hormones and Immune Function: A Broader View of Autoimmunity in Women

Section 6.2 documented the striking female predominance of autoimmune thyroid disease, but this pattern is not unique to the thyroid: the great majority of systemic autoimmune conditions — including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, and multiple sclerosis, in addition to autoimmune thyroiditis — disproportionately affect women, often by a ratio of several-to-one or greater, and gonadal steroid signaling is one of the principal biological hypotheses offered to explain this pattern across, rather than within, individual autoimmune diseases [36,37].

Mechanistically, estrogen has been shown, across multiple experimental systems, to modulate both innate and adaptive immunity: it can enhance B-lymphocyte survival and antibody (including autoantibody) production, shift T-helper cell differentiation, and modulate dendritic cell and macrophage activity, effects that are broadly, though not uniformly, immunostimulatory at the physiological concentrations characteristic of reproductive-age women, in contrast to androgens, which are more consistently immunosuppressive [36,37]. This estrogen-immune relationship offers one candidate explanation for the tendency of some autoimmune conditions to first present or to flare during periods of relatively higher or more variable estrogen exposure (puberty, pregnancy in some conditions, the postpartum period) and to remit, in some cases, after menopause. As introduced in section 6.2, this hormonal hypothesis is understood to act alongside, not instead of, several other proposed contributors to the female predominance of autoimmunity, including X-chromosome dosage and inactivation effects, fetal microchimerism from prior pregnancies, and environmental and microbiome factors, and the current literature treats female-predominant autoimmunity as genuinely multifactorial rather than attributable to sex hormones alone [36,37].

18. Hormonal Contraception: Pharmacological Manipulation of the HPG Axis

Hormonal contraceptives are, from a biochemical standpoint, a direct and deliberate application of the feedback principles established in section 3: they work by supplying exogenous steroid hormone at concentrations sufficient to engage the same negative-feedback circuitry that ordinarily regulates the natural cycle, thereby suppressing the endogenous signals required for follicular development and ovulation [86,87].

Combined hormonal contraceptives (containing both an estrogen, most commonly ethinylestradiol, and a progestin) act through several converging mechanisms. The progestin component is the dominant suppressive agent: sustained progestin exposure slows GnRH pulse frequency and directly suppresses pituitary LH secretion, preventing the LH surge required for ovulation, while the estrogen component principally suppresses FSH secretion, preventing the recruitment and selection of a dominant follicle described in section 3.6 [86]. Beyond this central suppression of the HPG axis, combined hormonal contraceptives also raise hepatic synthesis of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) — the reverse of the SHBG-suppressing effect of hyperinsulinemia described in PCOS, section 8.3 — thereby lowering free androgen concentrations, which is the mechanistic basis for using certain combined oral contraceptives as a treatment for the hyperandrogenic symptoms (acne, hirsutism) of PCOS itself, and additionally thicken cervical mucus and thin the endometrium, providing secondary contraceptive and, in the case of endometrial thinning, therapeutic effects relevant to abnormal uterine bleeding [86].

Not all progestins are pharmacologically equivalent. Because the progesterone receptor is structurally related to the androgen, glucocorticoid, and mineralocorticoid receptors, synthetic progestins vary considerably in their off-target activity at these related receptors: some (for example, the 19-nortestosterone-derived progestins) retain partial androgen receptor agonism, while others (cyproterone acetate, drospirenone) are deliberately designed as androgen receptor antagonists, and drospirenone additionally acts as a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, giving it a mild antimineralocorticoid, potassium-sparing profile distinct from other progestins [86]. This receptor cross-reactivity is clinically consequential: comparative studies of combined oral contraceptives in PCOS have shown that antiandrogenic-progestin formulations produce a more pronounced reduction in circulating androgens and gonadotropins than formulations containing progestins with minimal androgen receptor affinity [86]. Progestin-only methods (the progestin-only pill, the etonogestrel implant, and, to a variable extent depending on formulation, the levonorgestrel intrauterine system and depot medroxyprogesterone acetate) rely more heavily on cervical mucus thickening and endometrial effects, with the degree of ovulation suppression varying by progestin dose and delivery route — depot medroxyprogesterone acetate reliably suppresses the HPG axis and ovulation at the doses used clinically, while lower-dose progestin-only pills suppress ovulation less consistently and depend more on their mucus and endometrial effects for contraceptive efficacy [86].

Because combined hormonal contraceptives are metabolized substantially through hepatic cytochrome P450 pathways (principally CYP3A4), they are subject to clinically significant drug-drug interactions with enzyme-inducing medications (certain anticonvulsants, rifampin, and some antiretrovirals), which can lower circulating contraceptive steroid concentrations below the threshold needed for reliable HPG axis suppression, and, conversely, hormonal contraceptives themselves can alter the metabolism of other hepatically cleared drugs — an interaction profile with direct clinical implications for contraceptive counseling in women taking interacting medications [87].

19. Endometriosis and Adenomyosis: Estrogen-Dependent Disease

Endometriosis, defined by the presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity, is among the clearest clinical illustrations that estrogen action is governed as much by local, tissue-level biochemistry as by circulating hormone concentration [88-91]. Ectopic endometriotic lesions are not passive recipients of circulating ovarian estradiol; they are themselves locally estrogen-synthesizing tissue, aberrantly expressing aromatase (CYP19A1) and other steroidogenic enzymes that are essentially silent in normal, eutopic endometrium, allowing lesions to generate a substantial fraction of their own local estradiol supply largely independent of ovarian cyclicity — a phenomenon termed local intracrinology, directly paralleling the peripheral intracrine steroidogenesis described for DHEA in section 9 [88,89].

Endometriotic lesions also show a markedly altered estrogen receptor profile relative to normal endometrium, with disproportionately elevated estrogen receptor beta (ESR2) relative to estrogen receptor alpha (ESR1); because ESR2 signaling in this context promotes lesion cell proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and a pro-inflammatory local microenvironment (including upregulated cyclooxygenase-2 and prostaglandin E2 production, which in turn further stimulates local aromatase expression in a self-amplifying feed-forward loop), this receptor imbalance is now considered a central, rather than incidental, feature of the disease [88,91]. A second defining biochemical abnormality is progesterone resistance: endometriotic lesion and eutopic endometrial tissue in women with endometriosis show reduced progesterone receptor expression and blunted downstream progesterone signaling relative to disease-free endometrium, which blunts progesterone's normal restraining, anti-proliferative, and anti-inflammatory action on the tissue (the same action described for normal endometrium in section 3.5) and is thought to contribute both to lesion persistence and to the relatively limited and variable clinical efficacy of progestin-based medical therapy in some patients [90].

This estrogen-dependent, progesterone-resistant biochemical phenotype directly explains the pharmacological logic of endometriosis treatment: therapies that lower circulating or locally available estrogen — combined hormonal contraceptives (section 18), progestins (which, despite the tissue-level progesterone resistance described above, still provide clinical benefit in many patients by suppressing ovulation and circulating estradiol), GnRH agonists exploiting the pulsatility-dependent desensitization mechanism described in section 3.1, and aromatase inhibitors that directly block the lesion's own local estradiol synthesis — form the backbone of medical management, while the disease characteristically improves after menopause or surgical removal of ovarian estrogen production, reinforcing its fundamentally estrogen-dependent nature [88-90]. Adenomyosis, the presence of endometrial-like tissue within the myometrium itself, is understood to share substantial biochemical overlap with endometriosis, including local estrogen production and altered steroid receptor signaling, though it is anatomically and, in some respects, mechanistically distinct [88].

20. Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause: Local Estrogen Deficiency

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — the contemporary term encompassing what was previously termed atrophic vaginitis — illustrates the converse clinical scenario from endometriosis: rather than pathological local estrogen excess, GSM reflects the direct local consequences of estrogen withdrawal on estrogen-dependent genitourinary tissue, affecting an estimated 50-70% of postmenopausal women when mild symptoms are included [92,93].

The vaginal epithelium, vulvar tissue, urethra, and bladder trigone all express estrogen receptors and are, throughout reproductive life, maintained in a well-vascularized, glycogen-rich, thickened state by ambient circulating estradiol (section 3.4); vaginal epithelial glycogen, in turn, supports colonization by lactobacilli, which ferment glycogen to lactic acid and thereby maintain the normally acidic vaginal pH that provides a degree of protection against urogenital infection [92]. The estrogen withdrawal of menopause (section 5.3) removes this trophic support: the vaginal and vulvar epithelium thins, vascularity and lubrication decrease, tissue elasticity is lost, glycogen content falls, lactobacilli-dominant flora is replaced by a less protective, higher-pH microbial community, and the urethral and bladder trigone mucosa undergo parallel atrophic change, together producing the syndrome's characteristic combination of vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and urinary symptoms including urgency, frequency, and recurrent urinary tract infection [92,93].

Because GSM reflects local, tissue-level estrogen deficiency rather than a systemic vasomotor or skeletal process, it responds preferentially to low-dose local (vaginal) estrogen therapy — delivered as creams, tablets, or a sustained-release ring — which restores epithelial thickness and vaginal pH within weeks, with maximal benefit typically by approximately 12 weeks, while achieving only minimal systemic estradiol absorption, sufficiently limited that it is generally regarded, and increasingly used in current practice, as an option even in some women for whom systemic hormone therapy carries relative contraindications, such as certain breast cancer survivors, though decisions in that population require individualized discussion with the treating oncology team [92,93]. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which frequently attenuate over several years after menopause as the hypothalamic thermoregulatory system adapts to the new, low-estrogen steady state, GSM is typically progressive rather than self-limited in the absence of treatment, because it reflects a structural, atrophic tissue change rather than a centrally mediated, adaptable neuroendocrine symptom — a distinction with direct implications for counseling women on the expected natural history of each condition [92].

21. Integrative Perspective: Cross-Talk Between Axes Across the Female Lifespan

The preceding sections have, of necessity, treated each hormonal system somewhat separately. But the recurring finding across this literature is that no axis discussed in this review operates in isolation, and several structural features recur across every system that make this crosstalk possible.

First, several axes share literal molecular components. LH, FSH, TSH, and hCG all share an identical alpha subunit, so that hCG's structural similarity to LH allows it to activate the LH receptor and rescue the corpus luteum (section 4.1), while the same structural kinship allows hCG's weak cross-reactivity at the TSH receptor to transiently stimulate the thyroid in early pregnancy (section 6.3) — a single molecular accident of subunit-sharing that produces two, entirely different, physiologically important effects in two different organ systems.

Second, several axes converge on the same upstream neuronal populations. Kisspeptin neurons, the proximate drivers of GnRH pulsatility (section 3.2), are modulated by leptin (linking energy status to reproduction, section 8.2), and GnRH neuronal activity is independently modulated by CRH (linking psychological and physiological stress to reproduction, section 7) — meaning that hypothalamic amenorrhea from caloric restriction, obesity-associated anovulation, and stress-induced menstrual irregularity, though clinically distinct presentations, converge on a shared final common pathway at the level of GnRH pulse generation.

Third, gonadal steroids exert actions well outside the reproductive tract that place them in direct dialogue with nominally unrelated axes: estrogen sensitizes HPA reactivity (section 7.2) and suppresses hepatic GH/IGF-1 signaling (section 11) even as it restrains osteoclastic bone resorption (section 12); androgens dampen HPA reactivity (section 7.2) while serving as obligate aromatization substrate for estrogen synthesis (section 3.4); and progesterone's neuroactive metabolites act directly at GABA-A receptors, giving a reproductive steroid direct access to the same receptor system targeted by benzodiazepine anxiolytics (sections 3.5, 7.3).

Fourth, and most visibly, these interactions are not static but change systematically across the female lifespan, producing several discrete windows of coordinated, multi-axis reorganization: the kisspeptin-leptin-gated awakening of the HPG axis at puberty (section 3.2); the monthly, HPG-HPA-coupled oscillation of the menstrual cycle (sections 3.6, 7.3); the wholesale endocrine handover from ovary to placenta in pregnancy, with attendant reorganization of the HPT axis and maternal metabolism (sections 4, 6.3); the postpartum unmasking of oxytocin-prolactin cooperation and lactational suppression of the HPG axis (section 4.4-4.5); and the perimenopausal-to-postmenopausal transition, in which declining ovarian reserve (section 5.1) simultaneously destabilizes cyclicity, alters HPA and mood regulation, accelerates skeletal loss (section 12), and shifts GH/IGF-1 physiology (section 11) — a single upstream event (follicular depletion) with consequences that radiate through nearly every axis covered in this review. Adrenal DHEA/DHEAS output (section 9), by contrast, follows its own, largely menopause-independent decline ("adrenopause"), a useful reminder that not all of these systems are synchronized to the same biological clock, even though they interact continuously.

22. Clinical Correlations: A Summary by System

The biochemistry reviewed above underlies a substantial share of the conditions encountered in general and reproductive endocrinology practice. Disorders of the HPG axis itself include hypothalamic amenorrhea, premature ovarian insufficiency, and the gonadotropin and steroidogenic disturbances of PCOS (sections 3, 8.3). Pregnancy-specific endocrine pathology includes hyperemesis gravidarum (associated with exuberant hCG production), gestational trophoblastic disease (pathological hCG overproduction), and hypertensive and vascular disorders of pregnancy in which placental hormone secretion, including relaxin, has been investigated as both biomarker and possible mechanistic contributor (sections 4.1-4.3). Menopause-associated conditions include vasomotor symptoms, genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and postmenopausal osteoporosis, each with defined pharmacological management strategies grounded in the mechanisms described in sections 5 and 12. Thyroid disease — both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, and both autoimmune and non-autoimmune in etiology — is markedly more common in women and interacts bidirectionally with reproductive function and pregnancy outcome (section 6). Stress- and mood-related conditions, including premenstrual dysphoric disorder, postpartum depression, and perimenopausal mood instability, are increasingly understood through the lens of differential sensitivity to normal hormonal transitions rather than abnormal hormone concentrations per se, implicating the HPG-HPA crosstalk detailed in section 7. Metabolic-reproductive disease is best exemplified by PCOS, in which insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, and adipokine dysregulation form a self-reinforcing pathological loop (section 8). Androgen-related conditions in women span both excess (hyperandrogenic states, section 8.3) and deficiency (adrenal insufficiency, surgical menopause, and some cases of low sexual desire, section 9). Finally, disorders of growth hormone and skeletal physiology — GH deficiency, acromegaly, and osteoporosis — each show clinically significant, mechanistically grounded sex differences that affect both presentation and treatment dosing (sections 11-12).

23. Conclusion

The female endocrine system is not a single axis with occasional peripheral contributions from other glands; it is a densely interconnected network in which reproductive, metabolic, stress, thyroid, growth, and skeletal signaling pathways share molecular components, converge on common neuronal populations, and shift in a coordinated fashion across a small number of major life transitions — puberty, the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and lactation, and the menopausal transition. Appreciating "a myriad of hormones" as an integrated, cross-talking system, rather than as an inventory of individually acting molecules, is essential both for interpreting the normal physiology reviewed here and for understanding why disruption of one axis in a woman's body so reliably surfaces as a disturbance somewhere else — in the menstrual cycle, in mood, in bone density, or in metabolic health. As the pace of research into kisspeptin biology, adipokine signaling, membrane steroid receptors, and axis-crosstalk mechanisms continues, this integrated framework is likely to become still more detailed, but the core organizing principle documented throughout this review — that female biochemistry is a network, not a list — is already well supported by the current literature and is likely to remain the most useful lens through which to understand it.
