A Review of the Evolutionary, Genetic, and Anthropological Evidence

Abstract

Human scalp hair varies continuously in curvature, cross-sectional shape, diameter, and density across populations of different geographic ancestry, ranging from tightly coiled hair common among populations of sub-Saharan African ancestry, through looser curl and wave patterns common among many populations of European, South Asian, and Oceanian ancestry, to straight hair common among populations of East Asian and Indigenous American ancestry. This variation is not arbitrary. Comparative thermal physiology, biomechanics, and population genetics converge on the conclusion that hair curvature and its associated physical properties evolved, at least in part, as adaptations to the specific climatic regimes that ancestral populations occupied — principally solar heat load, ultraviolet (UV) exposure, and ambient humidity — together with the genetic loci (notably EDAR and TCHH) that have been identified as major contributors to hair-form variation. This review synthesizes the current evidence for these adaptive hypotheses and their genetic basis. It also addresses, and corrects, a common misconception in lay and historical treatments of this subject: that hair morphology sorts neatly into a small number of discrete biological “races.” Human hair-form variation, like the great majority of human biological variation, is clinal and population-level rather than organized into bounded racial categories. Each hair-form phenotype reflects a long history of adaptation well suited to the environment in which the relevant lineage evolved, without implying that any form is objectively superior or that humanity divides into discrete biological types.

1. Introduction

Human head hair is among the most visually salient of our species’ traits, and its variation — in curl pattern, cross-sectional shape, diameter, density, and growth rate — has long attracted both scientific interest and, historically, misuse. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, hair form was pressed into service as a marker in typological race science, used alongside skull measurements and skin color to sort humanity into a small number of ranked, discrete “varieties.” That typological project has been decisively rejected by modern biology and anthropology: human genetic variation is overwhelmingly clinal (it changes gradually across geography) and does not cluster into the bounded, non-overlapping units that the folk concept of “race” presumes (Fuentes et al., 2019). This does not mean, however, that hair-form variation is biologically meaningless. On the contrary, a substantial and growing body of work in evolutionary biology, thermal physiology, dermatology, and genetics shows that hair curvature and its correlated properties are genuinely adaptive traits, shaped by natural selection acting on ancestral populations in response to local climate — in much the same way that skin pigmentation tracks ultraviolet exposure (Jablonski & Chaplin, 2000).

This manuscript reviews that evidence. Its organizing claim is a corrected and more precise version of the premise implicit in much popular discussion of this topic: different hair morphologies are indeed well matched — “beneficial” — to the environments in which the ancestral populations that carry them evolved, but the correct unit of analysis is ancestry and geography, not “race” as a discrete biological category. Each form represents a distinct evolutionary solution to a shared set of physical problems: managing heat gain from solar radiation, protecting the scalp and underlying tissue from UV damage, and conserving water in environments where sweat is the primary cooling mechanism. None of these solutions is superior in an absolute sense; each is a well-adapted response to a particular set of selective pressures.

2. A Note on Terminology: Ancestry, Population, and “Race”

Before reviewing the adaptive evidence, it is necessary to be precise about terminology, because imprecision here is exactly what has historically enabled hair morphology to be misused. Contemporary biological anthropology and human population genetics treat human variation as continuous and clinal: allele frequencies for most traits change gradually across geographic space, and the genetic diversity within any traditionally defined “racial” group is substantially larger than the average difference between groups (Fuentes et al., 2019). “Race” as a folk taxonomy of a handful of discrete, biologically bounded human types is not supported by patterns of genetic variation and is now understood by the relevant scientific societies to be primarily a social and historical classification rather than a biological one.

This is compatible with — indeed, it is the necessary backdrop for — the fact that specific, identifiable traits, including hair curl pattern, do correlate with geographic ancestry and were shaped by natural selection in response to local environments. The scientifically accurate framing is therefore: hair-form variants are adaptations of specific ancestral populations to specific ancestral climates, distributed clinally and admixed extensively through human migration, rather than fixed markers of a small number of discrete races. Throughout this review, terms such as “populations of sub-Saharan African ancestry” or “populations of East Asian ancestry” are used in preference to racial labels, to keep the unit of analysis aligned with what the underlying biology actually shows.

3. Classifying Human Hair Morphology

Hair curvature is a continuous trait, but for descriptive and experimental purposes dermatologists and cosmetic scientists have developed quantitative classification schemes. Loussouarn (2007) introduced a widely used method that characterizes hair curvature using curve diameter, curl index, and number of waves per unit length, and applied it to a large, geographically diverse sample. That work, extended in a 24-population study of hair growth, diameter, and shape across five continents (Loussouarn et al., 2016), confirmed that hair form varies continuously from straight, through wavy and curly, to tightly coiled (sometimes called “coily” or “Afro-textured”) hair, and that populations differ in the central tendency and spread of this distribution rather than falling into sharply separated categories. Tightly coiled hair is, on average, most common among populations of sub-Saharan African ancestry; straight hair is most common among populations of East Asian and Indigenous American ancestry; and wavy-to-curly hair is most common, on average, among many European, Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Oceanian populations — with substantial variation and overlap within every one of these groups.

At the fiber level, curl pattern is produced by asymmetries in the hair follicle and shaft: an asymmetric, curved follicle bulb and non-uniform distribution of cortical cell types across the fiber’s cross-section cause the growing shaft to curve; the degree of curvature scales with the degree of this asymmetry. Straight hair fibers tend to be round in cross-section, while tightly coiled fibers are markedly elliptical and can show internal twisting along the shaft (Loussouarn, 2007; Thibaut et al., 2005 description reviewed in subsequent dermatological literature).

4. Evolutionary and Adaptive Hypotheses

4.1 Thermoregulation and Solar Heat Load

The most direct and recently tested adaptive hypothesis concerns heat exchange between the scalp and the environment. Lasisi et al. (2023), using a sweating thermal manikin fitted with wigs of straight, moderately curled, and tightly curled human hair and tested under simulated solar radiation, found that solar heat gain decreased progressively with increasing curl: the bare (nude) scalp gained the most heat, straight hair intermediate, and tightly curled hair the least. Critically, tightly curled hair achieved this heat-load reduction while requiring substantially less sweat to maintain thermal balance than straight hair or a bare scalp — that is, tightly coiled hair provided better protection from solar heat gain without a compensating increase in water loss through evaporative cooling. The authors interpret this as strong support for the hypothesis that tightly curled scalp hair evolved as a thermoregulatory adaptation in the hot, high-UV environments of early hominin evolution in equatorial Africa, allowing the brain — an organ especially sensitive to overheating — to be shielded from solar heat gain while conserving water, a scarce resource in savanna environments (Lasisi et al., 2023).

This finding also helps explain why humans, alone among primates, evolved long, dense scalp hair in combination with the loss of body hair elsewhere and the gain of eccrine sweat glands: rather than hair being incompatible with effective thermoregulation, curled scalp hair specifically appears to complement it, insulating the head from direct solar heat gain while leaving the rest of the skin free to sweat efficiently (Lasisi et al., 2023).

4.2 Ultraviolet Protection of the Scalp and Cranium

A second, longer-standing hypothesis parallels the well-established account of skin pigmentation: hair density and curl create a physical barrier that reduces the UV radiation dose reaching the scalp, and by extension the underlying dermis and, in infancy, the more UV-sensitive open fontanelles. Jablonski and Chaplin’s (2000) influential model of skin pigmentation evolution established that populations native to regions of high, year-round UV irradiance evolved darker skin to protect against UV-induced folate degradation and skin damage, while populations at higher latitudes with lower UV evolved lighter skin to permit adequate cutaneous vitamin D synthesis. Tightly coiled hair, by trapping air and creating a denser, more voluminous covering over the scalp than straight hair of equivalent length, is thought to provide an analogous, complementary layer of UV and physical protection for populations native to high-UV equatorial environments, consistent with the broader pattern of UV-tracking adaptations identified across human pigmentary and integumentary traits (Jablonski & Chaplin, 2000).

4.3 Water Conservation and Ambient Humidity

Because tightly curled hair reduces the sweat rate needed to offset solar heat gain, as demonstrated experimentally by Lasisi et al. (2023), it is also plausible that this trait carried a water-conservation advantage in arid and semi-arid ancestral environments where free water was not always readily available. Conversely, in humid tropical and temperate environments, evaporative cooling from the scalp is less constrained by water scarcity, which may have reduced the selective pressure toward the tightest curl patterns in some humid-zone populations while other selective forces (UV protection, solar heat load) remained relevant.

4.4 Sebum Distribution and Insulation in Temperate and Cold, Dry Climates

Sebum, the oily secretion of the scalp’s sebaceous glands, travels along the hair shaft primarily through gravity and capillary action and coats the fiber, providing lubrication, a degree of water repellency, and a barrier against heat loss. Sebum spreads far more readily along a straight fiber than along a fiber with many tight curves, where each bend interrupts the film of sebum moving down the shaft. This has led researchers to propose that straight hair, more efficiently lubricated and closer to the scalp, may have offered an advantage in colder, drier, and lower-UV environments — such as those occupied by ancestral populations in northern East Asia and, following the peopling of the Americas, the Americas — by keeping the scalp and hair better insulated and less prone to UV-related damage risk was not the dominant pressure there (this hypothesis is discussed alongside the genetic evidence for EDAR selection below; see Kamberov et al., 2013, and related literature on EDAR pleiotropy).

5. Genetic Architecture of Hair Form

Genome-wide and candidate-gene studies over the past two decades have identified specific loci that account for a substantial share of population-level variation in hair form, several of which show strong signatures of recent positive selection — direct molecular evidence that hair form has been a target of natural selection rather than a neutral, incidental trait.

5.1 EDAR and the East Asian / Indigenous American Hair Form

The ectodysplasin A receptor gene (EDAR) carries one of the best-documented examples of recent, strong positive selection in the human genome. A derived variant, EDAR370A (Val370Ala), arose in central China an estimated 15,000–30,000 years ago and rose to high frequency in East Asian and, via the peopling of the Americas, Indigenous American populations, but is rare or absent elsewhere (Fujimoto et al., 2008; Bryk et al., 2008). Fujimoto et al. (2008) first identified EDAR as a major determinant of Asian hair thickness in a genome-wide scan, and Tan et al. (2013) subsequently showed that the same variant, EDARV370A, is independently associated with straighter hair. Functional work using mice engineered to carry the human variant demonstrated that it increases hair-follicle density, alters hair-shaft thickness, and increases eccrine (sweat) gland density — a striking example of one mutation producing multiple, seemingly coordinated adaptive effects (pleiotropy), several of which plausibly relate to thermoregulation and moisture management in the environments where the variant rose to prevalence (Kamberov et al., 2013).

5.2 Trichohyalin (TCHH) and European Straight Hair

A separate genetic pathway contributes to straight hair in populations of European ancestry. Medland et al. (2009), in a genome-wide association study of Australian and Dutch twin cohorts, identified common variants in the trichohyalin gene (TCHH) — which encodes a structural protein of the inner root sheath of the hair follicle — as strongly associated with straight hair in Europeans, independent of the East Asian EDAR variant. This indicates that straight hair has arisen at least twice through different genetic routes in different populations, itself evidence against any simple single-origin, racial-typological model of hair-form variation.

5.3 FGFR2 and Other Modifying Loci

Additional loci, including fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2), have been associated with hair thickness and curl-related traits in Asian and other populations (see review in gene-mapping studies following Fujimoto et al., 2008), and ongoing genome-wide association work continues to identify additional modifiers of hair diameter, density, and curvature. The overall picture from this genetic literature is one of multiple, partially independent loci, each under some degree of local selection, jointly producing the continuous, population-graded distribution of hair morphology described in Section 3 — not a single “race gene” for hair type.

6. Geographic and Clinal Distribution of Hair Form

Consistent with the genetic evidence, large-scale phenotypic surveys show hair form distributed clinally rather than categorically. Loussouarn et al.’s (2016) 24-population study found continuous gradients and substantial within-population variance in curl index, diameter, and growth rate, with populations of mixed or intermediate ancestry (for example, in North and East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and admixed American populations) showing correspondingly intermediate average hair forms rather than falling into one of a small number of discrete bins. This is precisely the pattern predicted if hair-form alleles are distributed clinally and have been extensively admixed through human migration, and it is difficult to reconcile with a strict racial-typological account in which each “race” possesses one fixed, diagnostic hair type.

7. Why “Race” Is the Wrong Unit of Analysis, and What the Right Unit Shows

Bringing the evolutionary, genetic, and phenotypic evidence together yields a picture that is both more interesting and more scientifically defensible than a race-based account. Hair curvature is a genuinely adaptive trait: tightly coiled hair appears to have evolved in ancestral populations of equatorial Africa as a highly effective, water-conserving defense against solar heat gain and UV exposure (Lasisi et al., 2023; Jablonski & Chaplin, 2000); straight hair appears to have arisen independently at least twice — via EDAR in East Asian and Indigenous American lineages and via TCHH in European lineages — in colder, lower-UV environments where efficient sebum distribution and close scalp insulation may have been favored (Fujimoto et al., 2008; Medland et al., 2009; Kamberov et al., 2013); and the full continuum of wavy and looser curl forms found across much of the rest of the world reflects intermediate selective histories and extensive population admixture (Loussouarn et al., 2016).

What this evidence does not show is that humanity divides into a small number of discrete biological “races,” each with one hair type that is simply “theirs.” It shows something more precise: specific, identifiable ancestral populations, defined by geography and history rather than by racial category, evolved hair-form variants well matched to their local climate, through several independent genetic mechanisms, and those variants are distributed clinally and have mixed extensively wherever populations have come into contact. The popular intuition that hair type is “particularly beneficial” to the population that carries it is, in this specific and narrow sense, well supported by the science — but the science locates the explanation in climate adaptation and population genetics, not in racial essence, and it offers no basis for ranking one hair form as generally superior to another (Fuentes et al., 2019).

8. Conclusion

Human hair morphology is a genuine product of natural selection, shaped over tens of thousands of years by the specific thermal, UV, and hydric demands of the environments ancestral human populations occupied. Tightly coiled, curly, wavy, and straight hair each represent well-adapted solutions to those demands, arising through at least two independent genetic pathways (EDAR and TCHH) and distributed clinally across human populations rather than sorted into a small number of fixed racial types. A scientifically accurate account of “why different hair types benefit the populations that carry them” is therefore available, and it is a genuinely interesting story about climate adaptation and molecular evolution — but it is a story about ancestry, geography, and gene-environment interaction, not about race as a biological category, and it carries no implication that any human hair type, or any human population, is superior to another.
