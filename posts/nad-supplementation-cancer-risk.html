Abstract

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) precursors- nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), nicotinamide riboside (NR), and nicotinamide (NAM) itself- sit alongside rapamycin and senolytics at the center of the emerging longevity medicine movement, marketed to restore a coenzyme that declines with age and to improve markers of metabolic and mitochondrial health. Oncologists and oncology patients are not exempt from this trend: cancer survivors and even patients undergoing active treatment report using NAD⁺-boosting supplements, often without informing their care team. This review examines the evidence for and against NAD⁺ precursor supplementation through an oncologic lens. Human trials in non-cancer populations support the tolerability of NMN and NR at commonly marketed doses, but these trials were neither designed nor powered to detect oncologic harm. A substantial preclinical literature demonstrates that NAD⁺ metabolism, and specifically the salvage-pathway enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), supports cancer cell proliferation, invasive phenotype switching, and immune evasion, while a 2026 study directly demonstrated that NMN, NR, and NAM protect pancreatic cancer cells from oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and gemcitabine in vitro and in vivo. Set against this, a landmark randomized trial found that pharmacologic-dose nicotinamide reduces new skin cancers in high-risk patients, illustrating that the same molecule can be protective or permissive depending on dose, tissue, disease stage, and whether the goal is preventing a first malignancy or treating an established one. Because fewer than one-third of patients disclose supplement use to their oncologist, the practical harm of this evidence gap may already be occurring silently. This review concludes that NAD⁺ precursor supplementation should be treated as contraindicated or, at minimum, flagged for individualized risk-benefit discussion in patients with active or recently treated malignancy, that routine supplement screening belongs in every oncology intake, and that prospective clinical trials in cancer populations are urgently needed before this pillar of longevity medicine can be safely recommended to survivors.

Keywords: Longevity Medicine, NAD+, nicotinamide mononucleotide, nicotinamide riboside, nicotinamide, NAMPT, oncology, cancer survivorship, chemotherapy resistance, anti-aging supplements

Introduction and Background

The pursuit of a longer, healthier life has moved from folklore to a formal clinical and commercial field. Longevity medicine now spans two overlapping tracks: the incremental, well-validated advances of mainstream clinical medicine, and a faster-moving frontier of rapamycin, senolytics, peptides, and NAD⁺-boosting compounds sold to slow or reverse biological aging. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) occupies an unusually prominent place on that frontier. It is a coenzyme required for glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid oxidation, and it is consumed by sirtuins, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs), and the ectoenzyme CD38, linking cellular energy metabolism directly to DNA repair, chromatin regulation, and immune signaling [1,2]. Because tissue NAD⁺ levels fall with age, largely through CD38-driven consumption, restoring the NAD⁺ pool has become one of the most heavily marketed anti-aging interventions, sold over the counter as nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) [3].

Oncology cannot treat this trend as someone else's problem. Cancer survivors are, almost by definition, longevity-medicine consumers: having already confronted mortality, many actively seek interventions promising to extend healthspan, and NAD⁺ precursors are inexpensive, unregulated as drugs, and widely available online and in retail stores. Yet the same NAD⁺ metabolism being marketed as protective is also one of the most extensively studied metabolic dependencies of cancer cells. This review examines that tension directly: what is actually known about NAD⁺ precursor safety in humans, what the cancer biology literature says about NAD⁺ metabolism in malignancy, what new direct evidence exists connecting NAD⁺ supplementation to chemotherapy resistance, and what a counterexample from skin-cancer chemoprevention teaches about when NAD⁺ pathway modulation helps versus harms. It closes with practical guidance for counseling patients and identifies where the evidence base most urgently needs to grow.

Review

NAD⁺ Biology and the Rationale for Supplementation

NAD⁺ exists in a constant cycle of consumption and resynthesis. It can be synthesized de novo from tryptophan or, more efficiently, salvaged from dietary and endogenous precursors: nicotinamide, nicotinic acid, nicotinamide riboside, and nicotinamide mononucleotide, through a pathway centered on the rate-limiting enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), which converts nicotinamide to NMN before its final conversion to NAD⁺ [1,2]. Sirtuins (NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases implicated in metabolic regulation and genomic stability), PARPs (activated by DNA damage to orchestrate repair), and CD38 (a major NAD⁺-consuming ectoenzyme expressed on immune cells) all compete for this same finite pool [2,3]. Camacho-Pereira et al. demonstrated that CD38 expression rises with age and is a principal driver of the age-related decline in tissue NAD⁺, providing much of the mechanistic rationale for NAD⁺ repletion as an anti-aging strategy [3].

This biology underlies the appeal of NMN and NR: both bypass rate-limiting steps of the salvage pathway to raise cellular NAD⁺ more efficiently than nicotinamide alone, and preclinical rodent studies summarized by Rajman et al. (2018) link NAD⁺ repletion to improved mitochondrial function, insulin sensitivity, and stress resistance across multiple organ systems [2]. It is this same repletion logic, however, that raises the oncologic concern explored in the sections that follow: a pathway that supports mitochondrial and DNA-repair capacity in aging normal tissue may, in a tumor or its microenvironment, support the same functions in malignant cells.

Clinical Safety Evidence for NAD⁺ Precursors in Non-Oncologic Populations

The human evidence base for NMN and NR is real but narrow. Martens et al. (2018) conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of NR (up to 1,000 mg/day for six weeks) in healthy middle-aged and older adults and found it well tolerated, with elevated blood NAD⁺ metabolome but no significant effect on blood pressure, arterial stiffness, or insulin sensitivity [4]. Dollerup et al. (2018) similarly found NR safe and tolerable in obese, insulin-resistant men over 12 weeks, again without a clinically meaningful metabolic benefit in that trial [5]. Irie et al. (2020) established single- and multiple-dose safety of oral NMN (up to 500 mg) in healthy Japanese men over four weeks, with mild elevations in some laboratory parameters but no serious adverse events [6]. In a more clinically striking result, Yoshino et al. (2021) reported that 250 mg/day of NMN for 10 weeks improved skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity in prediabetic postmenopausal women, one of the few trials to show a functional metabolic benefit rather than tolerability alone [7]. Even at doses well above typical retail supplements, Brakedal et al. (2022) NADPARK study of 1,000 mg/day NR in Parkinson disease patients over 30 days found the intervention safe, raised brain NAD⁺ metabolism measurably, and produced modest clinical improvement, though a signal toward accelerated non-motor symptom progression in a subgroup underscored that “safe” and “inert” are not synonyms [8].

Two limitations of this literature matter enormously for oncology. First, every one of these trials excluded patients with active or recent malignancy, so “safe” here means safe in the population studied, not safe in a patient undergoing chemotherapy or radiation. Second, none was designed or powered to detect an oncologic signal such as tumor growth, recurrence, or treatment resistance; the endpoints were metabolic and functional, observed over weeks to a few months, far short of the time horizon over which a cancer-promoting effect would plausibly manifest. Extrapolating these reassuring safety profiles to oncology patients is therefore an evidence gap, not a validated inference.

Mechanistic Concerns: NAD⁺ Metabolism in Cancer Biology

Independent of the supplement-safety literature, NAD⁺ metabolism has been studied in oncology for over a decade as a therapeutic vulnerability, precisely because malignant cells are often more NAD⁺-dependent than normal tissue. Chiarugi et al. (2012) in an early and influential review, described the “NAD metabolome” as a key determinant of cancer cell biology: elevated NAD⁺ turnover supports the aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) that fuels rapid proliferation, and many tumor types overexpress NAMPT, the same enzyme NMN and NR are designed to feed into [9]. Yaku et al. (2018) extended this point directly, noting that increased NAD⁺ availability enhances glycolytic flux and fuels cancer cells, and that NAMPT inhibitors (such as FK866) have been developed as anticancer agents specifically to deplete tumor NAD⁺ pools, though clinical development has been limited by hematologic toxicity in normal tissue that shares the same dependency [10]. Navas and Carnero's review reinforces this dual identity of NAD⁺ metabolism, describing how NAMPT upregulation supports cancer stem cell properties through activation of pluripotency pathways, in addition to its roles in immune regulation and DNA repair; the very features that make NAD⁺ boosting attractive for healthy aging are, in a tumor, the features that support stemness and persistence [11].

Two more specific mechanistic threads sharpen the concern. First, in melanoma, Ohanna et al. (2018) showed that NAMPT drives the switch of melanoma cells toward an invasive, drug-resistant phenotype, positioning NAD⁺ salvage-pathway activity as a direct contributor to metastatic progression and therapy resistance rather than merely a bystander metabolic effect [12]. Second, in the immune compartment, Chen et al. (2018) demonstrated that CD38, the same enzyme that consumes NAD⁺ and declines in activity with successful anti-aging repletion, is upregulated on tumor cells and myeloid cells as a mechanism of acquired resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade, with CD38 blockade restoring anti-tumor immunity in preclinical models [13]. Because CD38 itself consumes NAD⁺, the net effect of exogenous NAD⁺ precursor supplementation on this immunosuppressive axis in an actual tumor microenvironment is not straightforward to predict from either the aging literature or the immuno-oncology literature in isolation, and it has not, to date, been directly tested in patients receiving checkpoint inhibitors.

New Direct Evidence: NAD⁺ Precursors and Chemotherapy Resistance in Pancreatic Cancer

Until recently, the connection between NAD⁺ supplementation and clinical cancer risk was inferred from separate bodies of aging and oncology literature rather than demonstrated directly. That changed with a 2026 study from Case Western Reserve University, in which Nakazzi et al. (2026) tested NMN, NR, and NAM directly against pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell lines and mouse models treated with three chemotherapy backbones used in pancreatic cancer: oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and gemcitabine [14]. All three NAD⁺ precursors protected pancreatic cancer cells from chemotherapy-induced death, through three converging mechanisms: enhanced tumor cell energy production that increased resilience to metabolic stress, reduced oxidative stress that blunted a primary mechanism by which these chemotherapy agents kill cancer cells, and suppressed DNA damage signaling that limited the apoptotic response chemotherapy is intended to trigger. NMN produced the most pronounced protective effect among the three compounds tested.

This study has important limitations that should temper, without dismissing, its clinical implications. It is preclinical, conducted in cell lines and murine xenograft or genetically engineered mouse models rather than in patients, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma is a single, particularly NAD⁺-avid tumor type that may not generalize to all malignancies. The doses of NAD⁺ precursors used in vitro and in vivo may also exceed typical human supplement exposure. Even with these caveats, the study is significant because it closes an inferential gap: rather than relying on separate lines of evidence about NAMPT biology and aging supplementation safety, it directly tested the interaction between a commercially available longevity supplement and the chemotherapy regimens patients are actually receiving, and found a consistent, mechanistically coherent, and clinically concerning signal. The study's senior author was explicit about the clinical stakes, describing the findings as highlighting a potentially concerning role for NAD⁺-boosting supplements in the context of active cancer treatment and calling for improved screening of supplement use and further clinical investigation of these interactions [14].

A Counterexample: Nicotinamide as Skin-Cancer Chemoprevention

Any critical review of this topic must resist the temptation to flatten “NAD⁺ boosting” into a single uniformly hazardous exposure, because the best human oncology evidence on this coenzyme pathway points the opposite direction in a specific, well-studied context. In the ONTRAC trial, Chen et al. randomized 386 patients with a history of at least two nonmelanoma skin cancers in the preceding five years to oral nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily or placebo for 12 months, and found a 23% reduction in new nonmelanoma skin cancers, including statistically significant reductions in both basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma, with no meaningful difference in adverse events between groups [15]. Nicotinamide is itself an NAD⁺ precursor, feeding the same salvage pathway as NMN and NR, yet here it reduced rather than promoted carcinogenesis.

Reconciling this with the pancreatic cancer findings requires attention to context rather than treating the two studies as contradictory. The ONTRAC trial enrolled immunocompetent patients without an existing malignancy, using nicotinamide to prevent the first oncogenic transformation in UV-damaged skin; the proposed mechanism is that nicotinamide restores cellular ATP and NAD⁺ needed for nucleotide excision repair of UV-induced DNA damage and mitigates UV-induced immunosuppression, functioning as a DNA-repair enhancer in normal, damaged-but-not-yet-malignant keratinocytes [15]. The pancreatic cancer study, by contrast, examined the effect of NAD⁺ precursors on cells that had already undergone malignant transformation and were undergoing cytotoxic chemotherapy, where the same restored energetic and DNA-repair capacity instead protected established cancer cells from treatment-induced death [14]. The lesson for longevity medicine is that the clinical effect of NAD⁺ pathway modulation is not a fixed property of the molecule but depends on disease stage (prevention in healthy tissue versus treatment of an established tumor), tissue type, dose, and formulation; a distinction that a “longevity supplement” marketed with a single blanket safety claim cannot capture, and one that oncologists are well positioned to make explicit to patients.

The Disclosure Gap: Why Oncologists Need to Ask

The clinical relevance of this uncertainty is amplified by how commonly cancer patients use dietary supplements without informing their treatment team. Tank et al. (2021) surveyed ambulatory cancer patients and found dietary supplement use to be common, driven substantially by a desire to “do something” for their own prognosis and general health, with a meaningful proportion of patients not discussing this use with their oncologist [16]. NAD⁺ precursors are marketed directly to consumers as longevity and vitality products, sold without a prescription, and are unlikely to be volunteered by a patient who does not think of them as a “medication” at all. Given the preclinical signal for chemotherapy protection described above and the mechanistic plausibility from cancer biology more broadly, an oncologist who does not specifically ask about NAD⁺, NMN, NR, or “anti-aging” supplement use may be treating a patient whose tumor is being pharmacologically shielded from the very chemotherapy being prescribed, without either party being aware of the interaction.

Recommendations for Clinical Practice and Future Research

Pending prospective clinical data, a precautionary approach is warranted. Supplement history, including specific questions about NAD⁺, NMN, NR, and nicotinamide-containing longevity products, should be a standard component of oncology intake and reassessed at each treatment cycle, not deferred to a general “are you taking any supplements” checkbox that patients may not connect to over-the-counter longevity products. Patients with active malignancy, and particularly those receiving chemotherapy regimens that act through oxidative stress or DNA damage (such as platinum agents, fluoropyrimidines, and gemcitabine), should be counseled that NAD⁺ precursor supplementation is not established as safe during treatment and, based on the mechanistic and preclinical evidence reviewed here, plausibly reduces treatment efficacy; the discussion should distinguish this from nicotinamide's separate, trial-validated role in skin-cancer chemoprevention, since patients and even clinicians may otherwise conflate the two indications. For cancer survivors without evidence of disease who are considering NAD⁺ precursors for general longevity purposes, the honest answer is that the safety data, while reassuring for tolerability, was not designed to detect an effect on micrometastatic disease or recurrence risk, and this uncertainty should be communicated rather than resolved by default reassurance in either direction.

The research agenda that would resolve this uncertainty is well defined even if it does not yet exist: pharmacokinetic and safety studies of NMN and NR specifically in patients with active malignancy and in survivors, ideally stratified by tumor NAMPT expression given its mechanistic centrality across the reviewed studies; correlative analysis of NAD⁺ precursor use and outcomes within existing cancer survivorship cohorts as a lower-cost first step; and mechanistic work clarifying how CD38-mediated immune effects of NAD⁺ modulation interact with checkpoint inhibitor therapy, given the direct evidence that CD38 mediates resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Until such data exist, guidance in this space should be explicit that it rests on preclinical and mechanistic evidence extrapolated to a clinical question, not on a completed clinical trial in cancer patients.

Conclusions

NAD⁺ precursor supplementation exemplifies the central tension of longevity medicine: an intervention with a genuine, mechanistically grounded rationale for extending healthy aging can, in the specific context of an established malignancy, plausibly extend the tumor's survival rather than the patient's. Human safety data support NMN and NR tolerability in healthy and non-oncologic populations, but that reassurance does not extend to patients with active cancer, where preclinical and mechanistic evidence- from NAMPT-driven proliferation and invasion to CD38-mediated immune evasion to direct 2026 evidence of chemotherapy protection in pancreatic cancer models- raises a specific and testable concern. The nicotinamide skin-cancer chemoprevention trial shows this is not a blanket indictment of the NAD⁺ pathway, but rather a reminder that context, dose, and disease stage determine whether NAD⁺ pathway modulation helps or harms. Given how rarely patients disclose supplement use, oncologists should actively screen for NAD⁺ precursor use, counsel accordingly, and advocate for the prospective clinical trials needed to replace mechanistic inference with clinical evidence.
