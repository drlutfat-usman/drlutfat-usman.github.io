Nigeria and Brazil agreed in August 2025 to cooperate in science, technology and innovation (STI). This brief proposes how to make that agreement produce real research over the next three years, largely by connecting funding schemes and fellowships the two countries already run.

Audience: Nigeria's Federal Ministry of Innovation, Science and Technology (FMIST); Brazil's Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation (MCTI); the foreign ministries and embassies of both countries.

Keywords: science diplomacy; South–South cooperation; research funding; researcher mobility; Nigeria; Brazil.

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23900622

Executive summary

Nigeria and Brazil signed a memorandum of understanding (MoU) on STI on 25 August 2025 [1, 2]. Public accounts of the agreement list its priority areas, but say nothing about who will pay for the work, who will manage it, or how progress will be judged. Unless those questions are answered, the MoU could go the way of the two countries' Strategic Dialogue Mechanism, which met for its second session in June 2025 after a break of more than ten years [6, 7].

Much of what is needed already exists. In May 2026 Brazil's National Council for Scientific and Technological Development (CNPq) opened a R$25 million call under its ProÁfrica programme [12]. Nigeria's Tertiary Education Trust Fund (TETFund) awarded N7.5 billion to 174 research teams in the 2025 cycle of its National Research Fund [13]. Connecting these schemes would be quicker and cheaper than designing new ones.

This brief recommends five actions:

  1. Establish a Joint STI Working Group with a two-country secretariat within six months.
  2. Open a Nigerian co-funding window in the National Research Fund that matches ProÁfrica projects with Nigerian partners, each country funding its own researchers.
  3. Fill existing fellowship routes, starting with the TWAS–FAPESP postdoctoral call (full proposals due 1 April 2027) [14].
  4. Designate a science focal point in each country's embassy.
  5. Report annually against a public results framework, beginning with a co-authorship baseline.

1. Context: the 2025 cooperation framework

The STI MoU was signed in Brasília by Nigeria's Minister of Innovation, Science and Technology, Uche Geoffrey Nnaji, and Brazil's Minister of Science, Technology and Innovation, Luciana Santos, with Presidents Tinubu and Lula as witnesses [1, 2]. It covers biotechnology, the bioeconomy, ocean science, energy, space, digital transformation and new materials [2].

The MoU was one of several agreements reached in 2025, and together they shape what it can realistically achieve.

2. Problem statement

The two research systems differ greatly in size, and so far nothing has been set up to turn the political commitment into research on the ground.

Relative to the size of its economy, Nigeria spends about a third of what the African Union commitment calls for, and it has roughly one researcher for every forty in Brazil, per head of population. This gap is the main reason the partnership is worth pursuing. It also means that joint projects cannot assume Nigerian teams start with the same equipment and staffing as their Brazilian partners; building that capacity has to be part of the design.

2.2 Implementation gap

Four gaps stand out.

First, no body appears to be responsible for delivery. Nigeria's science minister proposed a Nigeria–Brazil Joint Innovation and Technology Working Group and a Science and Innovation Dialogue [8], but no public record could be found that either has met.

Second, routes for researcher mobility are fragile. The TWAS–CNPq programme, one of the main ways for students from developing countries to take a PhD in Brazil, did not open a call in 2026 [15].

Third, Nigeria's diplomatic missions have little dedicated science capacity. Its embassy in Washington, for instance, lists no science counsellor among its officers [19], and opportunities in host countries are easily missed as a result.

Finally, there is no agreed way to measure progress. No published baseline of Nigeria–Brazil co-authored papers could be found, so in three years' time it would be difficult to show whether the MoU has made any difference.

3. Evidence and lessons

3.1 Analytical framework

The Royal Society and the American Association for the Advancement of Science distinguish three dimensions of science diplomacy: science in diplomacy (scientific advice informing foreign policy), diplomacy for science (facilitating international research cooperation) and science for diplomacy (using scientific cooperation to improve relations) [11]. The 2025 MoU is primarily an instrument of diplomacy for science. It should therefore be judged by the research it produces and the capacity it builds, rather than by the number of documents signed.

3.2 Lessons from Brazil's cooperation in Africa

Brazil's earlier cooperation in Africa offers two contrasting lessons.

The Cotton-4 project, run since 2009 by the Brazilian Cooperation Agency (ABC) and EMBRAPA with Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, trained 425 African researchers, and yields on its experimental fields were reported to have roughly quadrupled [16]. It suggests that patient, long-term technical work built around training can pay off.

ProSAVANA in Mozambique shows the other side. This Brazil–Japan–Mozambique programme, inspired by an earlier Japan–Brazil programme in Brazil's Cerrado, met strong opposition from civil society over land rights, and its plans changed substantially before implementation [17]. Agricultural projects under the new MoU should therefore consult affected communities early and test whether Brazilian practices suit local conditions before scaling them up.

Some Brazilian technologies are also inexpensive to trial. Brazilian law has recognised crushed-rock soil remineralisers as an agricultural input since 2013 (Law 12.890) [20], and joint field trials on Nigerian soils would be a modest, low-risk place to start.

3.4 Joint funding models

International joint calls commonly use one of two models [18]. In a real common pot, funders pay into a central account that distributes grants. In a virtual common pot, each national funder pays its own researchers within jointly selected projects. The virtual model suits this partnership: it avoids cross-border transfers and currency exposure, and each agency keeps its own rules. Its main weakness is that a delay on one side can stall the whole project, which synchronised calendars can reduce.

6. Implementation roadmap

The plan runs over three years in three phases. Each phase should begin only after the preceding gate has been passed.

Gate 2 is the decision point. If the first annual report shows projects on track, the partners open a second joint call; if not, the Working Group revises the design before committing further funds.

9. Conclusion

The 2025 agreement is a good start, but on its own it will not produce research. Most of the pieces are already in place: Brazil's ProÁfrica call, Nigeria's National Research Fund, international fellowships and a direct flight between Lagos and São Paulo. What has been missing is someone to connect them, a modest amount of matched funding, and a public record of progress. None of this is expensive, and the first steps could be taken within the 2027 budget year.
