A Comprehensive Review

Abstract

Water is the medium, the reactant, the structural scaffold, and in many respects the co-author of life's chemistry. Despite its apparent simplicity — two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen — water exhibits a set of anomalous physical and chemical properties that arise from its capacity to form a dynamic, three-dimensional hydrogen-bonding network. This review synthesizes current understanding of the biochemistry of water across scales, from the quantum mechanical origins of the hydrogen bond to the organismal physiology of fluid balance. We examine the anomalous properties of bulk water and their molecular origins; the thermodynamics of hydration, including the hydrophobic and hydrophilic effects that drive protein folding, membrane self-assembly, and molecular recognition; the structural and dynamical roles of water in stabilizing proteins, nucleic acids, and lipid membranes; the specialized machinery — aquaporins — that cells use to move water across membranes; the contested question of whether intracellular water differs fundamentally from bulk water; the concept of water activity and its role in microbial ecology and food preservation; the remarkable strategies of anhydrobiotic organisms that survive near-complete desiccation; the physiology of water homeostasis in humans and its clinical relevance in aging and disease; and the biophysical and computational methods used to interrogate water's behavior in biological contexts. We also critically evaluate the controversial claims surrounding "exclusion zone" or "fourth phase" water, situating them against the weight of mainstream physical chemistry. Throughout, we emphasize that water is not a passive solvent but an active, structurally and energetically indispensable participant in the molecular events that constitute life.

Keywords: water, hydrogen bonding, hydration, hydrophobic effect, protein folding, aquaporin, osmoregulation, water activity, anhydrobiosis, biophysics

1. Introduction

Approximately 60–70% of the mass of a typical mammalian cell is water, and in some tissues — the vitreous humor of the eye, for instance — the fraction exceeds 98% (Guyton & Hall, 2020). It is tempting, given these numbers, to think of water as a passive backdrop against which the "real" chemistry of life — the folding of proteins, the replication of DNA, the transport of ions — takes place. This view is incorrect, and the purpose of this review is to make the case, drawing on more than half a century of biophysical, biochemical, and physiological research, that water is an active participant in nearly every molecular process biologists study.

The case for water's active role rests on several pillars. First, water's own physical behavior is anomalous in ways that matter enormously for biology: its high boiling point relative to molecules of similar mass, its density maximum at 4°C, its high heat capacity and heat of vaporization, and its unusually high surface tension all trace back to its capacity to form transient, cooperative hydrogen bonds (Nilsson & Pettersson, 2015; Kontogeorgis et al., 2022). These properties buffer organisms against thermal extremes, permit ice to float and thereby protect aquatic life, and make water an exceptional solvent for polar and ionic species.

Second, the thermodynamics of how water rearranges itself around solutes — the so-called hydrophobic and hydrophilic effects — are now understood to be primary driving forces behind protein folding, membrane self-assembly, and the specificity of molecular recognition (Chandler, 2005; Tanford, 1980). A protein does not fold merely because its backbone finds a low-energy conformation; it folds because burying nonpolar side chains away from water is thermodynamically favorable, releasing ordered water molecules back into the bulk phase and increasing the entropy of the system as a whole (Kauzmann, 1959).

Third, water is not merely a passive medium around biomolecules but forms structured, kinetically distinct hydration shells whose properties differ measurably — though modestly and controversially in degree — from bulk water (Bagchi, 2005; Halle, 2004; Levy & Onuchic, 2006). These hydration layers participate directly in protein dynamics, ligand binding, enzymatic catalysis, and the stability of the DNA double helix (Otting et al., 1991; Saenger, 1987).

Fourth, cells have evolved dedicated molecular machinery — aquaporin water channels — to control the flux of water across membranes with extraordinary speed and selectivity (Agre, 2004; Preston et al., 1992), and multicellular organisms have evolved elaborate hormonal systems, centered on vasopressin, to regulate whole-body water balance (Bankir et al., 2017).

Fifth, the amount of water present, and more specifically the amount of water thermodynamically "available" rather than bound to solutes — water activity — governs whether microorganisms can grow in a given environment, a principle that underlies food preservation techniques developed over millennia and formalized biochemically only in the twentieth century (Scott, 1957; Tapia et al., 2020).

Sixth, some organisms have evolved the capacity to survive the near-total loss of cellular water through anhydrobiosis, deploying molecules such as trehalose and intrinsically disordered proteins to vitrify their cytoplasm into a stable glassy state that protects macromolecular structure in the absence of the solvent that normally stabilizes it (Crowe et al., 1998; Hengherr et al., 2008).

Finally, precisely because water is so central to biology, it has also been a magnet for speculative and, in some cases, pseudoscientific claims — most prominently the assertion that water adjacent to hydrophilic surfaces organizes into an ordered "fourth phase" with exotic electrical and structural properties distinct from three-dimensional ice, liquid water, or vapor (Pollack, 2013). We treat this literature critically in a dedicated section, distinguishing the reproducible core observation (an interfacial exclusion zone for certain colloidal particles) from the more expansive theoretical claims built upon it, which have not withstood scrutiny from the broader physical chemistry community (Elton et al., 2020; Elton & Spencer, 2021).

This review is organized to move from the molecular to the organismal. We begin with the physical chemistry of the isolated water molecule and the hydrogen-bonded network of bulk liquid water (Sections 2–3), proceed to the thermodynamics of hydration that link bulk water behavior to biomolecular structure (Section 4), examine water's structural and functional roles in proteins, enzymes, nucleic acids, and membranes (Sections 5–9), ascend to the cellular and organismal levels to discuss osmoregulation, the intracellular water debate, water activity, anhydrobiosis, and human physiology (Sections 10–14), and close with a survey of the experimental and computational tools used to study biological water and a critical appraisal of controversial claims in the field (Sections 15–17).

2. Physical and Chemical Properties of the Water Molecule

2.1 Molecular Geometry and Electronic Structure

A single water molecule, H₂O, consists of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms. The molecule is bent rather than linear, with an experimentally determined H–O–H bond angle of approximately 104.5° in the gas phase — close to, but measurably smaller than, the idealized tetrahedral angle of 109.5° predicted by simple valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) reasoning. The oxygen atom retains two lone pairs of electrons, and the combination of two bonding pairs and two lone pairs around the oxygen center gives the molecule its characteristic bent geometry and, critically, an asymmetric distribution of electron density.

Oxygen is considerably more electronegative than hydrogen (3.44 versus 2.20 on the Pauling scale), so the shared electrons in each O–H bond are pulled preferentially toward the oxygen nucleus. This produces a substantial bond dipole along each O–H axis; because the molecule is bent rather than linear, these two bond dipoles do not cancel but instead sum to a net molecular dipole moment of approximately 1.85 debye in the gas phase — one of the largest dipole moments of any small, neutral molecule. In the condensed (liquid) phase, cooperative polarization effects from hydrogen bonding raise the effective dipole moment further, to an estimated 2.9–3.0 debye, a fact of considerable importance for water's dielectric behavior (Gerstein & Levitt, 1998).

This combination of a permanent dipole, two hydrogen-bond-donating O–H groups, and two hydrogen-bond-accepting lone pairs, is the single most important fact in the biochemistry of water. It means that in principle, and very nearly in practice, every water molecule in the liquid can simultaneously donate two hydrogen bonds and accept two hydrogen bonds, giving rise to the potential for a fully connected, three-dimensional, tetrahedrally coordinated hydrogen-bond network — a geometry water shares with, and indeed borrows conceptually from, the crystal structure of ordinary ice (ice Ih).

2.2 The Hydrogen Bond

A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to an electronegative donor atom (here, oxygen) is attracted to the lone pair electrons of an electronegative acceptor atom on a neighboring molecule (again, oxygen, in pure water). The O–H···O hydrogen bond in water has a strength of roughly 5 kcal/mol (about 21 kJ/mol) — intermediate between the weak van der Waals interactions (< 1 kcal/mol) that dominate nonpolar liquids and full covalent bonds (80–100+ kcal/mol). This intermediate strength is precisely what makes hydrogen bonds so biologically useful: they are strong enough to impart real structural cohesion and directionality, yet weak enough to be broken and reformed by thermal energy on picosecond timescales at physiological temperature, allowing water (and hydrogen-bonded biomolecular assemblies generally) to be simultaneously stable and dynamic.

In liquid water at room temperature, each molecule participates, on average, in approximately 3.5 hydrogen bonds at any instant, compared with the maximum of four (two donated, two accepted) achieved in the fully ordered ice lattice. This network is not static: individual hydrogen bonds break and reform on a timescale of roughly 1–2 picoseconds, so that the liquid is best conceived not as a fixed structure but as a rapidly fluctuating ensemble of transient, locally tetrahedral clusters (Nilsson & Pettersson, 2015).

2.3 Macroscopic Consequences of Molecular Structure

The molecular properties described above give rise to a set of bulk physical properties that depart substantially from what would be predicted by simple extrapolation from other small hydride molecules of comparable mass (e.g., H₂S, NH₃, HF, CH₄). Table 1 (described narratively below, as this review does not reproduce a numerical table) captures several of the most biologically consequential of these anomalies.

Water's boiling point (100°C at 1 atm) is roughly 200°C higher than would be predicted by interpolating the boiling points of the other Group 16 hydrides (H₂S boils at −60°C, H₂Se at −41°C). This reflects the substantial energetic cost of breaking the extensive hydrogen-bond network upon vaporization. Water's specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g·°C) is unusually high among common liquids, meaning that a given quantity of heat produces a comparatively small temperature change — a property that buffers organisms and bodies of water against rapid thermal fluctuation and is central to thermoregulation in endotherms and to the moderating effect large water bodies exert on regional climate. Water's heat of vaporization (2260 J/g) is likewise very high, underlying the effectiveness of evaporative cooling mechanisms such as sweating and panting.

Water's surface tension (72.8 mN/m at 20°C) is the highest of any common liquid except mercury, a consequence of the cohesive hydrogen-bond network that must be disrupted to create new surface area; this property underlies capillary action in the xylem of plants and the ability of small arthropods to walk on water. Water's density anomaly — reaching a maximum at approximately 4°C rather than continuing to increase monotonically as temperature falls toward the freezing point, and then decreasing further upon freezing so that ice floats — is arguably water's single most ecologically consequential anomaly, since it prevents bodies of water from freezing solid from the bottom up and thereby protects aquatic ecosystems during winter (Kontogeorgis et al., 2022). This behavior arises because, as water cools toward 0°C, the competition between two effects — the normal thermal contraction that increases density, and the increasing formation of a more open, lower-density, tetrahedrally hydrogen-bonded local structure reminiscent of ice — shifts in favor of the latter below 4°C (Errington & Debenedetti, 2001).

Water is also an exceptional solvent, a property directly traceable to its high dielectric constant (approximately 80 at 20°C, compared with roughly 2 for a typical hydrocarbon). Because the dielectric constant governs the degree to which a solvent screens electrostatic interactions between charged or polar solutes, water's high value allows it to readily dissolve ionic compounds (by stabilizing separated ions through hydration) and polar molecules, while excluding nonpolar species — the physical basis of the hydrophobic effect discussed in Section 4.

2.4 Isotopic Variants of Water

Naturally occurring water is not chemically homogeneous. Roughly 1 in 6400 water molecules contains deuterium (²H or D) rather than ordinary hydrogen (¹H or protium) at one of its two hydrogen positions, and a much smaller fraction contains the oxygen-18 isotope rather than the far more abundant oxygen-16. Heavy water (D₂O), in which both hydrogens are deuterium, is chemically similar to but physically distinguishable from ordinary water: it has a higher melting point (3.8°C), boiling point (101.4°C), and density, and its O–D bonds are slightly shorter and stronger than O–H bonds because of the larger mass of deuterium (a manifestation of the primary kinetic and equilibrium isotope effects). Heavy water is measurably toxic to most organisms at high concentrations because D₂O alters the kinetics of enzymatic reactions and the strength of hydrogen bonds in biomolecules, disrupting cell division and enzyme function; however, dilute doses of D₂O and stable isotope tracers based on ²H and ¹⁸O are widely used in biochemistry and physiology, most notably in the doubly labeled water method for measuring total energy expenditure in free-living animals and humans. These isotopic tools underscore a broader point: water's isotopic composition is not a chemical curiosity but a practical handle that biochemists and physiologists use to trace water flux, metabolic rate, and hydration status experimentally.

3. Hydrogen Bonding and the Anomalous Properties of Water

3.1 The Tetrahedral Network and Its Fluctuations

The central organizing concept for understanding liquid water's structure is the idea of a fluctuating, locally tetrahedral hydrogen-bond network. In ice Ih, every water molecule occupies a fixed lattice position and donates exactly two hydrogen bonds while accepting exactly two, producing a fully connected, open, tetrahedral crystal with a density lower than that of the liquid — the structural basis for ice floating. In the liquid phase, thermal energy is sufficient to distort and break individual hydrogen bonds continuously, but not to eliminate the underlying tendency toward local tetrahedral coordination. The result, as revealed by X-ray and neutron diffraction studies and elaborated by decades of molecular dynamics simulation, is a liquid whose instantaneous structure resembles a distorted, defect-riddled, and constantly reorganizing version of the ice lattice (Nilsson & Pettersson, 2015; Kontogeorgis et al., 2022).

A body of research associated with Nilsson, Pettersson, and colleagues has argued, on the basis of X-ray absorption and emission spectroscopy, that liquid water at ambient conditions may be usefully described as a fluctuating mixture of two types of local environments: a more tetrahedrally ordered, lower-density local structure and a more disordered, higher-density local structure, with the equilibrium between them shifting with temperature and pressure (Nilsson & Pettersson, 2015). This "two-state" or "two-structure" interpretation remains debated within the water physics community; other researchers, applying different spectroscopic and computational analyses, argue that liquid water is better described as a single, continuously distributed ensemble of hydrogen-bonded environments without a genuine bimodal split (Kontogeorgis et al., 2022). What is not in dispute is that the correlation between local tetrahedral order and water's anomalous density behavior is real and robust: computational studies that track a local order parameter alongside density find that increases in tetrahedral order accompany the density anomalies water exhibits under supercooling (Errington & Debenedetti, 2001).

3.2 Supercooled Water and the Second Critical Point Hypothesis

Water's anomalies become more pronounced, not less, as the liquid is supercooled below 0°C while avoiding crystallization — a metastable state achievable in small droplets or confined geometries. Several thermodynamic response functions (isothermal compressibility, isobaric heat capacity, the magnitude of the thermal expansion coefficient) appear to diverge or grow sharply as temperature is lowered toward roughly −45°C, a phenomenon that has motivated the hypothesis of a second, liquid-liquid critical point in deeply supercooled water, separating two metastable liquid phases of differing density (a low-density and a high-density amorphous-like liquid). This hypothesis, while still actively investigated and not universally accepted, provides a coherent thermodynamic framework for understanding why water's response functions behave so anomalously compared with normal liquids, and it dovetails with the two-state structural picture described above (Nilsson & Pettersson, 2015).

3.3 Water's Dynamic Heterogeneity

Beyond static structure, water exhibits dynamic heterogeneity: at any instant, different local regions of the hydrogen-bond network relax (that is, reorganize their local bonding pattern) at different rates, some regions behaving as comparatively "slow" and locally ordered, others as "fast" and disordered, with molecules exchanging between these populations on sub-picosecond to picosecond timescales (Chen et al., 2006). This dynamic heterogeneity is not merely an academic curiosity: it directly informs how biological macromolecules experience the aqueous environment around them, since a protein or nucleic acid surface will, at any given moment, be in contact with a heterogeneous population of water molecules with a distribution of local mobilities and hydrogen-bonding configurations, a theme developed further in Section 5.

3.4 Anomalies Enumerated and Their Biological Significance

Taken together, water exhibits upward of sixty documented physical anomalies relative to the behavior expected of a "normal" simple liquid, a catalog maintained and updated by researchers such as Martin Chaplin (Chaplin, 2006). The anomalies most relevant to biochemistry include: the density maximum near 4°C and the expansion upon freezing (ecological protection of aquatic life; buffering of intracellular ice formation dynamics during freeze tolerance and cryopreservation); the unusually high heat capacity, heat of fusion, and heat of vaporization (thermal buffering of organisms and evaporative cooling); the high surface tension (capillary transport in plants, surface-dwelling insect locomotion, alveolar surface tension physiology in the lung, where surfactant is required precisely because water's surface tension would otherwise be too high for stable, low-work-of-breathing lung inflation); the high dielectric constant (solvation of ions and polar biomolecules, screening of electrostatic interactions that allows folded proteins and nucleic acids to remain stable in aqueous solution despite carrying substantial fixed and counterion charge); the anomalously low viscosity response to pressure at low temperature (relevant to deep-sea and high-pressure-adapted organisms); and the existence of a temperature of maximum density that shifts with solute concentration and pressure, which has practical consequences for the ecology of lakes, sea ice, and the survival strategies of overwintering ectotherms.

It is worth pausing on the epistemic status of this catalog of anomalies. The precise microscopic origin of many of water's anomalies remains an active area of research and, in some cases, genuine scientific dispute — the review by Kontogeorgis et al. (2022) is explicit that "the origin of water's anomalous properties is far from clear," and that hydrogen bonding, while necessary, is not by itself a sufficient explanation for the full panoply of observed behavior. This review therefore presents the hydrogen-bond network model as the best-supported and most widely used explanatory framework in contemporary water science, while noting that the field continues to refine and, in some particulars, revise the details of that framework.

3.5 The Hofmeister Series and Ion-Specific Effects on Water Structure

Different dissolved ions perturb the hydrogen-bond network of surrounding water to different degrees and in different directions, a phenomenon first systematized empirically by Franz Hofmeister in 1888 through his observations of how different salts affected protein precipitation ("salting out" versus "salting in"). The resulting Hofmeister series ranks common ions by their propensity to stabilize (kosmotropic, or "structure-making") versus destabilize (chaotropic, or "structure-breaking") the native structure of proteins and the hydrogen-bond network of surrounding water. For anions, a typical ordering runs approximately: citrate³⁻ > sulfate²⁻ > phosphate > fluoride > chloride > bromide > nitrate > iodide > thiocyanate, moving from strongly kosmotropic to strongly chaotropic. Contemporary research has substantially revised the original interpretation of Hofmeister effects as arising purely from bulk water structure-making or -breaking; current evidence indicates that direct ion-macromolecule interactions (particularly with charged and polar surface groups) contribute at least as much as, and in many cases more than, any generic effect on bulk water structure (Zhang & Cremer, 2006). Nonetheless, the Hofmeister series remains an indispensable practical and conceptual tool in protein biochemistry, governing buffer and precipitant choice in protein purification and crystallization, and illustrating concretely that "water structure" in a biological context cannot be considered independently of the ions and solutes dissolved within it.

4. Thermodynamics of Hydration: The Hydrophobic and Hydrophilic Effects

4.1 Hydrophilic Hydration

When a polar or charged solute dissolves in water, surrounding water molecules reorient so that their hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor groups form favorable electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions with the solute — a process called hydrophilic hydration. For small ions, this process is strongly exothermic: the hydration enthalpy of Na⁺, for example, is approximately −405 kJ/mol, and that of Cl⁻ approximately −340 kJ/mol, energies that must be paid back (or "unpaid," from the solute's perspective) whenever an ion pair such as NaCl is desolvated, as occurs transiently during many biochemical processes including ion channel permeation and enzyme catalysis involving charged transition states. Hydrophilic hydration is generally enthalpically driven and, because it involves the ordering of water molecules into relatively fixed orientations around the solute, is often accompanied by a decrease in entropy relative to bulk water — the opposite entropic signature from the hydrophobic effect discussed below.

4.2 The Classical (Entropic) View of the Hydrophobic Effect

The hydrophobic effect refers to the tendency of nonpolar (apolar) solutes and surfaces to minimize their contact with water, driving processes such as the aggregation of oil droplets, the self-assembly of lipids into bilayers and micelles, and the burial of nonpolar amino acid side chains in the interior of folded proteins. Historically, the hydrophobic effect was explained by the "iceberg" or "flickering cluster" model proposed by Henry Frank and Marjorie Evans in 1945 and elaborated by Walter Kauzmann and Charles Tanford in the mid-twentieth century: because a nonpolar solute cannot participate in hydrogen bonding, water molecules adjacent to it were thought to reorganize into a more ordered, ice-like cage (a "clathrate" structure) in order to preserve as many hydrogen bonds as possible among themselves, at the cost of reduced orientational entropy. On this model, the thermodynamic signature of dissolving a small nonpolar solute in water — large, unfavorable, negative entropy of solvation, coupled with a smaller, sometimes even favorable, enthalpy change — arises directly from this ordering of the surrounding water shell, and the hydrophobic effect is understood as fundamentally entropic in nature: apolar solutes aggregate together not primarily because they attract one another, but because doing so releases the ordered water molecules from their surfaces back into the higher-entropy bulk phase (Kauzmann, 1959; Tanford, 1980).

This classical picture correctly predicts the experimentally observed thermodynamic signature for the transfer of small nonpolar solutes from a nonpolar liquid into water at room temperature: a positive free energy of transfer (unfavorable), driven by a large unfavorable entropy term that outweighs a favorable or near-zero enthalpy term, together with a large positive heat capacity change (ΔCp) — this last observation, the anomalously large and positive ΔCp of hydrophobic hydration, is considered one of the most diagnostic and reliable experimental signatures of the hydrophobic effect and is widely used in calorimetric studies of protein folding and ligand binding to identify buried nonpolar surface area (Chandler, 2005).

4.3 The Modern (Length-Scale-Dependent) View

Beginning in the 1990s, statistical mechanical theory and molecular simulation substantially revised and extended the classical picture, particularly for hydrophobic solutes and surfaces larger than approximately 1 nanometer in radius. David Chandler and colleagues showed that the physics of hydration changes qualitatively as the size of the nonpolar solute or surface increases: for small solutes (comparable in size to a single water molecule or a small side chain), water can accommodate the solute by reorganizing its hydrogen-bond network around it without breaking many hydrogen bonds, preserving the entropically costly but enthalpically cheap "cage" picture described above. For larger nonpolar surfaces (larger than roughly 1 nm, a size scale relevant to protein folding interfaces, membrane surfaces, and the aggregation of unfolded polypeptide segments), however, water can no longer wrap around the solute while maintaining an intact hydrogen-bond network; instead, an actual depletion layer of reduced water density forms at the interface, and the free energetic cost becomes dominated by the surface tension associated with creating this water-poor interface, analogous to macroscopic oil-water interfacial tension, rather than by an entropic "ordering" penalty (Chandler, 2005; Despa & Berry, 2007).

This length-scale-dependent picture has important consequences for how biochemists interpret hydrophobic driving forces in protein folding and molecular assembly. It implies that the "collapse" of an extended, unfolded polypeptide chain in water — an early and thermodynamically important step in protein folding — may behave more like the classical small-length-scale wetting of individual side chains during the earliest stages of compaction, transitioning to large-length-scale dewetting physics as folding intermediates accumulate substantial buried nonpolar surface area, particularly at hydrophobic interfaces between folded domains, in the hydrophobic cores of oligomeric protein complexes, and at protein–ligand and protein–protein binding interfaces (Chandler, 2005).

4.4 Interfacial Water Depletion and Its Experimental Signatures

The dewetting transition predicted by the modern theory of hydrophobicity has been observed experimentally and computationally at extended hydrophobic surfaces, where water density measured by X-ray and neutron reflectivity, as well as by simulation, is found to be reduced by 10–20% within roughly 3–4 Å of the surface relative to bulk density (Chandler, 2005). This depletion layer has functional consequences: it lowers the free energy barrier for two hydrophobic surfaces to come into direct contact (since less interfacial water must be displaced), a phenomenon directly relevant to protein–protein association, ligand docking into hydrophobic binding pockets, and the fusion of lipid bilayers. Confined-water simulations, including of water passing through hydrophobic carbon nanotube channels, have shown that under some conditions water can also move cooperatively and with surprisingly low friction through nonpolar confinement, single-file, forming and breaking hydrogen bonds with neighboring water molecules rather than with the channel wall — a finding with direct implications for understanding the mechanism by which water crosses the hydrophobic interior of channel proteins such as aquaporins (Hummer et al., 2001), discussed further in Section 9.

4.5 Thermodynamic Signatures in Protein Folding and Binding

Protein folding and ligand binding reactions are typically characterized calorimetrically (via isothermal titration calorimetry or differential scanning calorimetry) in terms of their changes in Gibbs free energy (ΔG), enthalpy (ΔH), entropy (ΔS), and heat capacity (ΔCp). A large negative ΔCp upon folding or binding is generally interpreted as evidence for burial of nonpolar (hydrophobic) surface area, consistent with the release of ordered hydration water; empirical correlations relating ΔCp to the amount of nonpolar versus polar surface area buried (developed originally by Spolar, Record, and others building on Tanford's foundational work) remain a standard tool for interpreting binding thermodynamics in structural biochemistry. At the same time, contemporary work cautions against overly simplistic decomposition of binding thermodynamics into purely "hydrophobic" and "hydrophilic" contributions, since enthalpy-entropy compensation, changes in conformational entropy of the protein itself, and specific, tightly bound water molecules at the binding interface (sometimes displaced upon binding, sometimes retained and directly bridging protein and ligand via hydrogen bonds) all contribute to the observed thermodynamics in ways that are difficult to disentangle experimentally (Chandler, 2005; Levy & Onuchic, 2006).

4.6 Osmolytes, Kosmotropes, and Chaotropes

Beyond simple ions and nonpolar solutes, a special class of small organic solutes called osmolytes profoundly affects protein hydration thermodynamics and is used by organisms across the tree of life to protect proteins under osmotic, thermal, and desiccation stress. Compatible osmolytes such as trehalose, glycine betaine, and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) are termed "protecting" osmolytes because they are preferentially excluded from the immediate hydration shell of proteins (a phenomenon called preferential exclusion or preferential hydration), which thermodynamically favors the more compact, folded state of the protein because unfolding would increase the protein's surface area and thus the unfavorable free energy cost of excluding osmolyte from an enlarged hydration shell (Yancey et al., 1982). Conversely, denaturing osmolytes such as urea interact favorably and directly with the peptide backbone, preferentially accumulating near the protein surface and thermodynamically favoring the unfolded state, which exposes more backbone for such interactions. The classification of solutes as kosmotropic (structure-stabilizing, preferentially excluded) versus chaotropic (structure-destabilizing, preferentially bound) is central to understanding the protective strategies used by organisms in osmotically stressful environments — halophilic and xerotolerant organisms in particular — and is discussed further in Section 13 in the context of anhydrobiosis (Somero & Yancey, 1997; Yancey et al., 1982).

4.7 Water Activity Introduced

The concepts developed in this section — that not all water in a system is thermodynamically equivalent, and that solutes profoundly alter the free energy of the surrounding water — converge in the practically important concept of water activity (aw), formally defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water in a given system to the vapor pressure of pure water at the same temperature, and thus a direct thermodynamic measure of the "escaping tendency," or chemical potential, of water in a solution or food matrix, distinct from the total water content by mass (Scott, 1957). Water activity ranges from 0 (no available water) to 1.0 (pure water) and, critically for biology, governs the growth of microorganisms independent of the absolute quantity of water present: most bacteria require aw above approximately 0.91 to grow, most yeasts require aw above approximately 0.88, and molds can tolerate aw as low as approximately 0.65, explaining why foods such as honey (very high total water content but very low aw, owing to the high solute concentration of sugars binding available water) are microbiologically stable, while fresh produce (aw of 0.97–0.99) spoils rapidly. We return to water activity in greater depth in Section 12, after first establishing water's structural and dynamical roles in biomacromolecules.

5. Water and Protein Structure, Folding, and Stability

5.1 The Hydration Shell: Definition and Extent

Every folded protein in aqueous solution is surrounded by a hydration shell — a layer of water molecules whose structural and dynamical properties are measurably perturbed relative to bulk water by their proximity to the protein surface. The first hydration shell, comprising water molecules in direct contact with the protein surface (roughly one layer, or about 0.3 nm thick), is the most strongly perturbed; a second, more weakly perturbed shell extends a further 0.3–0.6 nm, beyond which water properties converge rapidly toward bulk behavior (Halle, 2004; Raschke, 2006). For a typical globular protein of 20–30 kDa, the first hydration shell comprises several hundred water molecules — a substantial population that must be accounted for in any complete structural or thermodynamic description of the folded protein.

Crystallographic studies, beginning with high-resolution X-ray structures in the 1970s and 1980s, revealed that a subset of hydration-shell water molecules are sufficiently well-ordered and long-lived to be resolved as discrete electron density peaks, occupying specific, often conserved positions bridging polar side chains, backbone carbonyl and amide groups, and sometimes ligands, across many independently solved structures of homologous proteins (Schoenborn et al., 1995; Teeter, 1991). These crystallographically ordered waters are not simply an experimental artifact of the crystal lattice: many of the same positions are recovered by solution NMR studies using nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) measurements between protein protons and water protons, confirming that these bound water molecules persist, at least transiently, in solution as well (Otting et al., 1991).

5.2 Dynamics of Hydration-Shell Water

Nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation dispersion, ultrafast infrared and terahertz spectroscopy, and molecular dynamics simulation together indicate that most hydration-shell water molecules are not permanently bound but exchange with bulk water on timescales ranging from tens of picoseconds to a few nanoseconds — dramatically faster than the "bound water" terminology might suggest, but still measurably slower than the sub-picosecond reorientation time of bulk water molecules (Halle, 2004; Pal & Zewail, 2004). A small subpopulation of waters, typically those buried in surface pockets or coordinating structurally important ions, exchange more slowly, with residence times extending to hundreds of nanoseconds or, in rare deeply buried cases, much longer (Halle, 2004).

This picture has been sharpened by femtosecond and terahertz spectroscopic studies pioneered by groups including Zewail, Havenith, and Bakker, which use the reorientational dynamics of water's OH stretch or its far-infrared/terahertz absorption as a direct read-out of local hydrogen-bond network dynamics. Ahmed Zewail's ultrafast spectroscopy work demonstrated that hydration dynamics around proteins and DNA occur on a hierarchy of timescales, from sub-picosecond librational motions to slower (tens of picoseconds) collective network reorganizations that are measurably retarded near the biomolecular surface relative to bulk water — a phenomenon sometimes called the "biological water" slow relaxation component (Pal & Zewail, 2004). Martina Havenith and colleagues extended this picture using terahertz absorption spectroscopy, showing that the dynamical perturbation of water extends further from the protein surface than static structural measures alone would suggest — an "extended dynamical hydration shell" affecting water tens of angstroms from the protein, albeit with a perturbation that decays smoothly rather than defining a sharp boundary (Ebbinghaus et al., 2007; Nibali & Havenith, 2014).

It should be emphasized that this remains an actively contested quantitative question. A 2017 study by Mondal, Mukherjee, and Bagchi, provocatively titled "Protein Hydration Dynamics: Much Ado about Nothing?", argued that when analyzed carefully, the majority of hydration-shell water retains largely bulk-like translational and orientational dynamics, with only a modest fraction of waters — those in direct, geometrically constrained contact with the protein surface — showing substantially retarded dynamics, and cautioned against overstating the extent and biological significance of "slow water" (Mondal et al., 2017). This tension between groups emphasizing an extended, biologically important dynamical perturbation and groups emphasizing predominantly bulk-like behavior with only local perturbation is a recurring theme throughout the biological water literature and is revisited in Section 11.

5.3 Water's Role in Protein Folding

The classical thermodynamic picture developed in Section 4 provides the conceptual backbone for understanding protein folding as a competition between the conformational entropy of the unfolded chain, the internal enthalpy of intramolecular interactions (hydrogen bonds, van der Waals packing, and electrostatic interactions) formed upon folding, and the hydration thermodynamics of burying nonpolar surface area away from water while exposing polar and charged surface area to it. The hydrophobic collapse model, in which the earliest steps of folding are driven substantially by the burial of nonpolar side chains, remains a widely used organizing framework, though contemporary views increasingly emphasize that folding is not a simple two-step hydrophobic-collapse-then-search process but rather a funneled, often highly cooperative process in which hydrophobic collapse, native hydrogen-bond formation, and secondary structure formation occur in a closely coupled, not strictly sequential, manner (Levy & Onuchic, 2006).

Water plays an active, not merely passive, mechanistic role in this process. Molecular dynamics simulations and experimental kinetic studies indicate that water molecules must be displaced from the interfaces of folding intermediates as secondary and tertiary structure forms, and that this desolvation step can itself be rate-limiting for specific folding transitions, particularly the docking of preformed secondary structural elements into their final tertiary arrangement (Levy & Onuchic, 2006). In this sense, water is sometimes described as an active "lubricant" or even a transient structural component of the folding transition state, rather than simply an inert medium in which folding occurs.

5.4 Water and Protein Stability

The stability of the folded state relative to the unfolded state — typically only 5–15 kcal/mol for a globular protein under physiological conditions, a remarkably small margin given the hundreds of favorable interactions formed upon folding — reflects a delicate balance in which hydration thermodynamics plays a decisive role. This marginal stability is now understood to arise because the large favorable enthalpy of forming intramolecular hydrogen bonds and packing interactions in the folded state is very substantially offset by the unfavorable loss of conformational entropy of the polypeptide chain and by the enthalpy "cost" of desolvating polar and charged groups that were well-hydrated in the unfolded state (Kauzmann, 1959). Osmolytes, as discussed in Section 4.6, shift this balance by altering the free energy of the unfolded, more solvent-exposed state relative to the compact, folded state, providing organisms with a chemical mechanism for tuning protein stability without altering protein sequence — a strategy exploited especially by marine and desert organisms subjected to variable temperature, pressure, and osmotic stress (Somero & Yancey, 1997; Yancey et al., 1982).

Water molecules that are unusually well-ordered and buried within a folded protein's interior — as opposed to the surface hydration shell — can also make direct, quantifiable contributions to folding stability, typically by bridging hydrogen bonds between backbone or side-chain groups that would otherwise be geometrically unable to interact directly, effectively acting as an integral structural component of the fold rather than as solvent (Rupley & Careri, 1991). Studies mutating out such buried waters (for example, by filling the cavity with a bulkier side chain) generally find a measurable, sometimes substantial, destabilization of the folded state, underscoring that a small number of water molecules can be as structurally important, on a per-molecule basis, as amino acid side chains themselves.

6. Water in Enzyme Catalysis

6.1 Water as Substrate, Product, and Catalytic Participant

Water is directly involved as a reactant or product in an enormous fraction of enzymatic reactions: hydrolases (including proteases, nucleases, phosphatases, and glycosidases) use a water molecule as the nucleophile that attacks the substrate; many biosynthetic condensation reactions (e.g., peptide bond formation, glycosidic bond formation, and ester formation) release water as a product; and oxidoreductases such as cytochrome c oxidase reduce molecular oxygen to water as the terminal step of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, a reaction of first-order importance to aerobic bioenergetics. In these reactions, the active site must precisely position, activate (often via deprotonation to generate a more nucleophilic hydroxide-like species), and orient a single water molecule with a specificity and rate enhancement that would be essentially impossible in bulk solution, where the same reaction (for instance, uncatalyzed peptide bond hydrolysis) can proceed with a half-life of hundreds of years (Kornblatt & Kornblatt, 2002).

6.2 Active-Site Water Networks

Beyond serving as a direct reactant, water plays a broader structural and mechanistic role within enzyme active sites. Surveys of high-resolution (< 1.5 Å) enzyme crystal structures find that the overwhelming majority contain continuous, ordered chains of water molecules linking active-site residues to the bulk solvent (Sindic et al., 2024). These water "wires" or networks serve multiple functions: they can participate directly in proton transfer via a Grotthuss-type mechanism, in which a proton hops sequentially along a chain of hydrogen-bonded water molecules rather than requiring a single water molecule to physically diffuse the entire distance, dramatically accelerating long-range proton shuttling; they provide a route for recharging the protonation state of catalytic residues after each turnover; and they can modulate substrate and product binding and release kinetics by controlling access to and egress from the active-site cavity (Pocker, 2000; Kornblatt & Kornblatt, 2002).

Carbonic anhydrase provides a particularly well-studied example. This enzyme, which catalyzes the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide to bicarbonate (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺) at rates approaching the diffusion limit (up to 10⁶ reactions per second per enzyme molecule, among the fastest known enzymatic turnover rates), relies on a zinc-bound hydroxide ion as the catalytic nucleophile and on a proton-relay network of active-site water molecules and a histidine residue to shuttle the product proton out of the active site and regenerate the catalytically competent zinc-hydroxide state. Recent ultrafast structural studies have shown directly that the dynamics of this active-site water network, not merely its static structure, are rate-limiting for the product-release step of the catalytic cycle, providing direct experimental evidence that water dynamics — not simply water's presence — can govern enzymatic turnover (Kim et al., 2025; Boone et al., 2013).

6.3 Water Exclusion as a Catalytic Strategy

Paradoxically, given the above, many enzymes achieve rate enhancement precisely by excluding bulk water from their active sites once substrate is bound. Creating a low-dielectric, largely desolvated microenvironment around the reacting atoms can dramatically stabilize charge-separated or highly polarized transition states relative to the same transition state in bulk water, where the high dielectric constant and hydrogen-bonding competition of water actually stabilize the ground state (and thus raise the effective activation barrier) more than they stabilize most transition states. This principle — articulated in various forms by Warshel, Jencks, and others in the physical-organic and computational enzymology literature — helps explain why so many active sites are lined with nonpolar residues and undergo conformational changes ("induced fit" or "lid closure") upon substrate binding that physically occlude the active site from bulk solvent, even as the enzyme depends on a small number of precisely placed water molecules (of the kind described in Section 6.2) for direct chemical participation. This apparent tension — needing water for chemistry while excluding water for electrostatic transition-state stabilization — is resolved by the fact that enzymes control water at a level of precision (individual molecules, individual protonation states, individual residence times) unavailable to uncatalyzed solution chemistry (Colussi, 2026).

6.4 Water in Enzyme Conformational Dynamics: The Protein Dynamical Transition

Enzymes, like all proteins, undergo a well-documented "dynamical transition" near 200–220 K, below which large-amplitude, anharmonic conformational motions (implicated in many catalytic cycles, including domain closure, loop motions gating substrate access, and allosteric conformational changes) are frozen out, and above which such motions become thermally accessible. A substantial body of neutron scattering, Mössbauer spectroscopy, and molecular dynamics simulation evidence indicates that this dynamical transition is not an intrinsic property of the protein alone but is coupled to, and in a meaningful sense caused by, the freezing and thawing of mobility in the surrounding hydration shell: below the transition temperature, hydration water itself becomes kinetically arrested (entering a glassy state), and this arrest mechanically couples to and suppresses the larger-scale conformational motions of the protein (Tarek & Tobias, 2002). This finding provides one of the clearest demonstrations that protein function — specifically, the conformational flexibility required for many catalytic mechanisms — is thermodynamically and kinetically coupled to the behavior of the surrounding solvent, reinforcing the broader theme of this review that water and biomacromolecule cannot be treated as fully independent systems.

7. Water and Nucleic Acid Structure

7.1 The Minor Groove Spine of Hydration

The B-form DNA double helix, the predominant conformation of DNA under physiological conditions, is characterized by a distinctive pattern of ordered water molecules occupying its minor groove, first observed crystallographically in the Dickerson-Drew B-DNA dodecamer structure and subsequently confirmed and refined across many independent high-resolution crystal structures. In AT-rich sequences, a single-file, zig-zag chain of water molecules — the so-called "spine of hydration" — bridges the N3 atoms of adenine and O2 atoms of thymine on opposite strands at the floor of the narrow minor groove, with a second, less regular layer of water bridging the first spine to the surrounding bulk solvent (a "hydrat-ion" spine, when a bound monovalent cation such as Na⁺ replaces one or more of the waters at specific positions, is also observed). Higher-resolution structures have refined this classical picture further, revealing a more extended network of fused water pentagons and hexagons within the minor groove, leading some researchers to prefer the term "ribbon of hydration" over "spine of hydration" to capture the two-dimensional, sheet-like character of the network rather than a strictly one-dimensional chain (McDermott et al., 2017; Tereshko et al., 1999).

Solution-state NMR studies, which can directly detect nuclear Overhauser effects between DNA protons and the protons of individual, slowly exchanging water molecules, have confirmed that the minor-groove spine of hydration observed crystallographically is not an artifact of the crystal lattice but persists in solution, with residence times for individual spine waters extending well beyond one nanosecond — orders of magnitude longer than the sub-picosecond residence time of bulk water molecules near a generic solute surface (Liepinsh et al., 1992). Functionally, this spine of hydration measurably stabilizes the B-DNA duplex against thermal fluctuations and local base-pair opening in the premelting temperature regime, effectively acting as a structural "staple" that helps lock AT-rich tracts into the narrow-minor-groove B-form conformation characteristic of such sequences (Chen & Prohofsky, 1992).

7.2 Sequence Dependence and the A-to-B Transition

The extent and stability of minor-groove hydration is sequence-dependent: AT-rich tracts support a well-ordered, long-lived spine, while GC base pairs, whose minor groove presents an additional exocyclic amino group from guanine, disrupt the regular spine geometry and instead coordinate water in a less regular fashion. This sequence-dependent hydration pattern is mechanistically linked to the well-documented tendency of the DNA double helix to adopt a narrower minor groove at AT-rich sequences and is one of several molecular mechanisms (together with base-stacking energetics and counterion condensation) that couples DNA sequence to local helical geometry, with functional consequences for the sequence-specific recognition of DNA by many transcription factors and other DNA-binding proteins, several of which directly read out minor-groove width and shape rather than (or in addition to) directly contacting the bases via hydrogen bonding (Chen & Prohofsky, 1992; Liepinsh et al., 1992).

More broadly, the balance between hydrophilic hydration of the sugar-phosphate backbone and bases versus the competing influence of counterions and dehydrating cosolutes governs the well-known A-form to B-form structural transition of double-stranded DNA: reducing water activity (for instance, by adding ethanol or increasing salt concentration) favors the more compact, dehydrated A-form geometry (also the predominant conformation of double-stranded RNA and RNA-DNA hybrids under physiological conditions, owing to steric clash from the 2′-hydroxyl group in the B-form geometry), while high water activity favors the more extended, more fully hydrated B-form characteristic of genomic DNA under standard physiological conditions (Yatsunyk & Neidle, 2024).

7.3 Water and RNA Structure and Function

RNA molecules, which are typically single-stranded and fold into complex secondary and tertiary structures stabilized by an intricate combination of base-pairing, base-stacking, and long-range tertiary contacts, depend on hydration in ways that parallel but also extend beyond those documented for DNA and protein. The A-form helical geometry adopted by double-stranded RNA regions positions the 2′-hydroxyl group of the ribose sugar to participate directly in an extended hydrogen-bond network with both intramolecular RNA groups and ordered water molecules in the deep, narrow major groove characteristic of the A-form, contributing to the generally greater thermodynamic stability of RNA duplexes relative to equivalent-sequence DNA duplexes. In folded ribozymes and riboswitches, ordered water molecules, much like the buried waters described in Section 5.4 for proteins, frequently bridge tertiary contacts between distant regions of the RNA that would otherwise be geometrically incompatible for direct hydrogen bonding, and are increasingly resolved as integral components of high-resolution RNA crystal and cryo-EM structures.

8. Water and Biological Membranes

8.1 The Lipid Bilayer as a Hydrophobic Barrier

Biological membranes are built around a lipid bilayer whose nonpolar acyl chains form a continuous hydrophobic core roughly 3 nm thick, flanked on both faces by the polar/zwitterionic headgroups of the constituent phospholipids, which interact directly and extensively with water. This architecture is itself a direct manifestation of the hydrophobic effect described in Section 4: amphipathic lipid molecules self-assemble spontaneously into bilayers (or, depending on their geometry, micelles or inverted structures) because doing so minimizes the unfavorable free energy of exposing their nonpolar acyl chains to water, releasing ordered hydration water and increasing overall system entropy, exactly as occurs during the hydrophobic collapse of a folding protein (Berkowitz et al., 2006).

The hydrophobic core of the bilayer is normally a formidable barrier to the passage of water and, especially, of polar and charged solutes, since crossing it requires the energetically costly desolvation of the permeant species. Despite this, water is not, in fact, fully excluded from lipid bilayers: because the bilayer interior retains substantial thermal disorder (particularly in the fluid, liquid-crystalline phase characteristic of most biological membranes at physiological temperature), transient defects and density fluctuations allow individual water molecules to penetrate measurably into the acyl-chain region, and a small but functionally important basal permeability to water exists even in the complete absence of any protein channel (Berkowitz et al., 2006).

8.2 Mechanisms and Determinants of Passive Water Permeability

Passive (non-protein-mediated) water permeability across lipid bilayers, measured experimentally since the pioneering work summarized in classic reviews by Finkelstein and others, occurs via at least two proposed molecular mechanisms: direct solubility-diffusion, in which individual water molecules partition into and diffuse across the hydrophobic core following the same solubility-diffusion physics that governs permeation of other small, weakly polar solutes, and transient pore formation, in which rare, large-amplitude thermal fluctuations in the bilayer create short-lived, water-filled defects spanning the membrane that permit more rapid, cooperative water passage (Deamer & Bramhall, 1986).

Systematic comparison across lipid compositions has shown that water permeability correlates most strongly with the average area occupied per lipid headgroup — a proxy for how loosely or tightly packed, and thus how permeable to transient defects, the bilayer is — and correlates comparatively poorly with bilayer thickness or other bulk mechanical properties in isolation (Mathai et al., 2008). The addition of cholesterol, a near-universal component of animal cell plasma membranes, generally decreases water permeability by increasing lipid packing order and reducing the area per lipid in the surrounding phospholipids — one of cholesterol's several well-documented roles in modulating overall membrane barrier function, alongside its effects on membrane fluidity and mechanical rigidity.

8.3 Interfacial (Headgroup) Hydration and Membrane Interactions

The lipid headgroup region — comprising the phosphate, glycerol backbone, and choline, ethanolamine, serine, or inositol moieties that define the major phospholipid classes — is intensely hydrated, with 10–20 water molecules typically associated with each lipid headgroup in the fully hydrated fluid bilayer state (Rand & Parsegian, 1989). This interfacial hydration layer is not merely a passive consequence of the polar headgroup chemistry; it generates a measurable, short-range repulsive force — the hydration force or hydration repulsion — that opposes the close apposition of two bilayers and must be overcome for membrane fusion events (such as those mediating vesicular trafficking, exocytosis, and viral envelope fusion) to proceed. The magnitude and decay length of this hydration repulsion have been characterized quantitatively using the osmotic stress method pioneered by Rand and Parsegian, in which bilayer spacing is measured as a function of applied osmotic pressure from a bathing solution containing a membrane-impermeant polymer, allowing direct extraction of the free energy of interbilayer hydration as a function of separation distance (Rand & Parsegian, 1989).

Membrane fusion machinery, including the SNARE protein complexes that mediate synaptic vesicle fusion and viral fusion proteins such as influenza hemagglutinin, must therefore mechanically and energetically overcome this hydration repulsion, and specialized fusion peptides frequently act in part by locally perturbing and displacing interfacial hydration water to permit the close bilayer apposition required for lipid mixing and pore formation. This principle also underlies the mechanism of action of cryoprotectants and membrane-stabilizing sugars such as trehalose (discussed further in Section 13), which are believed to substitute for headgroup hydration water under desiccation stress via a mechanism termed the "water replacement hypothesis," preserving headgroup spacing and preventing the damaging lipid phase transitions that would otherwise occur upon complete membrane dehydration (Crowe et al., 1998).

9. Aquaporins and Water Channels

9.1 Discovery

Prior to 1992, the mechanism by which water crosses cell membranes rapidly enough to account for physiological phenomena such as red blood cell osmotic swelling and renal urine concentration — far faster than could be explained by simple passive diffusion through the lipid bilayer alone, as described in Section 8 — was unknown, despite decades of physiological evidence for a specialized, "facilitated" water transport pathway. The discovery of the first molecular water channel is credited to Peter Agre and colleagues, who, while investigating a 28-kDa membrane protein initially believed to be a proteolytic fragment of the Rh blood group antigen, recognized it as an abundant, unrelated integral membrane protein highly expressed in red blood cells and renal tubules. Expressing this protein (originally designated CHIP28) in Xenopus laevis oocytes — cells that are normally quite water-impermeable — and exposing the oocytes to hypotonic medium caused the CHIP28-expressing oocytes, but not control oocytes, to swell rapidly and, at sufficiently low osmolarity, to lyse, providing direct functional proof that the protein formed a dedicated water channel (Preston et al., 1992). This protein was subsequently renamed aquaporin-1 (AQP1), and its discovery earned Peter Agre a share of the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (jointly with Roderick MacKinnon, recognized for structural work on potassium channels) (Agre, 2004).

9.2 Structure and the Selectivity Filter

Aquaporins are assembled as homotetramers in the membrane, with each of the four subunits forming an independent, functional water-conducting pore, such that the tetramer as a whole provides four parallel water pathways (with a separate, fifth, poorly characterized central pore of debated functional significance at the tetramer's four-fold axis in some family members). Each monomer adopts a distinctive "hourglass" fold built from six transmembrane alpha helices plus two shorter, re-entrant half-helices that meet near the middle of the membrane, each contributing a conserved Asparagine-Proline-Alanine (NPA) sequence motif that positions two asparagine side chains at the pore's narrowest constriction, where they hydrogen-bond directly to permeating water molecules and orient them precisely as they cross the channel (Agre, 2004).

Immediately extracellular to this NPA region lies a second, even narrower constriction called the aromatic/arginine (ar/R) selectivity filter, formed by conserved aromatic and a strictly conserved, positively charged arginine side chain, which narrows the pore to approximately 2.8 Å — just wide enough to admit a single water molecule in single file, but too narrow to admit larger solutes, and critically, positioned so that the strong local positive electrostatic field from the conserved arginine, combined with a precisely engineered reorientation of the transient dipole of each water molecule as it threads through the NPA motifs above and below, prevents the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism (described in Section 6.2) from operating within the pore. This is the key structural insight, confirmed by molecular dynamics simulation following the first high-resolution aquaporin crystal structures published in 2000–2001, explaining how aquaporins achieve their central functional paradox: extremely high water permeability (individual channels conduct up to roughly 3 billion water molecules per second) combined with near-total exclusion of protons and all other ions and solutes, including even the smallest ion, H⁺ — a combination that would otherwise seem thermodynamically difficult to reconcile, since a water-filled pore wide enough for rapid water flux would, in the absence of this specific electrostatic and orientational mechanism, be expected to support rapid proton conduction via the Grotthuss mechanism (Agre, 2004; Preston et al., 1992).

9.3 Aquaporin Diversity and Physiological Roles

The human genome encodes thirteen aquaporin family members (AQP0–AQP12), which can be broadly classified into strict water-selective channels (orthodox aquaporins, including AQP1, 2, 4, and 5) and aquaglyceroporins (including AQP3, 7, 9, and 10), which additionally conduct glycerol and, in some cases, other small uncharged solutes such as urea. Aquaporins are essential to the physiology of virtually every organ system involved in fluid transport or fluid balance: AQP1 is highly expressed in red blood cells, renal proximal tubule, and the choroid plexus (contributing to cerebrospinal fluid production); AQP2 is the primary vasopressin-regulated water channel of the renal collecting duct principal cells, the direct downstream effector of the vasopressin signaling pathway discussed in Section 10; AQP4 is the dominant water channel of astrocytes in the central nervous system and plays a documented role in cerebral edema following stroke and traumatic brain injury; and AQP5 is essential for normal saliva, tear, and sweat secretion. Mutations or autoimmune targeting of specific aquaporins produce distinct human diseases: loss-of-function mutations in AQP2 cause nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (an inability to concentrate urine despite normal or elevated circulating vasopressin), while autoantibodies against AQP4 are now recognized as the primary pathogenic driver of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, a demyelinating autoimmune disease of the central nervous system distinct from, though historically confused with, multiple sclerosis (Agre, 2004).

9.4 Aquaporins in Plants and Microorganisms

Aquaporins are not unique to animals: plant genomes typically encode a substantially larger aquaporin family (often 30–70 members) reflecting the elaborate demands of whole-plant water transport from root to leaf, turgor-driven cell expansion, and stomatal regulation, and microbial aquaporins (including the well-studied bacterial glycerol facilitator GlpF, a founding member of the aquaglyceroporin subfamily whose structure was solved alongside AQP1 and provided key comparative insight into the molecular basis of selectivity-filter specificity) illustrate that the fundamental architecture of the aquaporin fold — and the underlying biophysical problem of achieving fast, selective water conduction across a hydrophobic membrane barrier — is conserved across all three domains of life.

10. Cellular Osmoregulation and Water Homeostasis

10.1 Osmotic Principles at the Cellular Level

Because most biological membranes are far more permeable to water than to most dissolved solutes, water moves osmotically down its own concentration gradient (equivalently, toward the compartment of higher solute concentration) far more readily than solutes move to equalize concentration directly. Animal cells, which generally lack a rigid cell wall, are consequently vulnerable to osmotic swelling and lysis in hypotonic environments and to osmotic shrinkage (crenation) in hypertonic environments, and must actively regulate their internal solute composition to maintain cell volume within survivable limits — a process called cell volume regulation. The two principal regulatory responses are regulatory volume decrease (RVD), in which a cell swollen by hypotonic stress extrudes osmotically active solutes, chiefly K⁺, Cl⁻, and organic osmolytes, through activated ion channels and cotransporters, followed passively by water efflux, and regulatory volume increase (RVI), the converse response to hypertonic shrinkage, in which cells import ions and accumulate organic osmolytes (many of the same compatible osmolytes discussed in Section 4.6, including taurine, betaine, and myo-inositol) to draw water back in. Plant, fungal, and bacterial cells, by contrast, possess rigid cell walls that resist osmotic swelling mechanically, generating turgor pressure as water enters, a mechanically load-bearing state central to plant structural support, growth, and stomatal function, and regulated osmotically via accumulation and mobilization of solutes such as potassium ions and, in plants specifically, sucrose and other soluble carbohydrates.

10.2 Whole-Body Water Homeostasis in Mammals

In mammals, whole-body extracellular fluid osmolality is maintained within an extraordinarily narrow physiological range (approximately 275–295 mOsm/kg in humans) through the integrated action of thirst behavior, renal water handling, and the hormone arginine vasopressin (AVP), also known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH) (Bankir et al., 2017). Specialized osmoreceptor neurons in the hypothalamus, concentrated in and around the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (a circumventricular organ lacking a complete blood-brain barrier and thus directly exposed to circulating plasma osmolality), detect even small increases in plasma osmolality (as little as a 1–2% rise) via osmotically driven changes in cell volume that are mechanically transduced into altered neuronal firing rate, triggering both the conscious sensation of thirst and the synthesis and release of vasopressin from magnocellular neurons whose axons terminate in the posterior pituitary.

Vasopressin acts on renal collecting duct principal cells via the V2 receptor, a G-protein-coupled receptor whose activation raises intracellular cyclic AMP and, via protein kinase A signaling, triggers the phosphorylation and rapid trafficking of aquaporin-2 (AQP2) channels from cytoplasmic storage vesicles to the apical plasma membrane, sharply increasing the water permeability of the collecting duct and permitting water reabsorption down the steep osmotic gradient established by the renal medullary countercurrent multiplier system (Bankir et al., 2017). In the sustained absence of vasopressin signaling (as occurs in central diabetes insipidus, resulting from inadequate vasopressin secretion, or nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, resulting from renal insensitivity to vasopressin, frequently due to AQP2 or V2 receptor mutations), the collecting duct remains constitutively water-impermeable, and patients excrete very large volumes (up to 15–20 L per day) of dilute urine, a condition that, if not matched by equally aggressive fluid intake, rapidly produces severe hypernatremic dehydration.

At higher circulating concentrations, vasopressin also exerts a direct vasoconstrictive effect via V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle — the physiological basis of its alternative name — which becomes physiologically significant chiefly during severe hypovolemia (e.g., hemorrhage), when vasopressin release is triggered not only by osmotic stimuli but also, independently and more powerfully, by baroreceptor-detected reductions in effective circulating blood volume, illustrating that the vasopressin system integrates both osmotic and volume/pressure information into a single regulatory hormone.

10.3 The Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System and Integrated Fluid Balance

Water homeostasis cannot be fully separated from sodium homeostasis, since the osmotic activity of extracellular fluid is dominated by Na⁺ and its accompanying anions. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), triggered by reduced renal perfusion pressure, sympathetic activation, or reduced distal tubular sodium delivery, ultimately increases circulating angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates thirst and vasopressin release, directly linking the RAAS and vasopressin systems) and aldosterone (which increases distal nephron sodium reabsorption, with obligate water following osmotically). Together, the vasopressin and RAAS systems provide mammals with two integrated, partially overlapping hormonal axes that jointly regulate extracellular fluid volume and osmolality, allowing the two variables — which are physiologically distinct and can, in disease states, be independently perturbed — to be defended largely independently under normal physiological conditions.

11. The Intracellular Water Debate: Bulk-like or Structured?

11.1 Historical Background: Ling's Association-Induction Hypothesis

Beginning in the 1950s and developed over subsequent decades, the physiologist Gilbert Ling proposed a radical alternative to the conventional membrane-pump model of cellular ion and water regulation, arguing that the overwhelming majority of intracellular water is not free, bulk-like solvent but is instead extensively "structured" — organized in multilayer arrays with restricted rotational and translational freedom by adsorption onto extended, unfolded intracellular protein surfaces, particularly those of the cytoskeleton and other structural proteins. On this "association-induction hypothesis," the characteristic asymmetric distribution of ions across the plasma membrane (high intracellular K⁺, low intracellular Na⁺) was attributed not primarily to the activity of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase ion pump — the mechanism supported by the overwhelming weight of subsequent biochemical, genetic, and biophysical evidence — but to selective ion adsorption onto structured intracellular water and protein surfaces (Ling, 2003).

Ling's hypothesis, while historically influential in raising the legitimate and still-active question of whether and to what extent intracellular water departs from bulk behavior, has not been supported by the broader body of subsequent experimental evidence, which has repeatedly and directly confirmed both the existence and the physiological necessity of active, ATP-dependent ion pumping via the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (whose structure, mechanism, and stoichiometry are now understood in near-atomic detail) and has found that the great majority of intracellular water, measured by a range of independent biophysical techniques (discussed below), behaves in a manner much closer to bulk water than Ling's model would predict. Contemporary reviews of cell water generally treat the association-induction hypothesis as a historically important but empirically superseded minority position, while acknowledging that Ling correctly anticipated, decades before it could be rigorously tested, that water very close to macromolecular surfaces is measurably perturbed — a qualitatively correct insight whose quantitative magnitude and physiological significance he substantially overestimated (Mentré, 2012).

11.2 The Contemporary Consensus: Mostly Bulk-like, with a Measurable Perturbed Fraction

Modern biophysical measurements — including nuclear magnetic resonance relaxometry, quasi-elastic and inelastic neutron scattering, terahertz and Raman spectroscopy, and, most recently, stimulated Raman scattering microscopy capable of directly imaging water structure within single living cells — converge on a nuanced intermediate picture. The large majority of intracellular water (estimates in different cell types and by different techniques generally converge in the range of 80–97%) exhibits translational and rotational dynamics that are statistically indistinguishable from, or only mildly slower than, bulk water, while a smaller, but experimentally robust and reproducible, minority fraction (estimates commonly falling in the range of a few percent up to perhaps 15%, depending on cell type, technique, and the specific structural/dynamical property measured) shows a genuinely and measurably perturbed hydrogen-bond network, reduced mobility, and altered local structure, attributable to direct interaction with the dense, crowded population of intracellular macromolecular surfaces (Persson & Halle, 2008; Lang et al., 2024).

This "mostly bulk, with a genuine minority perturbed fraction" consensus is broadly consistent with, and indeed a natural extension of, the extracellular protein hydration-shell literature discussed in Section 5: cells are extraordinarily crowded environments, with total macromolecular concentrations often exceeding 300–400 g/L (a phenomenon termed macromolecular crowding), so that a substantially larger fraction of total cellular water lies within one or two hydration shells of some macromolecular surface than would be the case for a comparably concentrated solution of a single, well-separated purified protein — even though the intrinsic degree of perturbation experienced by any individual water molecule within a hydration shell is, on the available evidence, not dramatically different intracellularly than it is for an isolated protein in dilute solution (Ball, 2008; Chaplin, 2006).

11.3 Macromolecular Crowding and Water Potential

A related and increasingly active area of research concerns how macromolecular crowding and phase-separated biomolecular condensates (membraneless organelles formed by liquid-liquid phase separation of intrinsically disordered proteins and RNA, a major focus of contemporary cell biology) affect and are affected by local water activity and water potential within the cell. Recent work has shown that macromolecular condensation itself can measurably buffer intracellular water potential, providing cells with an additional, water-mediated homeostatic mechanism operating alongside classical ion-based osmoregulation, and suggesting that the biophysics of intracellular water and the biophysics of biomolecular condensate formation are more mechanistically intertwined than previously appreciated, though this specific claim has itself since attracted methodological debate and counter-analysis (Watson et al., 2023).

11.4 Why the Debate Persists

Part of the persistence of disagreement in this literature reflects genuine differences in what different experimental techniques are sensitive to: neutron scattering and NMR relaxometry probe different aspects of molecular motion (translational versus rotational, and on different characteristic timescales) than does terahertz spectroscopy, which is most sensitive to the collective, low-frequency intermolecular vibrational modes of the hydrogen-bond network; a technique tuned to detect subtle collective effects may report a larger "perturbed" fraction than a technique sensitive chiefly to single-molecule translational diffusion, even when both are examining the same physical system (Jungwirth, 2015). This methodological pluralism is a healthy feature of an active research field, but it does mean that flat, single-number claims about "how much" intracellular water is structured versus bulk-like should be treated cautiously and always considered in light of the specific technique and property being measured — a caution equally relevant to the more expansive and less well-supported claims discussed in Section 17.

12. Water Activity and Its Biological Significance

12.1 Definition and Physical Basis

As introduced in Section 4.7, water activity (aw) is formally defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water in a given system (p) to the vapor pressure of pure water (p₀) at the same temperature: aw = p/p₀. At thermodynamic equilibrium, water activity is numerically equal to the equilibrium relative humidity of the headspace air above a sample, divided by 100 — a relationship that underlies the standard laboratory method for measuring aw using a calibrated hygrometer. Water activity is fundamentally distinct from total water content expressed as a percentage by mass: two foods can have identical total water content yet very different water activities, because water activity depends on how strongly the available solutes and macromolecular surfaces in the system bind or otherwise reduce the escaping tendency (chemical potential) of the water present, not on the total quantity of water itself. Honey, for example, typically contains 15–20% water by mass yet has a water activity as low as 0.5–0.6, because the very high concentration of dissolved sugars binds most of the available water so tightly that its effective vapor pressure, and hence its availability to support microbial growth or chemical degradation reactions, is drastically reduced (Scott, 1957).

12.2 Water Activity and Microbial Growth

Water activity is widely regarded, alongside temperature and pH, as one of the principal "hurdle" parameters governing whether and how rapidly microorganisms can grow in a food or environmental matrix, because all microorganisms require some minimum threshold of thermodynamically available water to support the biochemical machinery of growth and division. Different microbial groups exhibit characteristic minimum aw thresholds: most spoilage and pathogenic bacteria require aw above approximately 0.90–0.91 (with a small number of notably halotolerant/osmotolerant exceptions, such as Staphylococcus aureus, able to grow at aw as low as 0.83–0.86); most yeasts require aw above approximately 0.87–0.88; and molds, generally the most xerotolerant common food-spoilage organisms, can grow at aw as low as approximately 0.61–0.65 for the most extreme xerophilic species (Tapia et al., 2020). Below approximately aw 0.6, essentially no microbial growth is possible, a threshold that provides the biochemical rationale for the historical development of drying, salting, sugaring, and smoking as food preservation techniques long before the underlying physical chemistry was understood.

12.3 Applications in Food Science and Preservation

Formal recognition of water activity as the operative variable governing food stability — as opposed to total moisture content — is generally credited to food scientists including William James Scott in the mid-twentieth century, and the concept has since become foundational to industrial food formulation, shelf-life prediction, and regulatory food-safety frameworks (including U.S. Food and Drug Administration guidance defining "potentially hazardous foods" partly by aw threshold). Beyond microbial growth, water activity also governs the rates of many non-microbial food degradation reactions of biochemical origin, including lipid oxidation (which, counterintuitively, is often minimized at an intermediate aw around 0.3, since very low aw actually accelerates oxidative rancidity by exposing catalytic metal sites, while higher aw promotes it via increased reactant mobility) and non-enzymatic (Maillard) browning reactions, which typically peak at intermediate aw values around 0.6–0.7 where reactants are sufficiently mobile but not so dilute as to be kinetically limited.

12.4 Water Activity Beyond Food Science

The same fundamental principle governs the ecological limits of microbial life in natural and extreme environments: the aw of a saturated NaCl brine (approximately 0.75) defines an approximate lower boundary for halophilic microbial growth, and the very low aw of extremely arid soils, deep subsurface brines, and other water-limited natural habitats is now recognized by astrobiologists as a critical parameter, alongside temperature and radiation exposure, in assessing the habitability of extreme terrestrial analogue environments and, by extension, other planetary bodies such as Mars, whose near-surface brines are believed to have water activities close to or below the threshold for terrestrial microbial growth.

13. Extremophiles, Anhydrobiosis, and Vitrification

13.1 The Challenge of Complete Desiccation

While the organisms discussed in Section 12 tolerate reduced water activity in their surrounding environment, a distinct and more extreme phenomenon — anhydrobiosis ("life without water") — refers to the capacity of certain organisms to survive the near-total loss of their own cellular water (down to residual water contents of a few percent or less), entering a state of suspended metabolism (cryptobiosis) from which they can later revive upon rehydration. Anhydrobiotic capability is documented across a phylogenetically wide range of organisms, including certain bacteria and archaea, fungal and plant spores and seeds, the resurrection plants (a small number of vascular plant species capable of vegetative desiccation tolerance), brine shrimp (Artemia) cysts, bdelloid rotifers, nematodes, and — perhaps most famously — tardigrades (water bears), microscopic invertebrates capable not only of surviving complete desiccation but also, in this desiccated "tun" state, of tolerating extremes of temperature, pressure, and radiation exposure vastly beyond what the hydrated organism could survive, including brief direct exposure to the vacuum of space in orbital experiments (Clegg, 2001; Crowe et al., 1992).

The fundamental biochemical challenge of complete desiccation is that water is not merely a passive medium but, as established throughout this review, an active structural and dynamical participant in maintaining the native conformation of proteins, nucleic acids, and membranes (Sections 5, 7, and 8); its wholesale removal would, in an unprotected system, be expected to cause catastrophic, generally irreversible protein denaturation, membrane phase transitions and rupture, and nucleic acid damage. Anhydrobiotic organisms survive by deploying specific molecular strategies that substitute for, or otherwise compensate for, the structural and dynamical roles ordinarily played by water.

13.2 Trehalose and the Water Replacement / Vitrification Hypotheses

The disaccharide trehalose (α,α-1,1-glucopyranosyl glucopyranoside) accumulates to very high concentrations (in some cases exceeding 20% of dry body mass) in many, though notably not all, classically studied anhydrobiotic organisms, including brine shrimp cysts, resurrection plants, and many desiccation-tolerant yeasts and fungi, and has historically been considered the paradigmatic anhydrobiotic protectant molecule. Two, non-mutually-exclusive mechanistic hypotheses have been advanced to explain trehalose's protective action: the water replacement hypothesis, which proposes that trehalose's hydroxyl groups can hydrogen-bond directly to the polar and charged surfaces of proteins and membrane lipid headgroups in a geometry that substitutes structurally for the hydrogen bonds ordinarily made by water, thereby preserving native macromolecular conformation even in water's near-total absence; and the vitrification hypothesis, which proposes that trehalose's protective action derives chiefly from its exceptional ability, among small sugars, to form a stable, non-crystalline amorphous glass at physiological temperatures as the system desiccates, mechanically immobilizing cellular contents within a rigid, high-viscosity glassy matrix that dramatically slows the diffusion-dependent chemical reactions (protein aggregation, lipid peroxidation, and Maillard-type glycation reactions among them) that would otherwise cause cumulative, irreversible damage during extended desiccated storage (Crowe et al., 1998).

13.3 Tardigrades: A More Complex Picture

Tardigrades complicate the trehalose-centered model that emerged from earlier work on brine shrimp and yeast: several tardigrade species accumulate only very low levels of trehalose (well under 1% of dry mass) or, in some species, none that is reliably detectable, indicating that trehalose-independent mechanisms of anhydrobiotic protection must operate, at least as the dominant strategy, in this lineage (Hengherr et al., 2008). Subsequent research has identified a family of tardigrade-specific, intrinsically disordered proteins termed CAHS (cytoplasmic-abundant heat-soluble) proteins, which are highly expressed during the entry into anhydrobiosis and which, like trehalose, have been shown experimentally to form non-crystalline, vitrified amorphous solids upon drying, and to confer measurable desiccation tolerance when heterologously expressed in normally desiccation-sensitive cells, including human cell lines and yeast (Boothby et al., 2017). More recent biophysical work indicates that CAHS proteins and trehalose act synergistically rather than redundantly — combined treatment confers greater protection than either component alone — and that at least one CAHS protein (CAHS D) appears to protect primarily via vitrification and gel-forming mechanical stabilization of the desiccating cytoplasm rather than via direct, trehalose-like retention or replacement of water at the molecular level, indicating that tardigrades have evolved a mechanistically distinct, protein-centered anhydrobiotic strategy layered onto, or in some lineages substituting for, the more ancient sugar-based strategy (Nguyen et al., 2022; Sanchez-Martinez et al., 2023).

13.4 Broader Lessons for Biochemistry

The comparative study of anhydrobiotic mechanisms across trehalose-dependent and CAHS-protein-dependent lineages has practical significance well beyond basic organismal biology: understanding how these organisms substitute for or otherwise compensate for the structural roles of water has directly informed the rational design of improved cryopreservation and lyopreservation (freeze-drying) protocols for pharmaceuticals, including protein therapeutics and, notably, mRNA vaccines, and for the long-term, ambient-temperature storage of blood products, vaccines, and cells — an active and commercially significant area of applied biochemistry directly descended from basic anhydrobiosis research.

14. Water in Human Physiology, Aging, and Disease

14.1 Body Water Compartments

Total body water constitutes approximately 50–60% of total body mass in adult humans (somewhat higher in infants, somewhat lower in older adults and in individuals with higher adipose tissue mass, since adipose tissue is comparatively water-poor relative to lean tissue), distributed between the intracellular fluid compartment (approximately two-thirds of total body water) and the extracellular fluid compartment (approximately one-third), the latter further subdivided into interstitial fluid (bathing tissue cells directly) and blood plasma. Water moves continuously between these compartments in response to osmotic and hydrostatic pressure gradients, and the maintenance of appropriate distribution among them — not merely of total body water — is itself under active physiological regulation, since pathological shifts in fluid distribution (for example, into the interstitial compartment, producing clinically evident edema) can occur even when total body water and plasma osmolality remain within nominal reference ranges.

14.2 Aging and Altered Water Homeostasis

Water homeostasis becomes measurably less robust with advancing age, through several convergent mechanisms: reduced total body water as a fraction of body mass (owing chiefly to age-related loss of lean muscle mass, a comparatively water-rich tissue, relative to adipose tissue); blunted thirst perception in response to a given osmotic or volume stimulus, such that older adults frequently fail to increase voluntary fluid intake appropriately even when physiologically dehydrated; reduced maximal urine-concentrating capacity, related in part to age-associated decline in renal responsiveness to vasopressin and altered aquaporin-2 expression and trafficking; and a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions and medications (diuretics prominent among them) that further perturb fluid balance (Li et al., 2023). The clinical consequences of this age-related vulnerability are substantial: dehydration in older adults is independently associated with increased hospital length of stay, higher readmission rates, greater likelihood of intensive care admission, elevated in-hospital mortality, and measurable acute impairment of cognitive performance, and is now recognized as a significant and under-addressed contributor to morbidity in geriatric medicine (Frith, 2023).

14.3 Skin Hydration and Water Loss

The stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the epidermis, depends on an appropriate water content (typically cited as approximately 15–20% by mass, considerably lower than living tissue generally) to maintain its mechanical flexibility and barrier function; both excessive hydration and, far more commonly in clinical practice, insufficient hydration (dryness, or xerosis) compromise stratum corneum barrier integrity and mechanical properties. Transepidermal water loss (TEWL), the rate of water vapor diffusion outward across the stratum corneum, is a standard, non-invasively measurable index of skin barrier function used extensively in dermatological research; the corneocyte-and-lipid "brick and mortar" architecture of the stratum corneum, together with endogenous hygroscopic small molecules collectively termed natural moisturizing factor, jointly regulate TEWL and stratum corneum water content under normal physiological conditions. Reduced systemic hydration status (measured, for example, by serum osmolality) has been associated in clinical studies with reduced facial skin hydration, particularly among geriatric inpatients, and observational genetic-epidemiological work in middle-aged and postmenopausal women has further identified an interaction between habitual water intake and polymorphisms in genes involved in collagen metabolism, including EGFR, MMP16, and COL17A1, in determining visible facial wrinkling, though this remains an area with a comparatively modest evidence base relative to the more extensively documented systemic and renal consequences of dehydration discussed above (Park et al., 2021).

14.4 Water Balance in Disease States

Numerous disease processes converge on disordered water handling as a proximate clinical problem, even when the primary pathology lies elsewhere. Congestive heart failure and cirrhotic liver disease both produce a state of reduced effective circulating volume that is physiologically (if paradoxically, given that total body water and extracellular volume are often frankly increased in these conditions) sensed by baroreceptors as hypovolemia, triggering compensatory, ultimately maladaptive, activation of the RAAS and vasopressin systems described in Section 10.3, driving further fluid retention, dilutional hyponatremia, and, in advanced disease, the interstitial fluid accumulation recognized clinically as edema and ascites. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), in which vasopressin is secreted independent of, and inappropriately high relative to, plasma osmolality (commonly due to certain malignancies, central nervous system pathology, or medications), produces a distinct pattern of dilutional hyponatremia with preserved or expanded, rather than depleted, extracellular volume, illustrating clinically that "water problems" in medicine are rarely reducible to a single axis of "too much" or "too little" but instead reflect derangements in the specific hormonal and renal regulatory machinery discussed throughout Sections 9 and 10 of this review.

15. Biophysical Techniques for Studying Biological Water

15.1 X-ray and Neutron Crystallography

High-resolution X-ray crystallography remains the primary source of static, atomically resolved information about ordered water positions in proteins and nucleic acids, as discussed in Sections 5.1 and 7.1. Because hydrogen atoms scatter X-rays too weakly to be reliably resolved at typical macromolecular crystallographic resolutions, X-ray structures directly localize only the oxygen atom of each ordered water molecule, and orientation must be inferred computationally. Neutron crystallography, which is sensitive to hydrogen (and, with even greater scattering contrast, to deuterium, motivating the common practice of growing deuterated crystals or soaking crystals in D₂O for neutron studies) directly localizes hydrogen/deuterium positions and thus provides direct experimental information on hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor geometry and orientation that X-ray crystallography cannot, at the cost of requiring substantially larger crystals and longer data collection times owing to the comparative weakness of available neutron sources relative to X-ray sources (Schoenborn et al., 1995).

15.2 Solution NMR Spectroscopy

Solution nuclear magnetic resonance provides several complementary windows onto hydration in solution, free of the potential confound that crystallographically observed waters might be stabilized or selected for by crystal-packing contacts rather than reflecting genuine solution behavior. Nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) measurements between protein or nucleic acid protons and water protons directly detect water molecules in sufficiently close and sufficiently long-lived contact with the macromolecule to give an observable cross-relaxation signal, providing residence-time-sensitive information on individual, specific hydration sites, including the DNA minor-groove spine of hydration discussed in Section 7.1 (Otting et al., 1991). NMR relaxation dispersion and magnetic relaxation dispersion methods, applied across a range of magnetic field strengths, provide complementary information on the amplitude and timescale of water reorientational dynamics both in the bulk and in the hydration shell, and have been central to establishing the "mostly fast exchange, with a minority of longer-lived sites" picture of protein hydration dynamics discussed in Section 5.2 (Halle, 2004).

15.3 Vibrational and Ultrafast Spectroscopy

Infrared and Raman spectroscopy probe the vibrational modes of water (chiefly the O–H stretching and H–O–H bending modes), which are sensitive reporters of local hydrogen-bonding environment, since hydrogen-bond strength and geometry directly shift vibrational frequencies. Ultrafast (femtosecond to picosecond time-resolution) infrared spectroscopy, including two-dimensional infrared (2D-IR) spectroscopy, extends this static structural sensitivity to direct, real-time measurement of hydrogen-bond network reorganization dynamics, underlying the femtosecond and picosecond hydration dynamics measurements discussed in Section 5.2 (Pal & Zewail, 2004). Terahertz (THz) time-domain spectroscopy, operating in the far-infrared spectral region corresponding to collective, low-frequency intermolecular vibrational and librational modes of the hydrogen-bond network rather than the higher-frequency intramolecular vibrations probed by conventional infrared spectroscopy, has emerged over the past two decades as a particularly sensitive probe of extended, collective hydration dynamics around biomolecules, underlying the "extended dynamical hydration shell" findings discussed in Section 5.2 (Nibali & Havenith, 2014).

15.4 Neutron and X-ray Scattering (Non-Crystallographic)

Beyond crystallography, small-angle X-ray and neutron scattering (SAXS/SANS) and quasi-elastic and inelastic neutron scattering provide solution-state structural and dynamical information, respectively, on both bulk water structure and biomolecular hydration, without requiring the sample to be crystallized. Quasi-elastic neutron scattering (QENS) is particularly well-suited to directly measuring translational and rotational diffusion coefficients of water on picosecond-to-nanosecond timescales and nanometer length scales, and has been extensively applied to the protein dynamical transition (Section 6.4) and to the intracellular water debate (Section 11), since it can be applied directly to intact, hydrated biological samples, including whole cells, with the hydrogen-rich water signal dominating the incoherent scattering cross-section and thus providing an effectively water-selective probe within a complex biological sample (Nakagawa & Yamamoto, 2023).

15.5 Molecular Dynamics Simulation as an Investigative Tool

Although formally a computational rather than an experimental technique, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation has become an indispensable and, in practice, inseparable complement to the experimental techniques described above, since it provides direct, atomically resolved, femtosecond-time-resolution trajectories of water and biomolecular motion that can be used both to interpret and to generate testable predictions for experimental observables (scattering functions, NMR relaxation rates, vibrational spectra) that are otherwise only indirectly accessible from experiment alone. The specific methodological considerations and limitations of MD simulation as applied to water are addressed in the following section.

16. Computational Modeling of Water in Biomolecular Simulation

16.1 Classical Water Models

Because a fully quantum-mechanical treatment of even a single water molecule's electronic structure is computationally expensive, and simulating the many thousands to millions of water molecules typically required to solvate a biomolecular system at this level of theory remains impractical for most routine simulation applications, the great majority of biomolecular MD simulations use simplified, classical (non-polarizable, fixed-point-charge) water models parameterized to reproduce selected experimental bulk properties of water — commonly density, heat of vaporization, and the diffusion coefficient — at the cost of not being derived from first-principles quantum mechanics and therefore requiring empirical validation and, in some cases, exhibiting systematic deviations from experiment for properties not explicitly included in the parameterization target set.

The most widely used models in contemporary biomolecular simulation include the three-site TIP3P and SPC (simple point charge) models, which represent each water molecule with three point charges located at the oxygen and two hydrogen positions and a single Lennard-Jones interaction site on oxygen; the modestly more sophisticated SPC/E model, which adds an empirical polarization correction term to the original SPC model, improving agreement with experimental self-diffusion and dielectric properties; and four- and five-site models (TIP4P and its variants, including the widely used TIP4P/2005 reparameterization, and TIP5P), which improve the representation of the electrostatic potential around the water molecule by displacing the negative charge from the oxygen nucleus toward the position of the lone pairs, generally at increased computational cost per water molecule relative to three-site models (Mark & Nilsson, 2001).

16.2 Comparative Performance and Model Selection

No single classical water model reproduces all experimentally measured properties of real water simultaneously, and different models exhibit different, well-documented strengths and weaknesses: comparative benchmarking studies have found, for instance, that TIP4P/2005 achieves comparatively strong agreement with experimental self-diffusion coefficients and shear viscosity, that TIP5P better reproduces water's density anomaly and temperature of maximum density, and that TIP3P, despite comparatively poor reproduction of several bulk transport properties relative to the more sophisticated four- and five-site models, remains very widely used in biomolecular simulation chiefly because it was the model originally used to parameterize several major, still widely used protein and nucleic acid force fields (including early and, in modified form, current versions of the AMBER and CHARMM force field families), such that switching to a different water model can introduce subtle but consequential incompatibilities with the rest of the parameterized force field unless the entire force field is correspondingly reparameterized and revalidated (Dorrani & Mohebbi, 2023). This practical consideration — that water model choice is entangled with, and constrained by, the broader force field ecosystem in which it is embedded, rather than being a fully independent, freely optimizable choice — is an important and sometimes underappreciated methodological caveat for interpreting and comparing results across the biomolecular simulation literature.

16.3 Polarizable and Ab Initio Approaches

More sophisticated polarizable water models, which allow each water molecule's charge distribution to respond dynamically to its local electrostatic environment rather than using fixed point charges, offer improved physical realism, particularly in situations involving strong local electric fields (for example, in the immediate vicinity of a multiply charged ion or within a narrow, electrostatically asymmetric channel such as an aquaporin pore, discussed in Section 9.2), at substantially increased computational cost that has historically limited their routine adoption for large-scale or long-timescale biomolecular simulation, though this is changing as computational resources continue to grow. Ab initio and mixed quantum-mechanics/molecular-mechanics (QM/MM) approaches, in which water (or at least the specific water molecules directly involved in a chemical reaction, such as the catalytic water molecules discussed in Section 6) are treated with an explicit quantum-mechanical electronic structure method rather than a classical force field, are essential for accurately modeling bond-breaking and bond-forming chemical events (as opposed to purely structural and dynamical, non-reactive behavior) but remain computationally restricted, in most practical applications, to comparatively small active-site regions embedded within a larger classical simulation of the surrounding protein and solvent.

16.4 Confined and Interfacial Water Simulation

Simulating water under geometric confinement — within narrow protein channels such as aquaporins (Section 9), between apposed lipid bilayers (Section 8.3), or within the crowded intracellular environment (Section 11) — poses additional, well-recognized methodological challenges beyond bulk water simulation, since confinement can alter the effective hydrogen-bonding coordination number, dielectric response, and dynamics of water in ways that are not always faithfully captured by water models parameterized solely against bulk liquid properties; simulations of water conduction through carbon nanotubes and aquaporin channels, for example, have in some cases produced predictions (such as the specific single-file proton-blocking mechanism discussed in Section 9.2) that were subsequently validated experimentally, but the field continues to actively benchmark and refine confined-water simulation methodology against new experimental data as it becomes available (Hummer et al., 2001).

17. Controversies: "Exclusion Zone" and "Structured" Water Claims

17.1 The Core Observation

In the early 2000s, Gerald Pollack and colleagues at the University of Washington reported that when aqueous suspensions of small (micron-scale) colloidal microspheres are placed adjacent to certain hydrophilic surfaces (Nafion, a sulfonated fluoropolymer, being the most extensively studied), the microspheres are progressively excluded from a zone extending, in many reported experiments, several hundred micrometers from the surface — an "exclusion zone" (EZ) far larger than could be explained by simple electrostatic double-layer repulsion of the scale conventionally expected (typically tens of nanometers) from classical colloid science (Zheng et al., 2006). This core observation — that an unusually extended particle-exclusion region can form adjacent to certain hydrophilic surfaces under specific experimental conditions — has been independently reproduced by multiple laboratories, particularly for the Nafion-water interface, and is generally accepted within the water-science community as a genuine, reproducible physical phenomenon in need of a satisfactory mechanistic explanation (Elton et al., 2020).

17.2 Pollack's Interpretation: A "Fourth Phase" of Water

Pollack has proposed a far more expansive theoretical interpretation of this observation, arguing that exclusion-zone water constitutes a distinct, ordered "fourth phase" of water, structurally organized into stacked, hexagonally arranged sheets in which hydrogen atoms sit directly between, and are shared symmetrically by, neighboring oxygen atoms, and that this ordered EZ water carries a substantial net negative electrical charge, with protons released into the adjoining bulk water, effectively constituting a naturally occurring, light-driven (Pollack and colleagues report that EZ growth is enhanced by incident infrared radiation) charge-separation and energy-storage mechanism with proposed relevance to cellular bioenergetics, generalized as being active throughout the hydrophilic interior surfaces of living cells (Pollack, 2013).

17.3 Critical Evaluation

This more expansive theoretical framework has not been accepted by the broader physical chemistry and water-science community, for several convergent reasons documented in critical reviews of the topic (Elton et al., 2020; Elton & Spencer, 2021). First, the specific proposed hexagonal-sheet structure with hydrogens positioned symmetrically between oxygen atoms is not supported by, and is in some respects directly contradicted by, high-level quantum chemistry calculations and by decades of established spectroscopic and diffraction data on hydrogen-bond geometry in water and ice, none of which have ever independently reproduced or corroborated this proposed structural motif. Second, no clear, thermodynamically consistent driving force has been articulated for why such an extensive, macroscopically ordered phase transition (extending hundreds of micrometers — many orders of magnitude beyond the nanometer length scale over which conventional interfacial water structuring is expected to decay, as discussed in Section 8.1) should occur spontaneously at a simple hydrophilic interface at room temperature, a concern raised independently by multiple critical commentators. Third, several groups have proposed alternative, more conventional physical mechanisms that can account for at least a substantial portion of the reported exclusion-zone phenomenology without invoking a novel phase of water: J. Michael Schurr's diffusiophoresis-based model, for example, proposes that the exclusion zone arises from conventional colloidal transport physics driven by a chemical gradient generated at the Nafion surface, rather than from any novel bulk structural transition in the water itself (Elton et al., 2020). Fourth, and more broadly, water science has a documented history of episodic claims of exotic, ordered, or "memory"-bearing water structures (including the "polywater" episode of the late 1960s, ultimately shown to result from silica and other ionic contaminants leached from the narrow glass capillaries used in the original experiments, and, later, disputed claims regarding water's capacity to retain a specific molecular "memory" of dissolved substances after their removal by serial dilution) that initially attracted significant scientific and public attention before being substantially or entirely discredited upon closer, independently replicated scrutiny — a pattern that several commentators have argued the EZ/fourth-phase literature risks repeating in its more expansive theoretical claims, even while the underlying core experimental observation remains a legitimate subject of ongoing study (Elton & Spencer, 2021).

17.4 A Balanced Position

The appropriate scientific posture toward this body of work, consistent with how it is treated in the peer-reviewed critical literature cited above, is to distinguish clearly between the reproducible core phenomenon (an unusually extended colloidal exclusion zone adjacent to certain hydrophilic surfaces, most robustly documented for Nafion) — which merits continued, mechanistically open-minded investigation using conventional, well-validated physical chemistry — and the substantially more expansive theoretical superstructure built upon it (a genuinely distinct fourth phase of bulk-like ordered water with net charge separation and proposed general biological energy-storage function), for which the specific structural and mechanistic claims advanced by Pollack and colleagues have not been independently corroborated and are inconsistent with a substantial body of established water physics. Popular-science and wellness-industry framings of "EZ water" or "structured water" as an established, health-relevant phenomenon with direct implications for hydration practices or disease — framings that circulate widely outside the peer-reviewed literature — considerably overstate the current state of scientific evidence and should be treated with particular caution by readers of this review.

18. Conclusion and Future Directions

The picture that emerges from the biochemistry of water, synthesized across the length scales and topics addressed in this review, is one of water as a genuinely active, structurally and thermodynamically indispensable participant in the molecular events that constitute life, rather than a passive, chemically inert medium in which biology merely happens to occur. At the molecular scale, water's capacity for cooperative, tetrahedrally coordinated hydrogen bonding gives rise to its many anomalous bulk physical properties (Sections 2–3), which in turn underlie the thermodynamics of hydration — the hydrophobic and hydrophilic effects — that drive protein folding, membrane self-assembly, and molecular recognition (Section 4). At the scale of individual biomacromolecules, water forms structurally and dynamically distinct hydration shells that are directly implicated in protein folding and stability (Section 5), enzymatic catalysis (Section 6), and the sequence-dependent structural polymorphism of nucleic acids (Section 7). At the scale of membranes and organelles, water's exclusion from and, in regulated fashion via aquaporins, controlled passage across the hydrophobic bilayer barrier (Sections 8–9) underlies fundamental cell-biological processes from membrane fusion to renal urine concentration. At the cellular and organismal scale, the regulation of water content and distribution — whether through classical ion-based osmoregulation (Section 10), the more subtle and still incompletely resolved question of intracellular water structuring (Section 11), the ecological and food-preservation-relevant concept of water activity (Section 12), the remarkable anhydrobiotic strategies of desiccation-tolerant organisms (Section 13), or the hormonally regulated fluid physiology of mammals in health, aging, and disease (Section 14) — reveals water homeostasis as a genuinely organism-level, multiply redundant physiological priority.

Several directions appear particularly likely to shape the field over the coming years. First, continued development of ultrafast and multidimensional spectroscopic techniques, together with advances in cryo-electron microscopy resolution sufficient to resolve ordered hydration shells directly, should continue to sharpen — and, in some cases, further complicate — the quantitative picture of hydration-shell dynamics discussed in Sections 5.2 and 11.2, an area where genuine scientific disagreement about the magnitude, though not the existence, of biologically relevant water perturbation persists. Second, the increasing recognition that biomolecular condensates and liquid-liquid phase separation are pervasive features of intracellular organization (Section 11.3) is likely to generate substantial new interest in how local water activity and hydration thermodynamics contribute to condensate formation, stability, and function, an intersection that remains comparatively underexplored relative to the more mature hydration literature for folded, single-chain proteins. Third, continued refinement of polarizable and machine-learning-based water force fields (Section 16) promises to narrow, though likely not eliminate, the gap between computationally tractable classical water models and full quantum-mechanical accuracy, extending the reach of molecular simulation to more faithfully capture the subtle, sometimes counterintuitive behaviors documented throughout this review. Finally, continued rigorous, mechanistically grounded investigation of genuinely anomalous interfacial water phenomena, including the exclusion-zone observations discussed critically in Section 17, conducted with the same standards of independent replication and theoretical accountability applied to the rest of the field, offers the best path toward resolving legitimate open questions while avoiding the historical pitfalls of prematurely embracing exotic explanations for phenomena that may ultimately admit more conventional, if still scientifically interesting, physical explanations.

Water, in short, is not a backdrop to biochemistry. It is one of biochemistry's principal actors — and, as this review has aimed to demonstrate, one whose full script is still, after more than a century of dedicated study, being written.
