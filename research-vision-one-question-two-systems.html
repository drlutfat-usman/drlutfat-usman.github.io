Every research programme I build starts from the same observation: science generates enormous multi-omics knowledge about complex biological systems — and then largely fails to translate that knowledge into tools the people who need them can actually use. I have spent my career, across Nigeria, Malaysia, and Brazil, building the computational and analytical skillset to close that gap. My current work applies it to human disease. My longer-term vision is that the same architecture — multi-omics integration, interpretable machine-learning diagnosis, network-level compatibility screening — is not specific to human biology at all. It is a general method for translating biological complexity into calibrated, actionable intelligence, for any system where that translation gap exists.

Track One — MetaboScan: Precision Diagnosis for Human Disease

MetaboScan-IIM is the first expression of this architecture: a multi-omics diagnostic engine for inflammatory myopathies, translating transcriptomic and metabolomic patient data into calibrated, interpretable subtype diagnosis and evidence-based therapeutic guidance — with an explicit extension pathway into Type 2 Diabetes and metabolic syndrome via the Saudi Biobank. This track is where my deepest evidence base already sits: real patient cohorts, in-press publications, and years of direct clinical-omics experience in an academic hospital setting.

Track Two — CarboScan: The Same Architecture, Applied to Planetary Health

A second, earlier-stage question has been forming alongside the first: if this architecture works for translating a patient's multi-omics data into diagnosis, could the same architecture translate a soil's multi-omics data — its microbiome, its metabolome — into a calibrated prediction of its capacity for carbon sequestration? Enhanced rock weathering, a leading nature-based carbon removal strategy, is bottlenecked not by geochemistry but by exactly this kind of biological unpredictability: soil microbial communities strongly modulate weathering rate, yet no computational tool exists to predict or optimize this from multi-omics data the way MetaboScan does for human disease. CarboScan-EW is my working name for this idea — not yet a funded programme, but a genuine, literature-grounded extension I am actively exploring, including outreach to KAUST's Center for Desert Agriculture, whose own published work has called for exactly this kind of computational layer.

Why I See These as One Trajectory, Not Two

Same architecture: multi-omics harmonization, interpretable ML classification, network-level compatibility screening — reused, not reinvented, across both tracks.

Same underlying conviction: that complex biological systems — a diseased body, a degraded soil — can be diagnosed and guided toward health with the right computational translation layer.

Same personal throughline: an LMIC-embedded research trajectory across three continents that treats real-world deployability, not just discovery, as the primary design requirement.

In the near term, my focus is MetaboScan-IIM — it is the track with the evidence, the data, and the readiness to be funded now. CarboScan-EW is where I intend to grow next: not a distraction from the first track, but its logical extension into one of the defining scientific challenges of this decade. I am building toward a career where "multi-omics diagnostic architecture" becomes a methodology I am known for applying wherever it is needed most — starting with human health, and extending, deliberately and rigorously, to planetary health.
